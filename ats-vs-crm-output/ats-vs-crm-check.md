### As written

<h2>What is an applicant tracking system?</h2>
<p>An applicant tracking system is hiring software that gives a team one central place where job applications are submitted, then tracks each candidate through the stages of the hiring process from application to decision. According to <a href="https://www.sap.com/resources/what-is-an-applicant-tracking-system" target="_blank" rel="noopener">SAP</a>, an ATS "serves as the central database for job and applicant information where job requisitions, postings, and applications are managed and applicants are screened and selected." Core functions include resume parsing, interview coordination, automated candidate communication, and compliance recordkeeping.</p>

<p>A useful way to think about an ATS is as a system focused on the present. Every candidate in it has already raised their hand by applying for a specific, open role. The ATS answers one question at a time: where does this candidate stand right now, and what is the next step? Each candidate gets a single record — with their resume, every message exchanged, the team's interview notes, and a log of every stage they've moved through — so nothing lives in a private inbox or a Slack thread that disappears when someone leaves the channel.</p>

<p><a href="https://portal.shrm.org/Education/Registration/Details.aspx?cid=f0491a33-44f7-4fb5-930a-0dbd851218ab" target="_blank" rel="noopener">SHRM</a> reports that more than 90% of Fortune 500 companies use an applicant tracking system. For growing teams without a dedicated recruiting department, an ATS delivers the same structural benefit — a single place where every candidate, every piece of feedback, and every communication lives, so nothing gets dropped between the application and the offer. If you want a detailed walkthrough of what an ATS does under the hood, the guide on <a href="/blog/how-does-an-applicant-tracking-system-work">how an applicant tracking system works</a> covers each function in depth.</p>

<h2>What is a recruiting CRM?</h2>
<p>A recruiting CRM — short for candidate relationship management — is a software tool designed to manage and nurture relationships with potential candidates before they apply for a job. Where an ATS is transactional and requisition-focused, a CRM is relational and pipeline-focused.</p>

<p>Think of a recruiting CRM as a system focused on the future. Its database holds passive candidates who match the profile for roles you will eventually need to fill, former applicants who were strong but not selected for a previous opening, people who attended a career fair, and anyone a recruiter has been nurturing over time. The purpose is to keep those relationships warm so that when a role opens, the team already has a shortlist to contact rather than starting sourcing from scratch each time.</p>

<p>Adoption is high at the enterprise level. According to <a href="https://www.aptituderesearch.com/crm-for-recruiters/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Aptitude Research</a>, 76% of enterprise companies use or plan to use a recruiting CRM to support their recruitment efforts. That figure also reflects the audience for which a CRM makes the most sense: large organizations with dedicated sourcers and recruiters who run always-on talent pipelines for roles that open repeatedly. For a team without that capacity, the tool is a different proposition entirely.</p>

<h2>Where the two tools diverge</h2>
<p>The easiest way to draw the line is by timing. The moment a candidate submits an application, they move from CRM territory into ATS territory. Before that moment, the CRM holds the relationship. <h2>Where each tool falls short</h2>
<p>Neither tool covers the full arc of hiring, and understanding where each one runs out is as important as knowing what it does well. The practical limits are worth examining before you buy either one.</p>

<h3>The limits of an ATS</h3>
<p>An ATS is designed around active requisitions, and that design is also its boundary. It has no mechanism for warming up a passive candidate who matched last year's search, following up with a strong applicant who accepted another offer, or building a talent community for roles you haven't posted yet. When a new role opens, the search starts from zero unless someone has been keeping their own spreadsheet on the side.</p>
<p><a href="https://olibr.com/blog/recruiting-crm-vs-ats" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Olibr</a> frames the trade-off plainly: the ATS wins at "structured, time-bound hiring, moving one candidate from application to offer letter," but it was never designed for "everything that happens before and after that window, building a searchable pool you can return to without starting sourcing from scratch." For companies that hire for the same roles repeatedly — an engineering team adding developers every quarter, a retail operation replacing seasonal staff — that blind spot has a real cost. The ATS tells you nothing about the strong candidate you passed on eight months ago.</p>

<h3>The limits of a recruiting CRM</h3>
<p>A recruiting CRM has practical costs that teams often underestimate before buying. The first is data decay. CRM candidate records "become outdated quickly as people change jobs, phone numbers, and email addresses," according to <a href="https://softwarefinder.com/resources/candidate-relationship-management-systems" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Software Finder</a>, making the database a source of inaccurate information if nobody maintains it. The investment in building a talent pool only pays off when someone keeps it current, and that work is ongoing, not one-time.</p>
<p>Adoption is the second barrier. Without sustained training, leadership buy-in, and clear incentives tied to CRM usage, adoption tends to plateau well below the level needed to realize the tool's full value. For a small team where the hiring manager is also running the interview, writing the offer, and onboarding the new hire, that investment is hard to sustain alongside everything else.</p>
<p>Integration complexity is the third cost, and it cuts across both tools. Connecting a CRM to an existing ATS requires technical resources and careful field mapping. Poorly executed integrations result in duplicate candidate records, data loss, and sync errors that erode recruiter trust in the system over time. As Pin reports, teams running an ATS and CRM without clean data handoffs encounter the same frustrations repeatedly: duplicate records, missed outreach sequences, and interviewers going into calls without context from earlier sourcing conversations.</p>

<h2>What running both looks like</h2>
<p>Some organizations run an ATS and a recruiting CRM side by side, and for high-volume or proactive hiring programs, the combination makes sense. The CRM sources and warms candidates; the ATS takes over the moment they apply; both systems stay in sync through an integration. For context on how common talent-pipeline work has become, HR Dive reports that building a talent pipeline is the top priority for 48% of organizations — a figure that reflects how seriously larger teams take the pre-application stage of hiring.</p>

<p>The integration gap, however, is real. When the two systems don't share data cleanly, context gets lost at the handoff. A recruiter who spent three months nurturing a candidate may never surface that history to the interviewer. The candidate gets a generic experience; the team loses the advantage of the relationship it built. That is the scenario Pin's research documents, and it is common enough to treat as the default outcome rather than an edge case.</p>

<p>Olibr also notes that buying both as separate subscriptions "works, but it's rarely the cheapest or simplest path once your team scales past a handful of open roles." The administrative overhead, the data maintenance, and the subscription cost of running two systems are worth measuring honestly before committing to both. The question is not whether the combination can work but whether your team has the capacity to make it work well.</p>

<p>There is also something worth naming that neither system captures well on its own: the interview itself. The ATS holds the compliance record, and the CRM holds the engagement history, but neither preserves what was actually discussed in the room, which concerns the interviewer raised, or which rubric criteria went unexplored. That gap sits between the tools, and it matters for the quality of the final decision. Structured interview feedback, written and stored inside the ATS, is the closest a two-tool stack gets to closing it. The satisfaction gap at the enterprise level — where dedicated resources are available — makes the case for caution at the startup level, where those resources are not.</p>

## How to choose

### As written

<h2>How to choose</h2>
<p>The right starting point depends on where your hiring problem actually lives. Here is how that reasoning applies to each type of reader.</p>

<h3>Founders and hiring managers at growing companies</h3>
<p>Start with an ATS. If candidates are coming to you through job posts and referrals, your problem is managing the ones who have already applied — keeping track of where everyone stands, coordinating feedback across the team, and communicating with candidates without losing threads in email. That is what an ATS solves. <a href="https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat" target="_blank" rel="noopener">SHRM's</a> 2025 benchmarking data puts the average cost per hire at $5,475 for nonexecutive roles. A structured ATS process is one of the clearest ways to protect that investment by reducing the chance of a disorganized process costing you a strong candidate who moved on while your team was still figuring out next steps.</p>
<p>A CRM answers a different question. <h2>Do you need an applicant tracking system?</h2>
<p>The question of whether you need an ATS comes down to what happens to candidates between the moment they apply and the moment you make a decision. If the answer is that they go into an inbox, their resume lands in a downloads folder, and feedback lives in someone's head or a Slack thread, an ATS solves a real and immediate problem.</p>

<p>The candidate-side cost of a disorganized process is measurable. <a href="https://www.hrdive.com/news/candidates-have-quit-job-applications-before-finishing/758796/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">HR Dive</a> reports that 57% of job candidates have abandoned an application mid-process when it was overly complicated or long. A clean application form and consistent communication — both things an ATS delivers — reduce the friction that causes candidates to drop out before you have even seen their resume. Beyond the application stage, candidates who make it further into the process and then wait in silence are equally at risk of withdrawing. The ATS keeps them moving and keeps them informed.</p>

<p>The fuller question for a founder or hiring manager is not simply "do I need an ATS?" but "what does my hiring problem look like right now?" If too few qualified applicants are reaching you, an ATS won't solve that — sourcing is a different problem, and a CRM addresses it. If qualified applicants are reaching you and you are losing track of them, moving too slowly, or making decisions without the whole team's input, an ATS is the right tool. And if you are evaluating candidates, coordinating interviewers, writing feedback, and communicating with candidates across multiple open roles at once, an ATS is not optional — it is the infrastructure the process depends on. For more on building the foundation that sits inside the ATS, the guide on <a href="/blog/skills-mapping-for-hiring-a-complete-guide">skills mapping for hiring</a> covers how to define what you are looking for before candidates start arriving.</p>

### Final

<h2>Do you need an applicant tracking system?</h2>
<p>The question of whether you need an ATS comes down to what happens to candidates between the moment they apply and the moment you make a decision. If the answer is that they go into an inbox, their resume lands in a downloads folder, and feedback lives in someone's head or a Slack thread, an ATS solves a real and immediate problem.</p>

<p>The candidate-side cost of a disorganized process is measurable. <a href="https://www.hrdive.com/news/candidates-have-quit-job-applications-before-finishing/758796/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">HR Dive</a> reports that 57% of job candidates have abandoned an application mid-process when it was overly complicated or long. A clean application form and consistent communication — both things an ATS delivers — reduce the friction that causes candidates to drop out before you have even seen their resume. Candidates who have passed the application stage and then stop receiving updates remain at risk of withdrawing. An ATS moves each candidate through the pipeline and sends updates as their application progresses.</p>

<p>A founder or hiring manager should start by diagnosing the actual hiring problem. If too few qualified applicants are reaching you, an ATS won't solve that. Sourcing is a different problem, and a CRM addresses it. If qualified applicants are reaching you and you are losing track of them, moving too slowly, or making decisions without the whole team's input, an ATS is the right tool. And if you are evaluating candidates, coordinating interviewers, writing feedback, and communicating with candidates across multiple open roles at once, an ATS is not optional. It is the infrastructure the process depends on. For more on building the foundation that sits inside the ATS, the guide on <a href="/blog/skills-mapping-for-hiring-a-complete-guide">skills mapping for hiring</a> covers how to define what you are looking for before candidates start arriving.</p>
