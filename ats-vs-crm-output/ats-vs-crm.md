# ATS vs CRM: What's the Difference, and Which One Does a Hiring Team Need?

An applicant tracking system and a recruiting CRM both store candidate data and both send automated messages — so why do teams end up buying one when they need the other? Vendors often use the terms interchangeably, and the feature lists look alike on the surface. Teams buy an ATS when they need a CRM, leaving them with nothing to track passive candidates or keep relationships warm. Teams buy a CRM when they need an ATS, leaving active candidates with no structured way to move through the hiring process.

An ATS manages candidates who have already applied — it tracks each one through your hiring stages, coordinates interview feedback, and handles candidate communication from application to decision. A recruiting CRM manages people who are not in an active hiring process, such as passive candidates, former applicants, event attendees, and anyone a recruiter has been warming up for a role that may not be open yet. The moment a candidate submits an application, they move from CRM territory into ATS territory.

This article covers what each tool does, where they diverge, what using both looks like, and how to decide which one or which combination fits your hiring operation.

## What is an applicant tracking system?

An applicant tracking system is hiring software that provides a team with one central place where job applications are submitted, then tracks each candidate through the stages of the hiring process from application to decision. One [definition](https://www.sap.com/resources/what-is-an-applicant-tracking-system) describes it as "the central database for job and applicant information where job requisitions, postings, and applications are managed and applicants are screened and selected." Core functions include resume parsing, interview coordination, automated candidate communication, and compliance recordkeeping.

An ATS is a system focused on the present. Every candidate in it has already raised their hand by applying for a specific, open role. At any moment, the ATS answers one question — where does this candidate stand, and what happens next? Each candidate gets a single record, with their resume, every message exchanged, the team's interview notes, and a log of every stage they've moved through, so nothing lives in a private inbox or a Slack thread that disappears when someone leaves the channel.

That structure scales. [SHRM](https://portal.shrm.org/Education/Registration/Details.aspx?cid=f0491a33-44f7-4fb5-930a-0dbd851218ab) reports that more than 90% of Fortune 500 companies use an applicant tracking system. Smaller and mid-size teams without a dedicated recruiting department gain the same benefit — every candidate record, piece of feedback, and communication is kept in one place, visible to everyone on the hiring team, from application to offer. If you want a detailed walkthrough of what an ATS does under the hood, the guide on [how an applicant tracking system works](/blog/how-does-an-applicant-tracking-system-work) covers each function in depth.

## What is a recruiting CRM?

A recruiting CRM, short for candidate relationship management, manages and nurtures relationships with potential candidates outside an active hiring process, both before they enter one and after one ends. [JobAdder](https://jobadder.com/blog/what-is-candidate-relationship-management-crm-in-recruiting/) defines it as a system distinct from the Customer Relationship Management tools used by sales and marketing teams. Where an ATS is transactional and requisition-focused, a CRM is relational and pipeline-focused.

Think of a recruiting CRM as a system focused on the future. Its database contains passive candidates who match the profile for roles you will eventually need to fill, former applicants who were strong but not selected for a previous opening, people who attended a career fair, and anyone a recruiter has been nurturing over time. The goal is to maintain enough contact that when a role opens, the team already has a shortlist of people ready to call.

That approach is widespread at the enterprise level. According to [Aptitude Research](https://www.aptituderesearch.com/crm-for-recruiters/), 76% of enterprise companies use or plan to use a recruiting CRM. That figure also points to which organizations get the most from one: large companies with dedicated recruiters building talent pipelines for roles that open repeatedly. For a team that hires occasionally and has no dedicated recruiter, the value is harder to justify.

## Where the two tools diverge

The clearest dividing line between them is timing. While a candidate is in an active hiring process for a role, the ATS manages that process. Before that process starts and after it ends, the CRM manages the relationship, usually focusing on candidates recruiters have identified as particularly strong. These include passively sourced candidates and silver medalists who did not get one role but remain interested in future ones.

The difference between the two tools is also one of purpose: compliance versus engagement. An ATS is built around compliance — EEOC reporting, offer letter management, structured interview feedback, and an auditable record of every stage move and decision. A CRM, by contrast, is built around engagement through drip campaigns, event-based outreach, newsletters to a talent pool, and the relationship-building that keeps a passive candidate warm over months or years. An ATS is poorly suited to sending multi-touch nurture sequences for candidates who haven't expressed interest in a specific role yet.

## Where each tool falls short

Neither tool covers the full arc of hiring, and knowing the limits of each is as important as knowing what it does well. The practical limits are worth examining before you buy either one.

### The limits of an ATS

An ATS is designed around active requisitions, and that design is also its boundary. An ATS can send messages, but it tracks the status of the hiring pipeline. It does not track the status of your relationship with a candidate, such as when you were last in contact. It has no feature focused on warming up a passive candidate who matched last year's search, running a nurture campaign for a strong applicant who accepted another offer, or building a talent pool for roles you haven't posted yet. When a new role opens, the search starts from zero unless someone has been keeping their own spreadsheet on the side.

[Olibr](https://olibr.com/blog/recruiting-crm-vs-ats) frames the trade-off plainly: the ATS wins at "structured, time-bound hiring, moving one candidate from application to offer letter," but it was never designed for "everything that happens before and after that window, building a searchable pool you can return to without starting sourcing from scratch." For companies that hire for the same roles repeatedly, that blind spot means restarting sourcing from scratch every time instead of drawing on candidates already vetted. The ATS still contains the record of the strong candidate you passed on eight months ago, but it does not prompt you to stay in touch with them, and it usually has no way to highlight them when a similar role opens. Some more advanced ATSs let you tag candidates as favorites or silver medalists, but those features are where an ATS starts to take on CRM capabilities.

### The limits of a recruiting CRM

A recruiting CRM has practical costs that teams often underestimate before buying. The first is data decay. CRM candidate records "become outdated quickly as people change jobs, phone numbers, and email addresses," according to [Software Finder](https://softwarefinder.com/resources/candidate-relationship-management-systems). An unmaintained database accumulates stale records. A talent pool only pays off when someone keeps it current, and that maintenance is continuous.

Adoption is the second barrier. Without sustained training, leadership buy-in, and clear incentives tied to CRM usage, adoption stalls before recruiters are logging notes, updating statuses, or sending outreach sequences consistently. On a small team where the hiring manager is also conducting the interview, writing the offer, and onboarding the new hire, the time CRM upkeep demands is rarely available.

The third cost is integration. Connecting a CRM to an existing ATS requires technical resources and careful field mapping. Poorly executed integrations result in duplicate candidate records, data loss, and sync errors that erode recruiter trust in the system over time. Teams using an ATS and CRM without clean data handoffs encounter these problems consistently, compounding until recruiters stop relying on either system.

## What using both looks like

Some organizations use an ATS and a recruiting CRM side by side, and for proactive hiring programs, the combination makes sense. The CRM keeps in touch with strong candidates between roles, the ATS manages each hiring round for a specific role that they enter, and an integration keeps both systems in sync. HR Dive reports that building a talent pipeline is the top priority for [48% of organizations](https://www.hrdive.com/news/talent-pipeline-top-priority-for-recruiters/707784/).

The integration gap, however, is real. When the two systems don't share data cleanly, context gets lost at the handoff. A recruiter who spent three months nurturing a candidate may never surface that history to the interviewer. The candidate gets a generic experience; the team loses the advantage of the relationship it built.

Before committing to both, add up the administrative overhead, the data maintenance burden, and the combined subscription cost, then weigh that total against what the setup actually delivers. Using two systems well requires keeping data clean, integrations maintained, and workflows aligned. Teams that underestimate that work end up paying for capabilities they never fully use.

In a setup with both tools, interview feedback belongs in the ATS. The ATS contains records of each interviewer's notes, including the concerns they raised and how the candidate scored against the rubric. That feedback assesses the candidate for one specific role, so it should stay with the record of their application for that role. Keeping it there also limits who can see sensitive information. A CRM manages relationships with people between roles, before and after they apply, and it is not built for feedback on a specific role. For how to structure that feedback, see the guide on [behavioral interview scoring matrices](/blog/behavioral-interview-scoring-matrix).

## The satisfaction problem with recruiting CRMs

High adoption of recruiting CRMs at the enterprise level does not translate to high satisfaction. According to [Aptitude Research](https://www.aptituderesearch.com/crm-for-recruiters/), only 1 in 4 companies express high satisfaction with their CRM vendors, and only 18% would recommend their CRM to another company. Those numbers put satisfaction well below what the spend would justify.

The same research finds that 61% of companies spend more on their recruiting CRM than on their ATS — a reversal of the intuitive priority order. For a tool that manages relationships outside of hiring rounds, the CRM is taking the larger share of the hiring software budget at those companies. High spend and low satisfaction together mean the tool is not returning what it costs.

For growing companies and startups without a dedicated recruiting function, this matters. A CRM makes most sense when a recruiter or talent acquisition specialist owns pipeline-building as their primary responsibility. Without that person, most hiring managers and founders are too stretched to sustain the proactive outreach a CRM requires, so the pipeline stalls. The satisfaction gap at the enterprise level, where dedicated resources are available, makes the case for caution at the startup level, where those resources are not.

## How to choose

The right starting point depends on where your hiring problem actually lives. Here is how that reasoning applies to each type of reader.

### Founding members at startups

For many founding members at startups, the question to settle first is whether you need any dedicated hiring software at all. Once you have more than one or two searches open at a time, email and spreadsheets cause candidates to go uncontacted and feedback to get lost across chains. When two roles are open at once and only one or two people are handling hiring, candidates can wait days for a response because nobody is sure who owns which thread. An ATS solves that specific problem without adding much overhead. A CRM solves the problem of too few qualified candidates reaching you, not too many falling through.

Start with the ATS, and revisit the CRM question when you have a person whose job is proactive sourcing. The candidate experience that the ATS enables also matters beyond the immediate hire. Gallup research finds that employees hired in the last five years who had an exceptional candidate experience are [3.2 times as likely to strongly agree they are connected to their organization's culture](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/651650/lasting-impact-exceptional-candidate-experiences.aspx). A structured ATS process, with consistent communication and clear stages, helps a small team create that experience.

### Hiring managers or ops leaders at growing companies

An ATS is the right place to start here too. If candidates are coming to you through job posts and referrals, your problem is managing the ones who have already applied by tracking where everyone stands, coordinating feedback across the team, and communicating with candidates without losing threads in email. That is what an ATS solves. [SHRM's](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat) 2025 benchmarking data puts the average cost per hire at $5,475 for nonexecutive roles. A structured ATS process is one of the clearest ways to protect that investment, keeping every candidate moving through defined stages so a strong hire does not stall while the team works out who responds next.

A CRM answers a different question. When qualified candidates are scarce rather than unmanaged, and you have the capacity for ongoing outreach to passive talent, a CRM fits that need. For most growing teams, that capacity comes later — once hiring is a function in its own right rather than something the founder or department head squeezes in around their other work. The article on [talent acquisition vs. recruitment](/blog/talent-acquisition-vs-recruitment) covers how the two approaches differ and when each fits.

### Recruiters or HR professionals at larger organizations

If you already have an ATS in place, the question becomes whether a CRM adds enough to justify its cost. The case is strongest when you are hiring for roles that open repeatedly, when passive candidate sourcing is a meaningful part of your process, or when your employer brand is an active driver of applications. If your team is already doing structured sourcing and maintaining talent pools in spreadsheets or a homegrown system, a CRM formalizes and scales what you are already doing.

The case is weaker when your applicant volume is already high, your existing ATS is underused, or the integration between a new CRM and your ATS would require significant technical work. [Aptitude Research](https://www.aptituderesearch.com/crm-for-recruiters/) finds that more than three-quarters of enterprise organizations use or plan to use a recruiting CRM, but only one in four companies is highly satisfied with its CRM vendor. Vendor selection and integration planning matter as much as the decision to buy.

## Do you need an applicant tracking system?

The question of whether you need an ATS comes down to what happens to candidates between the moment they apply and the moment you make a decision. If the answer is that they go into an inbox, their resume lands in a downloads folder, and feedback lives in someone's head or a Slack thread, an ATS solves a real and immediate problem.

Candidates pay a measurable price for that disorganization. [HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/candidates-have-quit-job-applications-before-finishing/758796/) reports that 57% of job candidates have abandoned an application mid-process when it was overly complicated or long. A clean application form and consistent communication — both things an ATS delivers — reduce the friction that causes candidates to drop out before you have even seen their resume. Candidates who have passed the application stage and then stop receiving updates remain at risk of withdrawing. An ATS moves each candidate through the pipeline and sends updates as their application progresses.

Before choosing a tool, a founder or hiring manager should diagnose the actual hiring problem. If too few qualified applicants are reaching you, an ATS won't solve that. Sourcing is a different problem, and a CRM addresses it. If qualified applicants are reaching you and you are losing track of them, moving too slowly, or making decisions without the whole team's input, an ATS is the right tool. And if you are evaluating candidates, coordinating interviewers, writing feedback, and communicating with candidates across multiple open roles at once, an ATS is not optional. It is the infrastructure the process depends on. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is the main difference between an ATS and a recruiting CRM?

An ATS manages candidates who have already applied for a specific role, tracking them through a defined hiring pipeline from application to decision. A recruiting CRM manages relationships with strong candidates between hiring rounds, such as passively sourced candidates and silver medalists a team wants to stay connected with for future roles. The two tools operate at different points in the hiring timeline and solve different problems.

### Do I need both an ATS and a recruiting CRM?

Most growing teams do not need both. An ATS should come first, because it manages the candidates already in your process. A recruiting CRM becomes relevant when you have a recruiter whose job is proactive sourcing and need a system to nurture passive candidates over time. Using both requires clean integration between the systems, sustained data maintenance, and enough volume to justify two subscriptions.

### Can a recruiting CRM replace an ATS?

A recruiting CRM cannot replace an ATS for active hiring. CRMs are not built for compliance workflows, structured interview feedback, offer management, or the stage-by-stage tracking that a live hiring process requires. They are designed for keeping strong candidates warm between hiring rounds.

### What is an applicant tracking system used for?

An applicant tracking system is used to collect job applications in one place, track each candidate through the stages of a hiring process, coordinate interview feedback across the hiring team, communicate with candidates, and maintain a compliance record of every hiring decision. It replaces the combination of email, spreadsheets, and shared documents that most teams start with before hiring becomes too complex to manage that way.

### When does a growing company actually need a recruiting CRM?

A growing company needs a recruiting CRM when it has a dedicated recruiter whose job is building a pipeline of passive candidates for roles that open repeatedly, and when the volume of sourcing work is high enough to justify a second system alongside the ATS. Without that dedicated capacity, the CRM's value depends on outreach and maintenance work that most hiring managers and founders cannot sustain alongside their other responsibilities.

### Are there ATS tools that include CRM features?

Some enterprise ATS platforms include light CRM features such as talent pools or candidate tagging, but these are not substitutes for a dedicated recruiting CRM's sourcing and nurturing workflows. For most growing teams, an ATS with strong candidate management, messaging, and team collaboration features covers the full scope of what active hiring requires, without the complexity of a separate CRM.
