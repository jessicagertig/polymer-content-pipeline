<h2>What screening interview questions are — and what they're for</h2>
<p>A screening interview is a short early-round conversation, usually 20 to 30 minutes by phone, between a recruiter or hiring manager and a candidate before the full interview begins. According to SHRM, a screening interview includes questions designed to eliminate candidates who are not eligible for consideration. The questions cover personality, requirements, and practical points like salary expectations and start date. What you're really doing is deciding which candidates deserve a full hour of the team's time — and which ones can be cleared from the pipeline with a short call.</p>
<p>Screening interview questions differ from full interview questions in scope, not depth. They don't try to uncover everything about a candidate. They're meant to confirm baseline fit quickly: does this person meet the basic requirements, do they understand what the role demands, and do they have a realistic picture of compensation and expectations? A good set of screening questions lets you sort a large applicant pool efficiently, before the deeper, more time-intensive work of a structured full interview.</p>

<h2>Why structured screening makes the difference</h2>
<p>A screening call without a consistent question set is easy to run and hard to compare. When every candidate is asked different questions, you're not comparing answers — you're comparing impressions. Structured interviews, defined by SHRM as interviews in which candidates are asked the same predetermined questions in the same order, with all responses evaluated on the same rating scale, produce far more useful signal. According to ERE, structured interviews predict job performance with 26% accuracy, on par with general cognitive ability tests, and research cited by SHRM shows that structured interviews are twice as predictive of job performance as unstructured ones because they minimize subjectivity and focus on job-relevant criteria.</p>
<p>That predictive edge comes from consistency. When you ask every candidate the same questions and score them on the same rubric, you're measuring the same things. That's true even at the screening stage, where the questions are shorter and lighter than the ones in a full interview. Using a scoring rubric — a five-point scale with specific behavioral indicators at each level — anchors your assessment to what candidates actually said, not how well the conversation flowed.</p>
<p>SHRM's 2024 Talent Trends Report found that 54% of organizations use pre-employment assessments to gauge applicants' knowledge, skills, and abilities during hiring, and 78% of those organizations say the assessments improved their quality of hire. A structured screening call, run consistently with a scored question set, is an assessment. The rigor you apply at the top of the funnel shapes the quality of every hire that comes out of it.</p>

<h2>How to choose your questions and how many to ask</h2>
<p>A 20-to-30-minute screening call has room for five to eight questions, depending on how much follow-up each one draws out. Start with logistical and motivational questions first — salary range, availability, reason for leaving — to establish baseline fit before moving into behavioral or role-specific questions. This follows a natural conversation rhythm and gives candidates time to settle before the more demanding questions arrive.</p>
<p>Choose questions based on what you most need to verify at this stage. For every role, that includes at least one logistical question (compensation, start date, location), one motivational question (why they're leaving, why this role), and one or two role-specific questions tied to the requirements that are hardest to assess from a resume alone. For roles with a large applicant pool, a knockout question on the application form — before the screening call — can filter out candidates who don't meet a non-negotiable requirement, so the call focuses on candidates who already cleared that bar.</p>
<p>Adjust the question mix based on who you're screening. For an intern, the motivational questions carry more weight because work history is thin. For a manager, add a question about how they build and develop a team. For a recruiter, ask about how they source candidates or manage a high-volume pipeline. The core logistical and motivational questions stay constant across the board; the role-specific ones shift with the position.</p>

<h2>Question types that open up real answers</h2>
<p>Not every question format works equally well in a screening call. ERE identifies five types of questions that belong in a structured screen, and knowing when to use each one helps you get more useful information from every candidate.</p>
<p>Direct questions — "How long did you work as a design engineer?" — develop an objective profile quickly. Open-ended questions — "How would you describe your management style?" — draw out depth that direct questions can't reach. Elaborative questions ask the candidate to expand: "Can you give me another example?" Reflective questions restate something the candidate said in question form, which invites them to confirm or clarify. Interpretive questions go one step further, restating with an implied meaning, which prompts the candidate to verify or correct your read of what they said.</p>
<p>For a screening call, the most useful combination is direct questions for logistics and open-ended or elaborative questions for motivation and experience. Follow-up questions are where the real signal comes from. When a candidate's answer is thin, ask: "What did you personally own in that?" or "Can you give me a specific example?" or "What would you do differently now?" Paul Falcone, CHRO at the Motion Picture &amp; Television Fund, as quoted by SHRM, puts it plainly: the best way to get detailed responses is to ask behavioral questions, because they naturally open up follow-up opportunities.</p>

<h2>Is the salary range for this position within your acceptable range?</h2>
<p>This question clears the most basic logistical requirement before anyone spends more time on the process. A candidate who needs significantly more than the role offers will not accept an offer, no matter how well the rest of the interviews go. Asking early is a courtesy to both sides.</p>
<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate gives a specific range or confirms the posted range works. If they're at the edge of the range, they explain the factors that affect their flexibility — equity, benefits, remote work — and show they've thought it through. The answer feels like a conversation, not a negotiation.</p>
<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate is vague ("I'm open to anything"), unwilling to give a number, or names a figure well outside the posted range without acknowledging the gap. Vagueness here often signals that the candidate hasn't thought seriously about whether this role is right for them.</p>
<p><em>Rubric note: This is a logistical question, not a behavioral one. Score on specificity and alignment rather than the five-point behavioral rubric above.</em></p>

<h2>Why are you searching for a new position?</h2>
<p>Motivation is one of the most important and least visible things you're assessing at the screening stage. This question surfaces why the candidate is in the market now — which is different from why they want this particular role — and it gives you an early read on how they talk about their current or most recent employer.</p>
<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate is direct. They point to something concrete: a cap on growth, a shift in the company's direction, or a gap between their current role and where they want to go. The answer connects to what drew them toward this opportunity, not just away from their last one. They speak about past employers without bitterness or blame.</p>
<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate is evasive, gives a generic answer like "I'm just exploring," or speaks negatively about a manager or team in a way that suggests the problem follows them from role to role. Neither is disqualifying on its own, but both warrant a follow-up: "Can you tell me more about what specifically prompted the search?"</p>

<h2>What are the top three duties in the job you now have or in your most recent job?</h2>
<p>Resumes describe titles and outcomes. This question describes daily work. The gap between the two reveals whether the candidate's experience maps to what the role actually requires, and it gives you a basis for the more specific experience questions that follow.</p>
<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate describes their actual work in concrete terms — specific tasks, tools, volume, and scope. They connect their current duties to the requirements of the role you're hiring for, without you having to ask. The answer makes it easy to see where there's overlap and where there are gaps.</p>
<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate describes their title or their team's function rather than their own work. They use vague language ("I handled communications," "I supported the team") that tells you little about what they actually owned. If the answer is thin, follow up: "What did a typical week look like for you in that role?"</p>

<h2>What do you see as your strongest skills, and what are your key challenges?</h2>
<p>This question does two things at once. The strengths half tells you whether the candidate's self-assessment lines up with the role's requirements. The challenges half tells you how self-aware they are — and whether they're growing.</p>
<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> For strengths, SHRM notes that strong candidates tie their answers directly to the duties of the open role — they're not citing generic virtues, they're connecting their self-motivation or technical ability to what the job actually asks of them. For challenges, stronger candidates describe specific steps they're taking to improve, rather than repackaging a strength as a weakness.</p>
<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> A candidate who describes strengths that have no connection to the role, or who can't identify a genuine challenge, is giving you a prepared answer rather than an honest one. Watch for deflection: "I work too hard" or "I care too much" tells you nothing useful about how the person will perform.</p>

<h2>What can you tell me about our company and industry?</h2>
<p>Candidates who want this role, specifically, will have done their homework. This question measures that. SHRM calls it the "candidate desire factor" — the degree to which a candidate's research went beyond the company homepage.</p>
<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate references something specific: a recent product launch, a press article, a shift in the industry, or something from the company's own job description that they connected to their background. They show they understand what the company does and why the role exists within that context.</p>
<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate describes what the company does in the same words as the homepage, or admits they haven't had time to research. Neither is an automatic disqualifier — a candidate who's genuinely excited about the role but applied quickly may still be worth talking to — but it's worth noting as a signal about how deliberate they are as a job seeker.</p>

<h2>Tell me about a recent piece of work you're particularly proud of. What did you personally contribute?</h2>
<p>Candidates naturally describe team achievements in the first person plural. This question cuts through that by asking what they owned. The answer reveals decision-making, initiative, and the kind of impact they're able to point to in their own name.</p>
<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate describes a specific project or deliverable, explains the decisions they made, and separates their contribution from the team's. The best answers include a measurable result — revenue generated, time saved, error rate reduced — and reflect on what made their contribution effective. The answer should make it possible for you to understand what this person can do without anyone else's help.</p>
<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate describes a team achievement and can't distinguish their role from the group's. They lean heavily on "we" without ever getting to "I." If the follow-up "What specifically did you own?" doesn't sharpen the answer, it's worth noting: either the contribution was limited, or the candidate lacks the self-awareness to articulate it.</p>

<h2>Which part of this role would be most familiar to you, and which would be the biggest stretch?</h2>
<p>This question does something most screening questions don't: it invites the candidate to identify their own gaps. That takes self-awareness, and it also gives you useful information about how they'll approach the parts of the job that are new to them.</p>
<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate is specific on both sides. They connect the familiar parts to concrete past experience, and they describe the stretch honestly — without catastrophizing or dismissing it. Strong candidates often add how they would approach the stretch, which signals that they've already started thinking about how to succeed in the role.</p>
<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate says everything looks familiar, or describes the stretch in terms so vague that it's clear they haven't read the job description closely. A candidate who can't identify a genuine stretch is either overconfident or hasn't engaged seriously with what the role requires.</p>

<h2>Why did you leave your previous employer, or why are you leaving your present job?</h2>
<p>This question is similar to the motivation question earlier, but it's more direct about departure. Where the earlier question asks about the search, this one asks about the leave. The answers are related but not always the same — and together, they give you a fuller picture of what drives the candidate's decisions.</p>
<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate is straightforward. They explain the circumstances clearly — a layoff, a restructure, a cap on the kind of work they wanted to do — and they speak without bitterness. The answer should sound like something that happened to them or a decision they made deliberately, not a grievance they're carrying.</p>
<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate describes conflict with a manager or team in terms that place all the fault elsewhere. That's not always a warning sign — sometimes the situation really was difficult — but it's worth a follow-up: "What would you have done differently?" A candidate who can't reflect on their own role in a difficult departure gives you less to work with.</p>

<h2>Do you have any questions for me?</h2>
<p>The questions a candidate asks at the end of a screening call tell you as much as most of the questions you asked them. Good questions show that the candidate has thought seriously about the role, the team, or what success looks like in the first few months. They also give you a window into what matters to this person as they make their own decision.</p>
<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate asks something specific: about the team, about the success criteria for the role in the first 90 days, about the challenges the last person in the role faced, or about how decisions get made. The best questions show that the candidate is evaluating the role, not just trying to get it.</p>
<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate says they have no questions, or asks only about vacation policy and benefits. That's not disqualifying, but it's a signal about engagement. A candidate who is genuinely excited about the role usually has something they want to understand better.</p>

<h2>How to run the screening call and use the rubric</h2>
<p>Send the candidate the screening interview questions, or at least the topic areas, in advance. This isn't about giving away the answers — it's about letting candidates prepare so that their responses reflect actual experience rather than on-the-spot recall. The goal is signal about the candidate, not surprise.</p>
<p>Run the call with your question list in front of you and a scoring sheet beside it. After each answer, mark your score and a brief note about what the candidate said. The note doesn't need to be a full transcript — a phrase or two that captures the specific detail is enough. That detail is what you'll need when you compare candidates later or explain a screening decision to the rest of the hiring team.</p>
<p>After the call, fill in any scores you didn't mark in real time. If a question didn't produce a useful answer, note that too — a score of 1 or 2 is itself information. When two or more team members run separate screens, compare scores before discussing impressions. The numbers surface disagreement faster than conversation does, and that disagreement is often where the most useful discussion happens.</p>

<p>Ten minutes of the right questions saves an hour of interviews. If you've ever spent a full hour with a candidate only to discover a salary mismatch, a schedule conflict, or a gap in core requirements that a two-minute question would have surfaced, you already understand what a screening interview is for. The challenge is knowing which questions cut through fastest — and how to compare answers across candidates once the calls are done.</p>

<p>A screening interview is a short early-round conversation, typically 20 to 30 minutes by phone, between a recruiter or hiring manager and a candidate before the full interview. SHRM describes it as a call with questions designed to confirm eligibility and cover practical points — salary expectations, start date, basic qualifications — before the team invests more time. The questions are shorter and lighter than the ones in a full interview, but they serve a precise purpose: determining which candidates deserve the next hour of your team's time.</p>

<p>This article covers what makes screening interview questions effective, how to structure the call, and eight questions you can use in your next screen — each with a scoring rubric so you can evaluate every candidate on the same basis.</p>

<h2>What makes a screening question work</h2>

<p>Effective screening questions share four qualities. They are brief enough for a candidate to answer in under a minute. They are tied directly to a competency or logistical requirement. They produce responses that can be scored consistently across all candidates. And they avoid phrasing that telegraphs the right answer before the candidate speaks — a question like "We value collaboration here. How do you work in a team?" has already told the candidate what to say.</p>

<p>Open-ended questions reveal more than yes-or-no checks do. A candidate who answers "Tell me about your most recent role's top three duties" gives you a window into their actual daily work, not just their job title. Behavioral questions go further still: Paul Falcone, CHRO at the Motion Picture &amp; Television Fund, as quoted by SHRM, notes that behavioral questions naturally open up follow-up opportunities. When a candidate says they dislike something, the interviewer can probe the last time that happened, the circumstances, and what came of it.</p>

<p>Follow-up questions are where the real signal comes from. Good follow-ups include "What did you personally own?", "Can you give me a specific example?", "What made that difficult?", and "What would you do differently now?" A candidate's first answer often describes what happened. The follow-up reveals how they think.</p>

<h2>How structured screening changes what you learn</h2>

<p>A screening call without a consistent question set is easy to run and hard to compare. When every candidate gets different questions, you are not comparing answers — you are comparing impressions. SHRM defines a structured interview as one in which candidates are asked the same predetermined questions in the same order, with all responses evaluated on the same rating scale. That consistency is what makes the screen useful as a decision tool.</p>

<p>The evidence for structured interviews is strong. According to <a href="https://www.ere.net/why-structured-interviews-are-the-best-way-to-hire/">ERE</a>, structured interviews predict job performance with 26% accuracy, on par with general cognitive ability tests. Research cited by <a href="https://vendordirectory.shrm.org/company/354019/whitepapers/11772/the-definitive-guide-to-structured-interviewing">SHRM</a> shows that structured interviews are twice as predictive of job performance as unstructured ones, because they minimize subjectivity and focus on job-relevant criteria. That predictive advantage holds even at the screening stage, where the questions are lighter than those in a full interview.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.shrm.org/content/dam/en/shrm/research/2024-talent-trends-research-overall-findings.pdf">SHRM 2024 Talent Trends Report</a> found that 54% of organizations use pre-employment assessments to gauge applicants' knowledge, skills, and abilities during hiring — and 78% of those organizations say the assessments improved their quality of hire. A structured screening call, run consistently with a scored question set, is an assessment. The rigor you apply at the top of the funnel shapes the quality of every hire that follows.</p>

<h2>How to choose your questions and sequence the call</h2>

<p>A 20-to-30-minute screen has room for five to eight questions, depending on how much follow-up each one draws out. Sequence matters. Start with logistical and motivational questions — compensation range, availability, reason for leaving — to establish baseline fit before moving into behavioral or role-specific questions. This follows a natural conversation rhythm and gives candidates time to settle before the more demanding questions arrive.</p>

<p>Choose questions based on what you most need to verify at this stage. For every role, that includes at least one logistical question (compensation, start date, location), one motivational question (why they are leaving, why this role), and one or two role-specific questions tied to requirements that are hard to assess from a resume alone. For roles with a large applicant pool, a knockout question on the application form — before the screening call — can filter out candidates who don't meet a non-negotiable requirement, so the call focuses only on those who already cleared that bar.</p>

<p>Adjust the question mix based on who you're screening. For an intern, motivational questions carry more weight because work history is thin; focus on what drew them to the field and how they approach new challenges. For a manager, add a question about how they build and develop a team. For a recruiter, ask about how they source candidates or handle a high-volume pipeline. The logistical and motivational questions stay constant across the board; the role-specific ones shift with the position.</p>

<p>Avoid two common failure modes in question design. Double-barreled questions — "Tell me about your communication style and how you handle deadlines" — ask two things at once and rarely get a complete answer to either. Questions that touch on protected characteristics, even indirectly, create legal risk and produce no useful information. Keep each question focused on one job-relevant competency.</p>

<h2>Is the salary range for this position within your acceptable range?</h2>

<p>This question clears the most basic logistical requirement before anyone spends more time on the process. A candidate who needs significantly more than the role offers will not accept an offer, no matter how well the rest of the interviews go. Asking early is a courtesy to both sides, and it focuses every subsequent conversation on candidates who are genuinely in range.</p>

<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate gives a specific range or confirms the posted range works for them. If they're at the edge of the range, they explain the factors affecting their flexibility — equity, benefits, or remote work — and show they've thought the trade-offs through. The answer feels like a conversation, not a negotiation.</p>

<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate is vague ("I'm open to anything"), unwilling to give a number, or states a figure well outside the posted range without acknowledging the gap. Vagueness here often signals that the candidate hasn't seriously considered whether this role is right for them. Follow up: "We're budgeted in the range of X to Y — does that work for where you are?"</p>

A candidate who speaks negatively about a manager or team in a way that suggests the problem follows them from role to role is worth noting. <h2>What are the top three duties in your current or most recent job?</h2>

<p>Resumes describe titles and outcomes. This question surfaces daily work. The gap between the two reveals whether the candidate's experience maps to what the role actually requires, and it gives you a basis for the more specific questions that follow. SHRM includes this question on its standard list of screening questions for exactly this reason.</p>

<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate describes their actual work in concrete terms — specific tasks, tools, volume, and scope. They connect their current duties to the requirements of the role you're hiring for, without you having to prompt the connection. The answer makes it straightforward to see where there is overlap and where there are gaps worth exploring in the full interview.</p>

<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate describes their title or their team's function rather than their own work. They use broad language — "I handled communications," "I supported the team" — that tells you little about what they personally owned. If the answer stays thin after the initial question, follow up: "What did a typical week look like for you in that role?"</p>

<h2>What do you see as your strongest skills, and what are your key challenges?</h2>

<p>This question does two things at once. The strengths half tells you whether the candidate's self-assessment lines up with the role's requirements. The challenges half tells you how self-aware they are — and whether they're growing. Together, the two halves give you a more complete picture than either would on its own.</p>

<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> For strengths, SHRM notes that strong candidates tie their answers directly to the duties of the open role. They are not citing generic virtues; they are connecting their self-motivation or technical ability to what the job actually asks of them. For challenges, the more self-aware candidates describe specific steps they are taking to improve, rather than repackaging a strength as a weakness.</p>

<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> A candidate who describes strengths with no connection to the role, or who can't identify a genuine challenge, is giving you a prepared answer rather than an honest one. Watch for deflection: "I work too hard" or "I care too much" tells you nothing about how the person will perform in the role.</p>

<h2>What can you tell me about our company and industry?</h2>

<p>Candidates who want this role, specifically, will have done their homework. This question measures that. SHRM calls it the "candidate desire factor" — the degree to which a candidate's research went beyond the company homepage. The answer tells you how deliberately the candidate pursued this application, and it signals how they approach new information in general.</p>

<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate references something specific: a recent product launch, a press article, a shift in the competitive landscape, or something from the job description that they connected to their own background. They show they understand what the company does and why the role exists within that context.</p>

<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate describes the company in the same words as the homepage, or admits they haven't had time to look into it. The former suggests surface-level preparation; the latter is worth noting as a signal about how seriously the candidate is pursuing this opportunity. A quick follow-up — "What about the role caught your attention?" — can sometimes recover useful information even when the company knowledge is thin.</p>

<h2>Tell me about a recent piece of work you're particularly proud of. What did you personally contribute?</h2>

<p>Candidates naturally describe team achievements in the first person plural. This question cuts through that by asking what they owned. The answer reveals decision-making, initiative, and the kind of impact they can point to in their own name — which is precisely the information a resume's "we" language leaves out.</p>

<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate describes a specific project or deliverable, explains the decisions they made, and separates their contribution from the team's. The best answers include a measurable result — revenue generated, time saved, error rate reduced — and reflect on what made their contribution effective. The answer should make it possible to understand what this person can deliver without anyone else's help.</p>

<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate describes a team achievement and can't distinguish their role from the group's. They lean heavily on "we" without ever getting to "I." If the follow-up "What specifically did you own?" doesn't sharpen the answer, the contribution was either limited or the candidate lacks the self-awareness to articulate it. Both are worth noting before the full interview.</p>

<h2>Which part of this role would be most familiar to you, and which would be the biggest stretch?</h2>

<p>This question does something most screening questions don't: it invites the candidate to identify their own gaps. That takes self-awareness, and the answer gives you useful information about how they will approach the parts of the job that are new to them. A candidate who can name a genuine stretch — and show they've thought about how to close it — is demonstrating something a resume cannot.</p>

<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate is specific on both sides. They connect the familiar parts to concrete past experience, and they describe the stretch honestly without catastrophizing or dismissing it. The best answers include how the candidate would approach the stretch — what they would read, whom they would talk to, how they would get up to speed. That forward-thinking signals genuine engagement with the role.</p>

<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate says everything looks familiar, or describes the stretch in terms so broad that it's clear they haven't read the job description closely. A candidate who can't identify a genuine stretch is either overconfident or hasn't engaged seriously with what the role requires. Either way, it's information worth having before you schedule the full interview.</p>

<h2>Do you have any questions for me?</h2>

<p>The questions a candidate asks at the end of a screening call tell you as much as most of the questions you asked them. Thoughtful questions show that the candidate has considered the role, the team, or what success looks like in the first few months. They also give you a window into what matters to this person as they make their own decision — because a screening call runs in both directions.</p>

<p><strong>What a strong answer looks like:</strong> The candidate asks something specific: about the team's current priorities, the success criteria for the role in the first 90 days, the challenges the previous person in the role faced, or how decisions get made on the team. The best questions show that the candidate is evaluating the role, not simply trying to get it. That's the kind of deliberateness you want to see before you bring someone in for a full interview.</p>

<p><strong>What a weak answer looks like:</strong> The candidate says they have no questions, or asks only about vacation policy and benefits. That's not disqualifying on its own, but it's a signal about engagement. A candidate who is genuinely interested in the role usually has something they want to understand better — about the work, the team, or the company's direction.</p>

<h2>How to run the call and use the rubric</h2>

<p>Send candidates the topic areas — if not the exact questions — before the call. This isn't about telegraphing the answers; it's about letting candidates prepare so their responses reflect actual experience rather than on-the-spot recall. The goal is signal about the candidate, not the ability to answer under surprise conditions.</p>

<p>Run the call with your question list in front of you and your scoring sheet beside it. Mark your score and a brief note after each answer — a phrase or two capturing the specific detail the candidate offered. That note is what you'll need when you compare candidates across a full week of screens or explain a decision to the rest of the hiring team. If a question produced a thin answer, a score of 1 or 2 is itself information worth recording.</p>

<p>When two or more team members run separate screens, compare scores before discussing impressions. <a href="https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat">SHRM's 2025 benchmarking data</a> shows that screening and interviewing average eight to nine days each within the overall hiring process. A shared scoring sheet surfaces disagreement faster than conversation alone — and that disagreement is often where the most useful discussion about a candidate happens.</p>

<p>After the scores are in, set a threshold before you start screening — a minimum total score, or a minimum score on any one question that is non-negotiable for the role. Setting that threshold in advance keeps the decision from drifting toward whoever made the best impression on the call rather than whoever gave the strongest answers.</p>

<h2>Put these questions to work</h2>

<p>A consistent set of screening interview questions, scored on the same rubric for every candidate, gives you a reliable basis for the decisions you make before the full interview begins. Candidates who advance are better matched to the role, and your team's time in later rounds goes toward the people most likely to succeed.</p>
