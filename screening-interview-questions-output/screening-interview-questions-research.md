## Definitions

**Screening interview (screening call):** A short early-round conversation between a recruiter and a candidate, before the full interview. Polymer describes it as a call where "a recruiter or someone else on the team gets on the phone with the candidate with a short list of questions in front of them," with questions "meant to get a sense of the candidate's personality, confirm a few requirements, and cover practical points such as salary expectations and start date." Source: Polymer, "How Does an Applicant Tracking System Work? A Clear Guide," https://www.polymer.co/blog/how-does-an-applicant-tracking-system-work. SHRM adds that "a typical pre-screening telephone interview lasts 20 to 30 minutes and includes questions designed to eliminate candidates who are not eligible for consideration." Source: SHRM, "Screening and Evaluating Candidates," https://www.shrm.org/ResourcesAndTools/tools-and-samples/toolkits/Pages/screeningandevaluatingcandidates.aspx.

**Structured interview:** SHRM defines it as "an interview in which candidates are asked the same predetermined questions in the same order," where "all responses given by the candidates are evaluated using the same rating scale." Source: SHRM, "SELECTION CRITERIA Overall SHRM Benchmarking: Talent Access," https://www.shrm.org/content/dam/en/shrm/topics-tools/legal-compliance/talent-access-report-total.pdf.

**Scoring rubric:** A tool used alongside a structured interview; Polymer describes it as "a five-point scale to evaluate how well someone meets the requirements." Source: Polymer, "How to Write Personalized Job Rejection Emails," https://www.polymer.co/blog/job-rejection-email.

## List items

**What makes screening interview questions effective — and what the best ones look like**

---

**Source 1: SHRM, "Screening and Evaluating Candidates"**
URL: https://www.shrm.org/ResourcesAndTools/tools-and-samples/toolkits/Pages/screeningandevaluatingcandidates.aspx

SHRM notes that a typical pre-screening telephone interview lasts 20 to 30 minutes and includes questions designed to eliminate candidates who are not eligible for consideration. SHRM offers this list of example screening questions exactly as published:

- Is the salary range for this position within your acceptable range?
- Why are you searching for a new position?
- What are the top three duties in the job you now have or in your most recent job?
- What is your highest degree?
- What do you see as your strongest skills, and what are your key challenges?

SHRM also notes that enabling candidates to share answers with depth and breadth about skills, knowledge, and experiences gives a hiring manager much more useful information than closed formats.

---

**Source 2: SHRM, "10 Classic Interview Questions and the Answers You Hope to Hear"**
URL: https://www.shrm.org/in/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/10-classic-interview-questions-answers-hope-to-hear

SHRM quotes Paul Falcone, CHRO at the Motion Picture & Television Fund, on what makes screening questions work: "When they are doing their jobs effectively, interviewers know that the best way to coax detailed responses is to ask behavioral questions." The article explains that behavioral questions naturally open up follow-up opportunities — if a candidate says they dislike something, the interviewer can probe the last time that happened, the circumstances, and results. SHRM's list of classic questions, given exactly as published:

1. Can you please tell me a little about yourself?
2. Why did you leave your previous employer (or why are you leaving your present job)?
3. What are your greatest strengths?
4. What are your weaknesses?
5. What can you tell me about our company and industry?
6. What do/did you like most and least about your present/most recent job?
7. Tell me what isn't on your resume.
8. Aren't you underqualified or overqualified for this position (depending on their past experience)?
9. Has your perception of this job opportunity changed based on our interview?
10. Do you have any questions? Can you think of anything else you'd like to add?

What makes answers strong: For strengths, look for self-motivation, initiative, and ability to work in a team, tied directly to duties of the open job. For weaknesses, savvier candidates talk about steps they are taking to improve. For company knowledge, strong candidates demonstrate research that went beyond the company's homepage — SHRM calls this the "candidate desire factor."

**Source 3: ERE, "Coach Your Candidate For These Five Types Of Interview Questions"**
URL: https://ere.net/articles/coach-your-candidate-for-these-five-types-of-interview-questions

ERE identifies five types of questions that belong in a structured screen, given exactly as published:

1. **Direct Question:** One that can be answered adequately in a few words. Examples: "How long did you work as a design engineer?" / "What was your favorite subject in college?"
2. **Open-end Question:** Examples: "Would you tell me about your career since you left Company X?" / "How would you describe your management style?" / "What are your strengths?"
3. **Elaborative Question:** Examples: "Could you give me another example of how you fired someone?" / "Will you expand on what you did to revise the accounting procedures?" / "What else did you do while you were in the marketing department?"
4. **Reflective Question:** A restatement or summary of an earlier statement in question form. Example — Candidate: "Once the project was over, I started having disagreements with my supervisor." Interviewer: "You had disagreements with your supervisor?"
5. **Interpretive Question:** A reflective question with an implied answer. Example — Candidate: "Once the project was over, I started having disagreements with my supervisor." Interviewer: "Could it be that the disagreements resulted from the recognition you received from top management when the project was completed?"

ERE notes that direct questions develop an objective profile of the candidate, while open-end, elaborative, and interpretive questions draw out depth that direct questions cannot.

**Source 4: Metaview, "The 20 best screening interview questions, & what to ask next"**
URL: https://www.metaview.ai/resources/interview-questions/screening-interview-questions

Metaview states that the best screening questions help the interviewer understand a candidate's relevant experience, motivation, expectations, and practical fit for the role. What makes them effective: open-ended questions reveal more than yes/no checks; a good question bank is a starting point, not a script; and follow-up questions are where useful signal emerges. Metaview's follow-up question list, given exactly as published:

- What did you personally own?
- Can you give me a specific example?
- Why did you make that decision?
- What made that difficult?
- What happened next?
- Who else was involved?
- How did you know it worked?
- What would you do differently now?
- What do you mean by that?
- Why was that important to you?

Metaview also highlights two example questions with what makes them work: "Tell me about a recent piece of work you're particularly proud of. What did you personally contribute?" — because candidates naturally describe team achievements as "we," asking about personal contribution reveals what they actually owned, the decisions they made, and the impact they had. And: "Which part of this role would be most familiar to you, and which would be the biggest stretch?" — because it gives candidates room to talk about both strengths and gaps.

**Source 5: evy.io, "How to Write Effective Screening Interview Questions"**
URL: https://evy.io/blog/how-to-write-effective-screening-interview-questions/

Evy.io states that effective screening questions share four characteristics, given exactly as published: they are brief enough for a candidate to answer in under a minute; they are tied directly to a competency or logistical requirement; they produce responses that can be scored consistently across all candidates; and they avoid phrasing that telegraphs the "right" answer before the candidate speaks.

The source also names common failure modes to avoid, given exactly as published:

- Questions that contain the answer ("We value collaboration here. How do you work in a team?")
- Double-barreled questions that ask two things at once ("Tell me about your communication style and how you handle deadlines.")
- Questions that touch on protected characteristics, even indirectly ("Do you have children? How do you handle work-life balance?")

On sequencing: start with logistical and motivational questions to establish baseline fit before moving into behavioral and technical questions. This mirrors a natural conversation flow and gives candidates time to settle in before the more demanding questions arrive.

On scoring: a 1-to-5 anchored scoring rubric with specific behavioral indicators is the most validated tool. Each score level must be defined with observable, behavior-based descriptions. The source gives this rubric exactly as published:

| Score | Behavioral description |
|-------|------------------------|
| 1 | Candidate gave no specific example; response was entirely general or off-topic. |
| 2 | Candidate referenced a situation but provided no detail on actions taken. |
| 3 | Candidate described a situation and actions but did not address the outcome. |
| 4 | Candidate gave a clear situation, specific actions, and a measurable outcome. |
| 5 | Candidate gave a clear example with measurable outcome and reflected on what they learned or would do differently. |
