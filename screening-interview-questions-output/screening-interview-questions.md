# Screening Interview Questions: What to Ask Before the Full Interview

Ask the right questions in a screening call and your team avoids spending a full round of interviews on candidates who were never a real fit. The challenge is that most screening calls run on instinct rather than a consistent question set, which means the first round produces impressions instead of information you can compare.

A screening interview is a short early-round conversation — typically 20 to 30 minutes — where someone on the hiring team works through a focused list of questions to confirm basic fit before bringing a candidate in for the full interview. The questions cover practical requirements like compensation and availability, motivational factors like why the candidate is in the market, and one or two role-specific probes that a resume alone can't answer.

This article covers which questions to ask at the screening stage and what each one is designed to reveal, how to sequence the call, how to score answers consistently across every candidate, and how to use the results to decide who moves forward.

## What makes a screening question work

Effective screening questions share four qualities. They are brief enough for a candidate to answer in under a minute. They are tied directly to a competency or logistical requirement. They produce responses that can be scored consistently across all candidates. And they avoid phrasing that telegraphs the right answer before the candidate speaks — a question like "We value collaboration here. How do you work in a team?" has already told the candidate what to say.

Open-ended questions reveal more than yes-or-no checks do. A candidate who answers "Tell me about your most recent role's top three duties" gives you a window into their actual daily work, not just their job title. Behavioral questions go further still: Paul Falcone, CHRO at the Motion Picture & Television Fund, as quoted by SHRM, notes that behavioral questions naturally open up follow-up opportunities. When a candidate says they dislike something, the interviewer can probe the last time that happened, the circumstances, and what came of it.

Follow-up questions are where the real signal comes from. Good follow-ups include "What did you personally own?", "Can you give me a specific example?", "What made that difficult?", and "What would you do differently now?" A candidate's first answer often describes what happened. The follow-up reveals how they think.

## How structured screening changes what you learn

A screening call without a consistent question set is easy to run and hard to compare. When every candidate gets different questions, you are not comparing answers — you are comparing impressions. SHRM defines a structured interview as one in which candidates are asked the same predetermined questions in the same order, with all responses evaluated on the same rating scale. That consistency is what makes the screen useful as a decision tool.

The evidence for structured interviews is strong. According to [ERE](https://www.ere.net/why-structured-interviews-are-the-best-way-to-hire/), structured interviews predict job performance with 26% accuracy, on par with general cognitive ability tests. Research cited by [SHRM](https://vendordirectory.shrm.org/company/354019/whitepapers/11772/the-definitive-guide-to-structured-interviewing) shows that structured interviews are twice as predictive of job performance as unstructured ones, because they minimize subjectivity and focus on job-relevant criteria. That predictive advantage holds even at the screening stage, where the questions are lighter than those in a full interview.

The [SHRM 2024 Talent Trends Report](https://www.shrm.org/content/dam/en/shrm/research/2024-talent-trends-research-overall-findings.pdf) found that 54% of organizations use pre-employment assessments to gauge applicants' knowledge, skills, and abilities during hiring — and 78% of those organizations say the assessments improved their quality of hire. A structured screening call, run consistently with a scored question set, is an assessment. The rigor you apply at the top of the funnel shapes the quality of every hire that follows.

## How to choose your questions and sequence the call

A 20-to-30-minute screen has room for five to eight questions, depending on how much follow-up each one draws out. Sequence matters. Start with logistical and motivational questions — compensation range, availability, reason for leaving — to establish baseline fit before moving into behavioral or role-specific questions. This follows a natural conversation rhythm and gives candidates time to settle before the more demanding questions arrive.

Choose questions based on what you most need to verify at this stage. For every role, that includes at least one logistical question (compensation, start date, location), one motivational question (why they are leaving, why this role), and one or two role-specific questions tied to requirements that are hard to assess from a resume alone. For roles with a large applicant pool, a knockout question on the application form — before the screening call — can filter out candidates who don't meet a non-negotiable requirement, so the call focuses only on those who already cleared that bar.

Adjust the question mix based on who you're screening. For an intern, motivational questions carry more weight because work history is thin; focus on what drew them to the field and how they approach new challenges. For a manager, add a question about how they build and develop a team. For a recruiter, ask about how they source candidates or handle a high-volume pipeline. The logistical and motivational questions stay constant across the board; the role-specific ones shift with the position.

Avoid two common failure modes in question design. Double-barreled questions — "Tell me about your communication style and how you handle deadlines" — ask two things at once and rarely get a complete answer to either. Questions that touch on protected characteristics, even indirectly, create legal risk and produce no useful information. Keep each question focused on one job-relevant competency.

## The scoring rubric to use with every question

Each behavioral question in a structured screen should be scored on the same five-point scale. The rubric below anchors each score to specific, observable behavior so two interviewers scoring the same candidate will land close to the same number. Use this alongside every behavioral question in the set that follows.

Mark your score and a brief note after each answer — a phrase or two capturing the specific detail. That note is what you'll need when you compare candidates later or explain a screening decision to the hiring team. For logistical questions, score on alignment and specificity rather than this behavioral scale.

## Is the salary range for this position within your acceptable range?

This question clears the most basic logistical requirement before anyone spends more time on the process. A candidate who needs significantly more than the role offers will not accept an offer, no matter how well the rest of the interviews go. Asking early is a courtesy to both sides, and it focuses every subsequent conversation on candidates who are genuinely in range.

**What a strong answer looks like:** The candidate gives a specific range or confirms the posted range works for them. If they're at the edge of the range, they explain the factors affecting their flexibility — equity, benefits, or remote work — and show they've thought the trade-offs through. The answer feels like a conversation, not a negotiation.

**What a weak answer looks like:** The candidate is vague ("I'm open to anything"), unwilling to give a number, or states a figure well outside the posted range without acknowledging the gap. Vagueness here often signals that the candidate hasn't seriously considered whether this role is right for them. Follow up: "We're budgeted in the range of X to Y — does that work for where you are?"

## Why are you searching for a new position?

Motivation is one of the most important and least visible things you're assessing at the screening stage. This question surfaces why the candidate is in the market now — which is distinct from why they want this particular role — and gives you an early read on how they talk about their current or most recent employer.

**What a strong answer looks like:** The candidate is direct. They point to something concrete: a cap on growth, a shift in the company's direction, or a gap between their current role and where they want to go. The answer connects to what drew them toward this opportunity rather than only away from their last one, and they describe past employers without bitterness or blame.

**What a weak answer looks like:** The candidate is evasive or gives a generic answer like "I'm just exploring." A candidate who speaks negatively about a manager or team in a way that suggests the problem follows them from role to role is worth noting. Neither is automatically disqualifying, but both warrant a follow-up: "Can you say more about what specifically prompted the search?"

## What are the top three duties in your current or most recent job?

Resumes describe titles and outcomes. This question surfaces daily work. The gap between the two reveals whether the candidate's experience maps to what the role actually requires, and it gives you a basis for the more specific questions that follow. SHRM includes this question on its standard list of screening questions for exactly this reason.

**What a strong answer looks like:** The candidate describes their actual work in concrete terms — specific tasks, tools, volume, and scope. They connect their current duties to the requirements of the role you're hiring for, without you having to prompt the connection. The answer makes it straightforward to see where there is overlap and where there are gaps worth exploring in the full interview.

**What a weak answer looks like:** The candidate describes their title or their team's function rather than their own work. They use broad language — "I handled communications," "I supported the team" — that tells you little about what they personally owned. If the answer stays thin after the initial question, follow up: "What did a typical week look like for you in that role?"

## What do you see as your strongest skills, and what are your key challenges?

This question does two things at once. The strengths half tells you whether the candidate's self-assessment lines up with the role's requirements. The challenges half tells you how self-aware they are — and whether they're growing. Together, the two halves give you a more complete picture than either would on its own.

**What a strong answer looks like:** For strengths, SHRM notes that strong candidates tie their answers directly to the duties of the open role. They are not citing generic virtues; they are connecting their self-motivation or technical ability to what the job actually asks of them. For challenges, the more self-aware candidates describe specific steps they are taking to improve, rather than repackaging a strength as a weakness.

**What a weak answer looks like:** A candidate who describes strengths with no connection to the role, or who can't identify a genuine challenge, is giving you a prepared answer rather than an honest one. Watch for deflection: "I work too hard" or "I care too much" tells you nothing about how the person will perform in the role.

## What can you tell me about our company and industry?

Candidates who want this role, specifically, will have done their homework. This question measures that. SHRM calls it the "candidate desire factor" — the degree to which a candidate's research went beyond the company homepage. The answer tells you how deliberately the candidate pursued this application, and it signals how they approach new information in general.

**What a strong answer looks like:** The candidate references something specific: a recent product launch, a press article, a shift in the competitive landscape, or something from the job description that they connected to their own background. They show they understand what the company does and why the role exists within that context.

**What a weak answer looks like:** The candidate describes the company in the same words as the homepage, or admits they haven't had time to look into it. The former suggests surface-level preparation; the latter is worth noting as a signal about how seriously the candidate is pursuing this opportunity. A quick follow-up — "What about the role caught your attention?" — can sometimes recover useful information even when the company knowledge is thin.

## Tell me about a recent piece of work you're particularly proud of. What did you personally contribute?

Candidates naturally describe team achievements in the first person plural. This question cuts through that by asking what they owned. The answer reveals decision-making, initiative, and the kind of impact they can point to in their own name — which is precisely the information a resume's "we" language leaves out.

**What a strong answer looks like:** The candidate describes a specific project or deliverable, explains the decisions they made, and separates their contribution from the team's. The best answers include a measurable result — revenue generated, time saved, error rate reduced — and reflect on what made their contribution effective. The answer should make it possible to understand what this person can deliver without anyone else's help.

**What a weak answer looks like:** The candidate describes a team achievement and can't distinguish their role from the group's. They lean heavily on "we" without ever getting to "I." If the follow-up "What specifically did you own?" doesn't sharpen the answer, the contribution was either limited or the candidate lacks the self-awareness to articulate it. Both are worth noting before the full interview.

## Which part of this role would be most familiar to you, and which would be the biggest stretch?

This question does something most screening questions don't: it invites the candidate to identify their own gaps. That takes self-awareness, and the answer gives you useful information about how they will approach the parts of the job that are new to them. A candidate who can name a genuine stretch — and show they've thought about how to close it — is demonstrating something a resume cannot.

**What a strong answer looks like:** The candidate is specific on both sides. They connect the familiar parts to concrete past experience, and they describe the stretch honestly without catastrophizing or dismissing it. The best answers include how the candidate would approach the stretch — what they would read, whom they would talk to, how they would get up to speed. That forward-thinking signals genuine engagement with the role.

**What a weak answer looks like:** The candidate says everything looks familiar, or describes the stretch in terms so broad that it's clear they haven't read the job description closely. A candidate who can't identify a genuine stretch is either overconfident or hasn't engaged seriously with what the role requires. Either way, it's information worth having before you schedule the full interview.

## Do you have any questions for me?

The questions a candidate asks at the end of a screening call tell you as much as most of the questions you asked them. Thoughtful questions show that the candidate has considered the role, the team, or what success looks like in the first few months. They also give you a window into what matters to this person as they make their own decision — because a screening call runs in both directions.

**What a strong answer looks like:** The candidate asks something specific: about the team's current priorities, the success criteria for the role in the first 90 days, the challenges the previous person in the role faced, or how decisions get made on the team. The best questions show that the candidate is evaluating the role, not simply trying to get it. That's the kind of deliberateness you want to see before you bring someone in for a full interview.

**What a weak answer looks like:** The candidate says they have no questions, or asks only about vacation policy and benefits. That's not disqualifying on its own, but it's a signal about engagement. A candidate who is genuinely interested in the role usually has something they want to understand better — about the work, the team, or the company's direction.

## How to run the call and use the rubric

Send candidates the topic areas — if not the exact questions — before the call. This isn't about telegraphing the answers; it's about letting candidates prepare so their responses reflect actual experience rather than on-the-spot recall. The goal is signal about the candidate, not the ability to answer under surprise conditions.

Run the call with your question list in front of you and your scoring sheet beside it. Mark your score and a brief note after each answer — a phrase or two capturing the specific detail the candidate offered. That note is what you'll need when you compare candidates across a full week of screens or explain a decision to the rest of the hiring team. If a question produced a thin answer, a score of 1 or 2 is itself information worth recording.

When two or more team members run separate screens, compare scores before discussing impressions. [SHRM's 2025 benchmarking data](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat) shows that screening and interviewing average eight to nine days each within the overall hiring process. A shared scoring sheet surfaces disagreement faster than conversation alone — and that disagreement is often where the most useful discussion about a candidate happens.

After the scores are in, set a threshold before you start screening — a minimum total score, or a minimum score on any one question that is non-negotiable for the role. Setting that threshold in advance keeps the decision from drifting toward whoever made the best impression on the call rather than whoever gave the strongest answers.

Candidates who advance are better matched to the role, and your team's time in later rounds goes toward the people most likely to succeed.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a screening interview?

A screening interview is a short early-round conversation, typically 20 to 30 minutes by phone, between a recruiter or hiring manager and a candidate before the full interview. SHRM describes it as a call designed to confirm eligibility and cover practical points — salary expectations, start date, and basic qualifications — so the team can decide which candidates to bring in for a longer conversation.

### How many questions should a screening interview include?

A 20-to-30-minute screening call has room for five to eight questions, depending on how much follow-up each one draws out. Start with logistical questions — compensation, availability — then move into motivational and role-specific questions. Keeping the list focused ensures the call stays within the time the candidate was told to expect.

### What is the difference between a screening interview and a full interview?

A screening interview confirms baseline fit quickly: requirements, compensation, availability, and motivation. A full interview goes deeper into skills, judgment, and culture fit, often over 45 to 90 minutes with multiple interviewers. The screening call determines who earns the full interview, which protects everyone's time.

### Should you ask behavioral questions in a screening interview?

Yes. Behavioral questions are well suited to screening calls because they reveal how a candidate actually worked in the past, not just how they describe themselves. SHRM notes that behavioral questions naturally open up follow-up opportunities, which is exactly what you need in a short call to get beyond surface-level answers.

### How do you score screening interview answers consistently?

Use a five-point anchored rubric in which each score level is defined by observable, behavior-based descriptions. Mark your score and a brief note immediately after each answer so your assessment reflects what the candidate said rather than a general impression formed at the end of the call. When multiple people run screens, compare scores before discussing impressions.

### What recruiter interview questions work best in a screening call?

For recruiter roles, the most revealing screening questions focus on sourcing, pipeline management, and candidate experience: how the candidate sources passive candidates, how they prioritize a high-volume pipeline, and how they handle a role where the requirements shift mid-search. Pair each with a behavioral follow-up — "Can you give me a specific example?" — to get past prepared answers.

### How do intern interview questions differ from other screening questions?

Intern screening questions weight motivation and learning orientation more heavily than work history, because the work history is thin. Focus on what drew the candidate to the field, how they approach unfamiliar problems, and what they hope to take away from the internship. The logistical questions — start date, schedule, compensation expectations — remain just as important as they are for any other role.
