# Screening Interview Questions: What to Ask Before the Full Interview

Ask the right questions in a screening call and your team avoids spending a full round of interviews on candidates who were never a real fit. When screening calls run on gut feel rather than a shared set of questions, the first round leaves you with impressions you cannot compare.

A screening interview is a short early-round conversation — typically 20 to 30 minutes — in which someone on the hiring team works through a focused list of questions to confirm basic fit before bringing a candidate in for the full interview. The questions cover practical requirements such as compensation and availability, motivational factors such as why the candidate is in the market, and one or two role-specific probes that a resume alone can't answer.

This article covers which questions to ask at the screening stage and what each one is designed to reveal, how to sequence the call, how to score answers consistently, and how to use the results to decide who moves forward.

## What makes a screening question work

Effective screening questions are brief enough for a candidate to answer in under a minute, tied directly to a competency or logistical requirement, capable of drawing out responses that can be scored consistently across all candidates, and free of phrasing that telegraphs the right answer before the candidate speaks — a question like "We value collaboration here. How do you work in a team?" has already told the candidate what to say.

Whether a question is open-ended or closed shapes how much a candidate reveals. Open-ended questions draw out details that yes-or-no checks cannot. A candidate who answers "Tell me about your most recent role's top three duties" has to describe their actual daily work, which reveals more than a job title does. Behavioral questions go further still, and Paul Falcone, CHRO at the Motion Picture & Television Fund, as quoted by SHRM, notes that they naturally open up follow-up opportunities. When a candidate says they dislike something, the interviewer can probe the last time that happened, the circumstances, and what came of it.

The follow-up is where the real signal comes from. Good follow-ups include "What did you personally own?", "Can you give me a specific example?", "What made that difficult?", and "What would you do differently now?" A candidate's first answer often describes what happened. The follow-up reveals how they think.

## How structured screening changes what you learn

A screening call without a consistent question set is easy to run and hard to compare. When every candidate gets different questions, you are not comparing answers — you are comparing impressions. SHRM defines a structured interview as one in which candidates are asked the same predetermined questions in the same order with all responses evaluated on the same rating scale. That consistency is what makes the screen useful as a decision tool.

The evidence for structured interviews is strong. According to [ERE](https://www.ere.net/why-structured-interviews-are-the-best-way-to-hire/), structured interviews predict job performance with 26% accuracy, on par with general cognitive ability tests. Research cited by [SHRM](https://vendordirectory.shrm.org/company/354019/whitepapers/11772/the-definitive-guide-to-structured-interviewing) shows that structured interviews are twice as predictive of job performance as unstructured ones, because they minimize subjectivity and focus on job-relevant criteria. That predictive advantage applies at the screening stage too. A first-round call structured around the same predetermined questions and rating scale produces candidate data you can actually compare.

That predictive advantage has a measurable effect on hiring outcomes. The [SHRM 2024 Talent Trends Report](https://www.shrm.org/content/dam/en/shrm/research/2024-talent-trends-research-overall-findings.pdf) found that 54% of organizations use pre-employment assessments to gauge applicants' knowledge, skills, and abilities during hiring — and 78% of those organizations say the assessments improved their quality of hire. A structured screening call, run consistently with a scored question set, is an assessment. Screening rigorously means the candidates who reach later interview rounds are better qualified.

## How to choose your questions and sequence the call

With the case for structure established, the practical question is which questions to put on your list and in what order. A 20-to-30-minute screen has room for five to eight questions, depending on how much follow-up each one draws out. Sequence matters. Start with logistical and motivational questions — compensation range, availability, and reason for leaving — then move into behavioral or role-specific questions once you have confirmed baseline fit. That order mirrors the natural arc of a conversation and gives candidates time to settle before the more demanding questions.

Choose questions based on what you most need to verify at this stage. For every role, that includes at least one logistical question (compensation, start date, location), one motivational question (why they are leaving, why this role), and one or two role-specific questions tied to requirements that are hard to assess from a resume alone. For roles with a large applicant pool, a knockout question on the application form, before the screening call, can filter out candidates who don't meet a non-negotiable requirement, so the call focuses only on those who already cleared that bar.

The role determines which specific questions belong in that last category. For an intern, motivational questions carry more weight because work history is thin; focus on what drew them to the field and how they approach new challenges. For a manager, add a question about how they build and develop a team. For a recruiter, ask about how they source candidates or handle a high-volume pipeline. The logistical and motivational questions stay constant across the board, while the role-specific ones shift with the position.

Once you have a draft list, check it against two common failure modes in question design. Double-barreled questions, such as "Tell me about your communication style and how you handle deadlines," ask two things at once and rarely get a complete answer to either. Questions that touch on protected characteristics, even indirectly, expose you to legal liability without telling you anything useful about the candidate's ability to do the job. Keep each question focused on one job-relevant competency.

## The scoring rubric to use with every question

Each behavioral question in a structured screen should be scored on the same five-point scale. The rubric below anchors each score to specific, observable behavior so two interviewers scoring the same candidate will land close to the same number. Use this alongside every behavioral question in the set that follows.

| Score | Behavioral description |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Candidate gave no specific example; response was entirely general or off-topic. |
| 2 | Candidate referenced a situation but provided no detail on actions taken. |
| 3 | Candidate described a situation and actions but did not address the outcome. |
| 4 | Candidate gave a clear situation, specific actions, and a measurable outcome. |
| 5 | Candidate gave a clear example with measurable outcome and reflected on what they learned or would do differently. |

For logistical questions, score on whether the candidate's answer meets the role's requirements and whether they give a concrete number or date rather than a vague one.

## Is the salary range for this position within your acceptable range?

This question clears the most basic logistical requirement before anyone spends more time on the process. A candidate who needs significantly more than the role offers will not accept an offer, no matter how well the rest of the interviews go. Asking early is a courtesy to both sides, and it focuses every subsequent conversation on candidates who are genuinely in range.

**What a strong answer looks like** — The candidate gives a specific range or confirms the posted range works for them. If they're at the edge of the range, they explain the factors affecting their flexibility (equity, benefits, or remote work) and show they've thought the trade-offs through. The answer states a number and addresses trade-offs without hedging.

**What a weak answer looks like** — The candidate is vague ("I'm open to anything"), unwilling to give a number, or states a figure well outside the posted range without acknowledging the gap. Vagueness here often signals that the candidate has not thought through whether the salary, the role, or both meet their actual requirements. Follow up: "We're budgeted in the range of X to Y. Does that work for where you are?"

| Score | What it looks like for this question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Candidate refuses to discuss salary or gives a non-answer. |
| 2 | Candidate acknowledges the question but provides no number or range. |
| 3 | Candidate gives a range that partially overlaps, with no explanation of flexibility. |
| 4 | Candidate confirms alignment or explains a specific, grounded reason for a slight gap. |
| 5 | Candidate confirms alignment and shows awareness of total compensation beyond base salary. |

## Why are you searching for a new position?

Motivation is difficult to assess at the screening stage, yet it shapes whether a candidate will commit to the role. This question surfaces why the candidate is in the market now, which is distinct from why they want this particular role, and gives you an early read on how they talk about their current or most recent employer.

**What a strong answer looks like** — The candidate is direct. They point to something concrete, such as a cap on growth, a shift in the company's direction, or a gap between their current role and where they want to go. The answer connects to what drew them toward this opportunity rather than only away from their last one, and they describe past employers without bitterness or blame.

**What a weak answer looks like** — The candidate is evasive or gives a generic answer like "I'm just exploring." A candidate who speaks negatively about a manager or team in a way that suggests the problem follows them from role to role is worth noting. Neither response disqualifies a candidate outright, but both warrant a follow-up: "Can you say more about what specifically prompted the search?"

| Score | What it looks like for this question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Candidate gives no reason or refuses to answer. |
| 2 | Candidate gives a vague answer with no connection to this role. |
| 3 | Candidate explains a reason for leaving but doesn't connect it to this opportunity. |
| 4 | Candidate explains why they're leaving and links it clearly to what this role offers. |
| 5 | Candidate gives a candid, specific reason for leaving, connects it to this role, and shows genuine knowledge of the company or position. |

## What are the top three duties in your current or most recent job?

Resumes describe titles and outcomes. This question surfaces daily work. The gap between the two shows whether the candidate's experience maps to what the role actually requires, and it gives you a basis for the more specific questions that follow. SHRM includes this question in its standard screening questions for exactly this reason.

**What a strong answer looks like** — The candidate describes their actual work in concrete terms: specific tasks, tools, volume, and scope. They connect their current duties to the requirements of the role you're hiring for, without you having to prompt the connection. The answer shows at a glance where the candidate's duties overlap with the role and where the full interview should probe further.

**What a weak answer looks like** — The candidate describes their title or their team's function rather than their own work. They use broad language like "I handled communications" or "I supported the team," phrases that tell you little about what they owned. If the answer stays thin after the initial question, follow up: "What did a typical week look like for you in that role?"

| Score | What it looks like for this question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Candidate describes the team or department rather than their own duties. |
| 2 | Candidate lists duties in general terms with no specificity about scope or tools. |
| 3 | Candidate describes specific duties but doesn't connect them to the role's requirements. |
| 4 | Candidate gives specific, concrete duties and draws a clear connection to the open role. |
| 5 | Candidate describes specific duties with measurable scope, connects them to this role, and identifies where there is overlap and where they would be learning something new. |

## What do you see as your strongest skills, and what are your key challenges?

This question does two things at once. The strengths half tells you whether the candidate's self-assessment lines up with the role's requirements. The challenges half tells you how self-aware they are and whether they're growing. Together, the two halves give you a fuller picture of the candidate than either would on its own.

**What a strong answer looks like** — For strengths, strong candidates tie their answers directly to the duties of the open role. They point to something specific (a technical skill, a working style, a track record) and explain how it applies to this position. For challenges, the more self-aware candidates describe specific steps they are taking to improve, rather than repackaging a strength as a weakness.

**What a weak answer looks like** — A candidate who describes strengths with no connection to the role, or who struggles to identify a genuine challenge, is likely giving you a rehearsed answer rather than an honest one. Watch for deflection: "I work too hard" or "I care too much" tells you nothing about how the person will perform in the role.

| Score | What it looks like for this question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Candidate gives generic strengths with no connection to the role; cannot identify a challenge. |
| 2 | Candidate names strengths and a challenge but neither connects to the job or to growth. |
| 3 | Candidate links strengths to the role but gives a deflecting or evasive challenge. |
| 4 | Candidate links strengths directly to the role's requirements and identifies a real challenge with awareness of its impact. |
| 5 | Candidate gives specific, role-relevant strengths and describes a genuine challenge along with concrete steps they are taking to address it. |

## What can you tell me about our company and industry?

Candidates who want this role, specifically, will have done their homework. This question measures that. The answer tells you how deliberately the candidate pursued this application and how thoroughly they research before committing to something new.

**What a strong answer looks like** — The candidate references something specific: a recent product launch, a press article, a shift in the competitive landscape, or something from the job description that they connected to their own background. They show they understand what the company does and why the role exists within that context.

**What a weak answer looks like** — The candidate describes the company in the same words as the homepage, or admits they haven't had time to look into it. The former suggests surface-level preparation; the latter is worth noting as a signal about how seriously the candidate is pursuing this opportunity. A quick follow-up ("What about the role caught your attention?") often surfaces useful information even when the company knowledge is thin.

| Score | What it looks like for this question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Candidate admits to no research or gives an entirely incorrect description. |
| 2 | Candidate restates the company's homepage description with no additional knowledge. |
| 3 | Candidate shows familiarity with the company but cannot connect it to the role or the industry context. |
| 4 | Candidate demonstrates research beyond the homepage and connects the company's position to why they applied. |
| 5 | Candidate references specific, recent information about the company or industry and ties it directly to what they would bring to the role. |

## Tell me about a recent piece of work you're particularly proud of. What did you personally contribute?

Candidates naturally describe team achievements in the first person plural. This question cuts through that by asking what they owned. The answer reveals decision-making, initiative, and the impact they can point to as their own, information a resume's "we" language leaves out.

**What a strong answer looks like** — The candidate describes a specific project or deliverable, explains the decisions they made, and separates their contribution from the team's. The best answers include a measurable result (revenue generated, time saved, error rate reduced) and reflect on what made their contribution effective. A strong answer makes it clear what this person delivers independently.

**What a weak answer looks like** — The candidate describes a team achievement and cannot distinguish their role from the group's. Their language stays in the first person plural and never arrives at "I." If the follow-up "What specifically did you own?" doesn't sharpen the answer, the candidate has not demonstrated an individual contribution worth advancing. That is reason to pause before scheduling the full interview.

| Score | What it looks like for this question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Candidate describes a team achievement with no individual contribution identified. |
| 2 | Candidate references a personal contribution but cannot describe what they specifically did or decided. |
| 3 | Candidate describes their contribution clearly but cannot point to a result. |
| 4 | Candidate describes specific actions they owned and the measurable outcome that followed. |
| 5 | Candidate gives a clear account of what they owned, the result it produced, and what they would do differently next time. |

## Which part of this role would be most familiar to you, and which would be the biggest stretch?

This question does something most screening questions don't: it invites the candidate to identify their own gaps. The answer tells you how they will approach the parts of the job that are new to them, and giving an honest one requires self-awareness. A candidate who can describe a genuine stretch and explain how they plan to close it is showing self-awareness that a resume cannot convey.

**What a strong answer looks like** — The candidate ties the familiar parts to concrete past experience and describes the stretch honestly without catastrophizing or dismissing it. The best answers spell out how the candidate would approach the stretch, including what they would read, whom they would talk to, and how they would get up to speed. A candidate who explains their plan for closing the gap has clearly read the job description and thought seriously about doing the work.

**What a weak answer looks like** — The candidate says everything looks familiar, or describes the stretch in terms so broad that it's clear they haven't read the job description closely. A candidate who fails to identify a genuine stretch is overconfident or disengaged from what the role requires. Either way, it's information worth having before you schedule the full interview.

| Score | What it looks like for this question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Candidate says everything is familiar or cannot identify a stretch. |
| 2 | Candidate identifies a stretch but describes it so vaguely that it's unclear they read the job description. |
| 3 | Candidate identifies familiar and stretch areas specifically but doesn't address how they'd close the gap. |
| 4 | Candidate gives specific examples on both sides and describes a plan or approach for the stretch. |
| 5 | Candidate gives specific examples on both sides, describes a concrete approach to the stretch, and frames it as a reason they're excited about the role. |

## Do you have any questions for me?

The questions a candidate asks at the end of a screening call can tell you as much as the questions you asked them. Thoughtful questions show that the candidate has read the role carefully and thought about what doing it well would require. They also show what matters to this person as they weigh their own decision, because screening calls run in both directions.

**What a strong answer looks like** — The candidate asks something specific: about the team's current priorities, the success criteria for the role in the first 90 days, the challenges the previous person in the role faced, or how decisions get made on the team. The best questions show that the candidate is weighing whether the role is right for them, not simply trying to get it. That's the kind of deliberateness you want to see before you bring someone in for a full interview.

**What a weak answer looks like** — The candidate says they have no questions, or asks only about vacation policy and benefits. That alone does not disqualify the candidate, but a candidate who is genuinely interested in the role tends to have specific things they want to understand about the work, the team, or the company's direction.

| Score | What it looks like for this question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Candidate has no questions. |
| 2 | Candidate asks only about benefits, vacation, or other administrative topics. |
| 3 | Candidate asks about the role but the question could apply to any company or position. |
| 4 | Candidate asks a specific question about the team, the role's success criteria, or the company's direction. |
| 5 | Candidate asks two or more thoughtful, specific questions that show they've engaged seriously with the job description and the company. |

## How to run the call and use the rubric

Send candidates the topic areas, if not the exact questions, before the call. The goal is signal about the candidate, not the ability to answer under surprise conditions. Letting candidates prepare means their responses reflect actual experience rather than on-the-spot recall.

Sharing the topic areas in advance changes what you get in the call itself, so run the call with your question list in front of you and your scoring sheet beside it. Mark your score and a brief note after each answer — a phrase or two capturing the specific detail the candidate offered. That note is what you'll need when you compare candidates across a full week of screens or explain a decision to the rest of the hiring team. If a question produced a thin answer, a score of 1 or 2 is itself information worth recording.

When two or more team members run separate screens, compare scores before discussing impressions. A shared scoring sheet surfaces disagreement faster than conversation alone, and that disagreement is often where the most useful discussion about a candidate happens.

Score comparison works best when the threshold for advancement is fixed in advance. It can be a minimum total score, a minimum score on any single question, or both — whatever is non-negotiable for the role. Setting that threshold in advance keeps the decision from drifting toward whoever made the best impression on the call rather than whoever gave the strongest answers.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a screening interview?

A screening interview is a short early-round conversation, typically 20 to 30 minutes by phone, between a recruiter or hiring manager and a candidate before the full interview. SHRM describes it as a call designed to confirm eligibility and cover practical points — salary expectations, start date, and basic qualifications — so the team can decide which candidates to bring in for a longer conversation.

### How many questions should a screening interview include?

A 20-to-30-minute screening call has room for five to eight questions, depending on how much follow-up each one draws out. Start with logistical questions — compensation, availability — then move into motivational and role-specific questions. Keeping the list focused ensures the call stays within the time the candidate was told to expect.

### What is the difference between a screening interview and a full interview?

A screening interview confirms baseline fit quickly: requirements, compensation, availability, and motivation. A full interview goes deeper into skills, judgment, and culture fit, often over 45 to 90 minutes with multiple interviewers. The screening call determines who earns the full interview, which protects everyone's time.

### Should you ask behavioral questions in a screening interview?

Yes. Behavioral questions are well suited to screening calls because they reveal how a candidate actually worked in the past, not just how they describe themselves. SHRM notes that behavioral questions naturally open up follow-up opportunities, which is exactly what you need in a short call to get beyond surface-level answers.

### How do you score screening interview answers consistently?

Use a five-point anchored rubric in which each score level is defined by observable, behavior-based descriptions. Mark your score and a brief note immediately after each answer so your assessment reflects what the candidate said rather than a general impression formed at the end of the call. When multiple people run screens, compare scores before discussing impressions.

### What recruiter interview questions work best in a screening call?

For recruiter roles, the most revealing screening questions focus on sourcing, pipeline management, and candidate experience: how the candidate sources passive candidates, how they prioritize a high-volume pipeline, and how they handle a role where the requirements shift mid-search. Pair each with a behavioral follow-up — "Can you give me a specific example?" — to get past prepared answers.

### How do intern interview questions differ from other screening questions?

Intern screening questions weight motivation and learning orientation more heavily than work history, because the work history is thin. Focus on what drew the candidate to the field, how they approach unfamiliar problems, and what they hope to take away from the internship. The logistical questions — start date, schedule, compensation expectations — remain just as important as they are for any other role.
