Every ATS pricing guide you'll find online is written by someone who won't tell you their own price. Review sites collect affiliate fees. Vendor-submitted data is what analyst firms aggregate and publish. The vendors themselves hide behind a "contact sales" button. If you're trying to figure out what an applicant tracking system will actually cost your company before you sit through a demo, you're working with estimates dressed up as facts.

An applicant tracking system is software that collects job applications in one place and manages candidates through each stage of your hiring process, from application to hire. Your team uses it from a single platform to publish jobs, review candidates, collaborate on decisions, and communicate with applicants — instead of tracking applicants across inboxes and spreadsheets. What you pay depends on the vendor, the tier, and your headcount.

This guide covers the four pricing models and what each costs in practice, real prices from every major vendor, the hidden fees that change the actual number, and what to do before you sign anything.

## Why ATS Pricing Is So Hard to Find

Vendors who don't publish pricing aren't hiding a bargain. The sales call that follows a "contact us" button gives the vendor a chance to establish your budget before providing a number. That dynamic — which is deliberate — is why Greenhouse pricing, Lever pricing, and several other enterprise platforms require a quote request even for a ballpark figure. The sales team learns what you're willing to pay before they tell you what it costs.

Transparent pricing removes that dynamic entirely. When a vendor publishes its rates, you can compare it to competitors before spending an hour on a demo. You know immediately whether the platform is in range, and you arrive at the conversation having already done the math. That's the standard this article holds every vendor to — and the reason the ranges below are as specific as the available information allows.

## The Four Pricing Models You'll Encounter

ATS vendors use four main pricing structures. Each one suits a different kind of team, and each one hides costs in different places. Understanding which model a vendor uses before you request a quote tells you which questions to ask.

### Per-seat (per-user) pricing

Each named login carries a monthly or annual fee. Standard tiers typically run $15–$100 per user per month; enterprise tiers can run $300 or more. Per-seat pricing works well for small, stable recruiting teams where the number of people making hiring decisions doesn't change much. Watch for seat minimums — many vendors require three to five seats as a floor, which sets your starting cost well above the advertised per-seat rate even if only one person needs regular access. A platform advertising $35 per user might actually start at $175 per month because of a five-seat minimum.

### Flat-rate pricing

A fixed monthly fee, usually capped by the number of active job openings rather than users. This model suits teams that want predictable costs and involve several people in hiring without paying for each one. The practical limit is the active-job cap: if your company opens eight roles at once and your plan allows five, you'll need to upgrade or close a posting prematurely. Teams with stable, low-volume hiring rarely hit the cap. Teams in an active growth phase find themselves upgrading mid-year to cover a temporary surge, then sitting on excess capacity for the rest of the billing period.

### Per-job (usage-based) pricing

A fee per active posting or open requisition. Best for companies that hire occasionally — say, two or three roles a year — because costs scale roughly with what you use. The limitation appears during hiring surges: a growth company opening ten roles in a single quarter can quickly find usage-based pricing more expensive than a flat rate. If your hiring volume is uneven from quarter to quarter, model both scenarios before choosing this structure.

### Per-employee (PEPM) pricing

Price is based on total company headcount on payroll, not on the number of recruiters or open roles. This model is common in HR suites that include an ATS as one module among several. A typical range runs $8–$25 per employee per month. A 50-person company paying $15 per employee pays $750 a month — before any add-ons — and that cost climbs automatically as the company hires. PEPM pricing ties your ATS cost to growth rather than to hiring activity, which can feel counterintuitive: the more successful your recruiting, the more the tool costs to run.

## What These Platforms Actually Charge

The table above gives starting prices. What follows is the fuller picture for each platform — what the tiers look like, where pricing becomes variable, and what the platform is built for. That context matters because the starting price rarely reflects what a team of your size will actually pay.

### JazzHR pricing

JazzHR publishes three flat-rate plans on its website. The Hero plan starts at $75/month and allows three active jobs. The Plus plan runs $269/month and removes the active-job limit entirely. The Pro plan runs $420/month and adds reporting, analytics, and additional automation features. All plans are billed annually; monthly billing carries a higher rate on each tier. JazzHR targets small businesses specifically and has been in this segment long enough to have a large library of integrations and a well-documented feature set. The active-job cap on the entry plan is the most common friction point for teams that move quickly between roles.

JazzHR pricing is worth evaluating if your team hires steadily at low volume and needs a tool that covers the standard workflow without a large implementation effort. The gap between the $75/month Hero plan and the $269/month Plus plan is significant, so it's worth estimating how many active jobs you'll carry at any given time before committing to the lower tier.

### Breezy HR pricing

Breezy HR publishes four plans. The Bootstrap plan is free but limited to one active position. The Startup plan runs $157/month (billed annually) and removes the active-job and position limits, making it the effective entry point for teams with more than one open role. The Growth plan runs $273/month and adds interview guides, advanced reporting, and candidate nurturing features. The Business plan runs $439/month and includes priority support and additional automation capabilities. All paid plans include unlimited users, which is an advantage for teams where multiple people review candidates without having clear recruiter roles.

Breezy HR's pricing structure is cleaner than most once you're past the free tier, because the upgrade path is based on features rather than job count. The Startup plan covers the core workflow well for teams hiring across several roles at once. The main variable is whether the features on the Growth and Business plans justify their premiums for your team's specific process — the jump from $157 to $273 is worth auditing against what you'll actually use.

### Recruitee pricing

Recruitee publishes two plans, both billed annually. The Start plan runs $199/month and allows ten active jobs with unlimited users. The Grow plan runs $329/month and removes the active-job limit. Recruitee is built around structured hiring workflows — pipeline customization, scorecard-driven reviews, and team-based decision-making — and prices toward teams that want those features built in rather than bolted on. The unlimited-user structure on both plans reflects that orientation: Recruitee expects multiple people to be active in the platform on any given role.

Recruitee pricing is competitive at the Grow tier for teams running five or more roles at once, where the per-job cost implied by the flat rate becomes favorable. The Start plan's ten-job cap works well for teams with a measured pace of hiring. The annual billing requirement means you're committing to a full year on first purchase, which makes the trial period — available before you pay — worth using thoroughly.

### Workable pricing

Workable pricing is partially published. The company lists a Starter plan at around $149/month for occasional, low-volume hiring. The Standard plan — the most commonly quoted entry point for teams with active pipelines — runs around $299/month. The Premier plan requires contacting sales. Workable's pricing combines a base platform fee with per-seat components and per-job charges on certain tiers, which makes the actual monthly cost variable depending on team size and the number of open roles at any given time. This combination is a common source of unexpected costs when teams move past the Starter tier and add users or roles.

Workable has a broad feature set and a long track record in the mid-market. Its partial pricing transparency means you can get a reasonable estimate from the published rates, but you'll need a direct conversation to understand what happens to the number when your team grows or your hiring volume spikes. If Workable is on your shortlist, ask the sales team to model your expected annual cost across two or three hiring scenarios before the first contract.

### Lever pricing

Lever does not publish pricing. All quotes require a sales call. Commonly reported estimates for Lever's LeverTRM platform start around $3,500 per year for small teams, with mid-market packages commonly reported in the $15,000–$25,000 per year range. Lever is built around talent relationship management as much as applicant tracking — it maintains a candidate relationship database alongside the pipeline, which means it functions as both an ATS and a recruiting CRM. That broader scope justifies a higher price point for teams that use both functions, but it also means smaller teams often pay for depth they won't reach.

Lever's implementation process is more involved than SMB-focused tools, and onboarding typically requires dedicated time from both the customer's team and Lever's implementation staff. Teams evaluating Lever should factor that time into the total cost of the first year. The [Lever alternatives guide](/blog/lever-alternatives) breaks down platforms that cover similar ground at lower price points for teams that don't need the full CRM layer.

### Greenhouse pricing

Greenhouse does not publish pricing. The company requires a sales call for all tiers, and the quote process typically involves a detailed discovery conversation about team size, role volume, and integration requirements before any number is offered. Commonly reported estimates for Greenhouse start around $6,000–$9,000 per year for small teams and climb steeply with seat count and feature tier. Mid-market contracts are frequently reported in the $20,000–$50,000 per year range. Greenhouse is purpose-built for structured hiring at scale — its scorecards, structured interview kits, and reporting infrastructure are most valuable when a dedicated recruiting team runs dozens of simultaneous pipelines across departments.

Greenhouse pricing reflects that scope. The platform's implementation process is substantive, and the configuration required to get the most out of it takes weeks for most teams. For a company with a recruiting department running high-volume hiring, that investment pays off. For a 25-person startup opening three roles, the same platform is months of overhead before it delivers its value. ## The Hidden Costs That Change the Actual Number

The subscription fee is only part of what an ATS costs. Several other charges regularly appear between the sales call and the first annual renewal, and understanding them before you sign separates an informed purchase from a year of unpleasant surprises.

### Implementation and onboarding fees

Enterprise platforms — Greenhouse and Lever among them — commonly charge implementation fees separate from the annual subscription. These fees cover system configuration, data migration from a previous ATS or spreadsheet setup, and guided onboarding sessions. The range is wide: straightforward deployments may fall in the low thousands, while complex implementations involving large historical data sets or custom integrations can reach well above $50,000. For SMB-focused platforms, implementation is usually self-serve and free, but that also means your team absorbs the configuration time internally — which has a real cost even if it doesn't appear on an invoice.

### Integration costs

Most platforms connect to background check providers, HRIS systems, payroll tools, and job boards through integrations. Some are included in the base subscription; many are not. Background checks are charged per use on virtually every platform. HRIS integrations on enterprise tools often require an API tier upgrade. Before selecting a platform, list the external tools your hiring workflow depends on and ask each vendor explicitly whether the integration is included, available as an add-on with a stated price, or requires a custom API arrangement. That list will often surface costs that don't appear in the headline price at all.

### Billing cadence and annual commitment

Annual billing on an ATS typically saves 15–20% compared to paying month-to-month, according to [Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/resources/applicant-tracking-system-pricing-2026/). That discount is real, but it comes with a 12-month commitment before you've spent significant time in the platform. For a first-time ATS purchase, a monthly plan — even at a higher per-month rate — gives you the flexibility to switch if the tool doesn't fit the way your team hires. Once you've run several hiring cycles and confirmed the platform works for your process, moving to annual billing is a straightforward way to reduce costs.

### Overage charges

Flat-rate plans with active-job caps generate overages when you exceed the limit. Per-seat plans generate additional charges when users are added mid-contract. Per-employee plans grow automatically as the company hires. AI screening credits, SMS message volumes, and premium support tiers are other common sources of charges that don't appear in the headline price. The right question to ask every vendor before signing is specific: under what conditions will my monthly or annual cost increase beyond the stated plan price, and by how much?

## How to Calculate What an ATS Will Actually Cost You

The monthly rate on a pricing page is a starting point, not a final number. Here is how to arrive at a realistic annual cost for any platform you're seriously evaluating.

1. **Start with the base subscription.** Take the monthly rate that matches your expected active jobs and team size, then multiply by 12 — or by the discounted annual rate if the vendor offers one.
2. **Add implementation costs.** For enterprise platforms, ask for this number directly. For SMB tools, estimate the internal hours your team will spend on configuration and training, then apply an hourly cost to get a comparable figure.
3. **Add integration costs.** List the tools you need to connect and confirm whether each is included or billed separately. Get a per-year number for each paid integration.
4. **Estimate overages.** If you're on a plan with active-job caps, estimate the months you'll exceed the limit and multiply by the per-job overage rate. Apply the same calculation to seat overages if you're on a per-seat plan.
5. **Divide by annual hires.** Take the total annual cost from steps one through four and divide by the number of people you expect to hire this year. The result is your ATS cost per hire.

That final figure puts the subscription in context. [SHRM's 2025 benchmarking survey](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat) puts the average cost per hire for nonexecutive roles at $5,475. A $3,000-per-year ATS subscription, divided across 20 hires, adds $150 to your cost per hire — a rounding error against that baseline. The question isn't whether you can afford an ATS. The question is whether the platform you're evaluating fits what your team actually does, and whether the total annual number is proportionate to your hiring volume.

## Matching the Pricing Model to Your Hiring Volume

The right pricing model depends heavily on how often you hire and how many people are involved in each decision. Those two variables narrow the field more than any feature comparison.

If your company hires one to five people a year, usage-based or low-tier flat-rate pricing is almost always the right answer. The overhead of a per-seat enterprise platform isn't justified by that volume. A flat-rate plan in the $75–$157/month range covers the workflow you need without paying for capacity you won't reach. The goal at that hiring volume is a tool that keeps applications organized and keeps the team aligned — not a system built for dozens of concurrent pipelines.

If your company hires 10 to 30 people a year and involves a cross-functional team in reviews, flat-rate pricing with unlimited users is usually the most cost-effective model. Per-seat pricing penalizes you for including hiring managers and department heads in the process — which is exactly the kind of involvement that leads to better decisions. Unlimited-user plans let you bring engineers, executives, and outside advisors into the pipeline without watching a seat count accumulate charges. The structure fits the way small, deliberate hiring teams actually work.

If your company hires 30 or more people a year, runs a dedicated recruiting function, and needs deep integrations with an HRIS and performance management tools, an enterprise platform may justify its cost. Greenhouse, for example, provides structured interview kits, scorecard-driven evaluation, and reporting infrastructure that serves a recruiting operation running dozens of simultaneous pipelines. That depth requires configuration time and a higher budget — but for a company at that stage, the investment returns in process consistency and hiring quality. The same platform for a 20-person startup opening three roles is months of overhead before it delivers its full value.

## Trade-offs to Know Before You Decide

Every pricing model involves a trade-off, and the right choice depends on where your team is now — not where it expects to be in three years. Building that into the decision upfront saves a mid-contract renegotiation.

Per-seat pricing gives you cost control if the recruiting team is small and the headcount making hiring decisions is stable. It becomes a liability the moment you want to involve more people in reviews, because adding a hiring manager or an executive interviewer means adding a seat. Teams that value broad collaboration tend to find per-seat models quietly expensive over time — especially when every person who touches one candidate represents another line item.

Flat-rate pricing with an active-job cap is predictable until you hit the cap. For teams with steady, low-volume hiring, that rarely happens. Teams in a growth phase — opening five roles in one quarter and two in the next — tend to upgrade mid-year to cover a temporary surge, then sit on excess capacity through a slower period. If your hiring volume is lumpy, model the worst-case quarter before choosing a plan based on average months.

Enterprise platforms — Greenhouse and Lever in particular — come with implementation depth and integration breadth that smaller tools don't match. That depth is genuinely valuable for a company with a dedicated recruiting department running structured, high-volume hiring. For a startup hiring its first ten employees, the same depth is overhead that takes weeks to configure and months to fully reach. [SHRM's 2026 benchmarking data](https://www.shrm.org/mena/topics-tools/research/recruiting-benchmarking/full-data-brief) puts the median executive cost per hire at $15,000 — a figure that makes a $20,000 annual enterprise ATS contract look proportionate for executive-level recruiting, and less so for filling three individual contributor roles.

Annual contracts reduce cost but increase commitment. Hiretrace's guidance for first-time buyers is to start with a monthly plan, run several hiring cycles, learn what your team actually needs from the tool, and then move to annual billing once you're confident in the fit. The 15–20% discount is real, according to Capterra, but it's not worth locking in a tool you haven't fully evaluated.

## What to Do Before You Sign

Narrowing the field on price is straightforward once you know your pricing model preference and hiring volume. Getting from a shortlist to a signed contract without surprises takes a few more steps.

Ask every vendor for an annual total based on your actual team size and expected hiring volume — not the advertised monthly rate. That all-in annual number is the figure to compare across vendors. A platform that looks $100/month cheaper than its competitor may cost more over twelve months once seats, integrations, and overages are included. Pitchnhire's buying guidance makes the same point: compare vendors on annual totals built from your actual recruiter count and hiring volume, not on the headline rates that led you to the demo.

Request a sample contract before the demo ends. The contract is where caps, overage rates, and auto-renewal terms appear. If a vendor declines to share contract language before you commit, treat that as a signal about how the relationship will go after you sign. The vendors most confident in their pricing are the ones most willing to show it in writing.

Run a trial on the actual workflow your team uses, not a demo designed by the vendor. Invite the people who will use the tool — the hiring manager, the interviewers, the person who reviews applications — and have them work through a real or realistic role. The platforms that look most impressive in a live demo are not always the ones that fit naturally into the way a small team hires. A tool that takes three clicks to move a candidate and two tabs to leave feedback costs more in daily time than the subscription fee suggests.

If you're evaluating an ATS for the first time, start with a tool that publishes its prices, offers a free trial, and doesn't require an annual contract on the first purchase. The goal is to get your process into a structured system, learn what your team needs from that system, and then decide whether a more feature-rich platform is worth the investment. For most growing teams, a mid-tier ATS used consistently and well delivers more value than a premium platform that never gets fully configured.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the average cost of an ATS?

ATS pricing ranges from free (limited free tiers) to over $50,000 per year for enterprise deployments. For growing teams and small businesses, monthly fees typically fall between $75 and $500, depending on active-job limits, seat structure, and included features. The right figure depends on hiring volume, team size, and which pricing model the vendor uses.

### Do ATS vendors charge per user or per job?

Vendors use different models: per-seat (per named user), flat-rate by active job count, per-job posting, or per employee on payroll. Some platforms, like Workable, combine per-seat and per-job elements in a hybrid model. Knowing which model a vendor uses before you request a quote tells you which costs to watch for and which questions to ask.

### Why don't some ATS vendors publish their pricing?

Vendors that require a sales call before quoting a price can learn your budget before giving you a number, then price to it. This is a deliberate sales approach rather than a reflection of pricing complexity. Transparent pricing allows you to compare vendors before a demo and arrive at any conversation having already done the math.

### What hidden costs should I watch for when buying an ATS?

The most common hidden costs are implementation and onboarding fees (significant for enterprise platforms), paid integrations with background check providers or HRIS systems, overage charges when you exceed active-job or seat limits, and the cost difference between monthly and annual billing. Ask every vendor for an all-in annual total based on your actual team size and hiring volume before signing.

### Is annual or monthly billing better for an ATS?

Annual billing typically saves 15–20% compared to monthly billing, according to Capterra. That said, for a first-time ATS purchase, monthly billing gives you the flexibility to switch if the tool doesn't fit your process. Once you've run several hiring cycles and confirmed the platform works, moving to annual billing is a straightforward way to reduce costs without changing anything else.

### How do I calculate the true cost of an ATS?

Start with the base annual subscription, add implementation costs (even internal configuration time), add integration fees, and estimate any overages based on your expected job volume and team size. Divide that total by your expected annual hires to get a cost per hire — the figure that makes the subscription meaningful in context rather than an abstract monthly number.

### What ATS pricing model is best for a startup or small business?

Flat-rate pricing with unlimited users is usually the best fit for startups and small businesses. It gives you predictable monthly costs, lets you involve the whole team in hiring without per-seat charges, and scales by active jobs rather than headcount. Per-seat and per-employee models tend to become expensive as you grow, while usage-based pricing can spike during active hiring periods.
