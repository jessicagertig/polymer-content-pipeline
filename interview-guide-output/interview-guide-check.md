<p>A hiring process typically moves a candidate from application to shortlist to interview rounds to decision. ## Phone and video screenings

### As written

<h2>Phone and video screenings</h2>

<p>A phone screening is a brief call — sometimes as short as ten minutes — in which an employer verifies basic qualifications, clarifies outstanding questions from the application, or confirms the candidate's continued interest, with the goal of building a shortlist before anyone invests more time. It is not a full interview, and it should not be run like one. Its job is to filter efficiently so that the in-depth conversations that follow involve only candidates who genuinely fit the role.</p>

<p>Good screening questions cover whether the candidate's experience matches what the role requires, whether their availability and salary expectations are compatible with the position, and whether their interest in the work is genuine. When answers are vague at the screening stage, brief follow-up questions — "Can you say more about how you used that?" or "What drew you to that kind of work?" — can tell you quickly whether the application reflects real experience or surface-level match.</p>

<p>A live video interview follows the same dynamics as an in-person conversation, with the added logistical advantage of not requiring either party to travel. One-way video interviews, in which candidates record answers to pre-recorded questions on their own schedule, are increasingly common at the screening stage because they let hiring teams review responses outside of real-time constraints. They also create a record every evaluator can review independently, which helps with consistency. Whatever the format, share what candidates can expect beforehand — who they're speaking with, how long the call will take, and what the next steps are.</p>

## Structured versus unstructured interviewing

### As written

<h2>Structured versus unstructured interviewing</h2>

<p>The difference between structured and unstructured interviewing matters for every format that follows. A structured interview standardizes the questions and evaluation: each candidate for the same role receives the same questions in the same order and is scored against the same criteria. An unstructured interview is a relatively open conversation with each candidate, aimed at getting a general sense of the person. Most teams land somewhere between the two — a rough list of questions, variable follow-ups, no formal scoring — which produces some of the drawbacks of each.</p>

<p>ERE, citing research from Google's re:Work initiative, reports that <a href="https://www.ere.net/why-structured-interviews-are-the-best-way-to-hire/">structured interviews predict job performance with 26% accuracy</a>, matching general cognitive ability tests as the second-strongest available predictor. Work sample tests come in just above that, at 29%. Gut-feel, unstructured interviewing falls well below both. And among organizations with the best-rated candidate experiences, <a href="https://www.ere.net/articles/5-research-backed-ways-to-improve-your-interviews/">seven of the top ten conduct structured interviews</a>. The discipline of standardizing questions and scoring is part of what makes those experiences good — not just for the employer's data quality, but for candidates who get a process that feels fair and prepared.</p>

<h2>Behavioral interviews</h2>

<p>Behavioral interviews focus on how candidates have acted in real situations in their previous jobs. The underlying principle is that past behavior can predict future behavior: how someone handled a conflict with a client two years ago is more informative than how they say they would handle one today. Questions begin with prompts like "Tell me about a time when…" or "Give me an example of a situation where you…."</p>

<p>The STAR method — Situation, Task, Action, Result — gives candidates a structure for answering these questions and gives interviewers a consistent framework for evaluating responses. When an answer stays at the level of "we" rather than "I," or describes what the team did without naming what the candidate specifically contributed, a follow-up probe brings it back to the individual: "What specifically did you do in that situation?" or "What would you handle differently today?" These are among the most useful questions in any interview, because they test whether the candidate can reflect on their own performance honestly.</p>

<p>Behavioral interviews are most effective when the competencies being assessed are defined before the first candidate arrives. For a structured set of questions organized by competency — problem solving, judgment, prioritization, ownership, leadership, and adaptability — see our article on <a href="/blog/unique-interview-questions">unique interview questions that reveal how candidates think</a>.</p>

<h2>Situational interviews</h2>

<p>Situational questions present candidates with a hypothetical scenario — often one they'd face in the new role — and ask how they'd respond. Starters include "What would you do if…" and "How would you handle…." They test reasoning and judgment in the moment, which makes them useful when the candidate has limited directly applicable experience. A recent graduate interviewing for a management role, for example, may not have fired anyone yet, but can be asked how they would approach a performance issue on their team.</p>

<p>The trade-off with situational questions is that the answers describe intent, not action. A candidate can describe a thoughtful, well-structured response without having done anything similar. That doesn't make the information worthless — it reveals how someone thinks through a problem — but it shouldn't carry the weight that behavioral evidence from real experience does. Combining situational and behavioral questions in the same interview covers both what a candidate thinks and what they've actually done, giving you a more complete picture.</p>

<h2>Technical interviews</h2>

<p>Technical interviews ask candidates to solve problems live, complete coding challenges, or walk through case analyses using tools and frameworks specific to the job. They are most common in engineering, data, and finance roles, and they tend to be the longest individual interview format, running anywhere from one to three hours.</p>

<p>The key to a technical interview that produces useful signal is building rubrics around specific, measurable competencies — problem decomposition, code quality, communication, testing awareness, and trade-off reasoning — rather than general impressions. Questions should be open-ended and have more than one valid approach, so the interviewer can assess reasoning rather than whether the candidate memorized a particular answer. The best technical interview questions reveal how a candidate thinks about edge cases, not just whether they arrived at a correct solution.</p>

<p>Level questions to seniority. Junior candidates get mechanics questions focused on fundamentals. Senior candidates get trade-off and architectural judgment questions, where the right answer depends on context and there is no single correct response. After a take-home assessment or coding exercise, hold a debrief conversation. The debrief often surfaces more than the written work alone — it reveals how clearly candidates can explain their decisions, whether they considered alternatives, and how they respond to questions about their own work. Ask them to explain a technical concept as if speaking to someone outside their field. The ability to calibrate language for the audience is a signal about communication skills that the code itself cannot give you.</p>

<p>As with behavioral interviews, consistency is what makes comparison possible. Asking every candidate for the same role the same core questions, in the same order, with the same time budget, is the only way to compare two candidates fairly.</p>

## Designing interview questions that produce useful answers

### As written

<h2>Designing interview questions that produce useful answers</h2>

<p>The questions you ask determine what you learn. Generic questions produce generic answers. Questions that begin with "Tell me about yourself" or "What is your greatest weakness?" are so widely rehearsed that the answers reveal little about how a candidate actually works. The question design stage is where the interview either becomes a reliable evaluation tool or remains an exercise in confirmation of first impressions.</p>

<p>Effective interview questions share a few qualities. They are rooted in real experience rather than hypotheticals alone. They have more than one valid answer, so the interviewer can assess reasoning rather than recall. They connect directly to what the role requires. If a customer-facing role demands patience under pressure, the question should probe for a real situation in which the candidate demonstrated that — not for a general statement about their approach to difficult customers. And they are phrased simply enough that the candidate can answer the question asked rather than the question they expected to be asked.</p>

<p>Question design also means knowing what not to ask. Any question that touches on arrest record, military status, pregnancy, age, religion, marital status, or other protected characteristics has no place in an interview. These questions expose the organization to legal risk. More directly, they leave candidates uncertain about whether a casual exchange about their personal life shaped a decision that was supposed to be about their qualifications.</p>

## Scoring what you hear

### As written

<h2>Scoring what you hear</h2>

<p>An interview without a scoring method produces opinions, not evidence. When each interviewer walks away with a different impression and no common framework for comparing notes, the debrief becomes a negotiation between strong personalities rather than a review of what candidates demonstrated. The person who speaks most confidently tends to carry the most weight, regardless of whether their assessment is the most accurate.</p>

<p>A <a href="/blog/behavioral-interview-scoring-matrix">behavioral interview scoring matrix</a> gives each interviewer a grid that lists the competencies the role requires and describes — in observable behavioral terms — what a weak, adequate, and strong answer looks like for each. Behaviorally anchored rating scales (BARS) go further, specifying what each score looks like in plain, specific behavior so that two interviewers rating the same answer have a much better chance of arriving at the same score. A useful scorecard includes space for direct quotes and specific examples, not just numerical scores, so the debrief has something concrete to discuss rather than just competing ratings.</p>

## Interview bias

### As written

<h2>Interview bias</h2>

<p>Interview bias is any preference or mental shortcut that shapes how an interviewer judges a candidate on factors unrelated to their ability to do the job. Most of these judgments happen quickly and feel like sound instinct to the person making them — which is exactly what makes bias difficult to address. It rarely feels like bias from the inside.</p>

<p>The types that appear most often in hiring include first impression bias, the halo and horns effect, affinity bias, confirmation bias, the contrast effect, recency and primacy bias, stereotype bias, and groupthink. <a href="/blog/first-impression-bias">First impression bias</a> causes people to form a quick and incomplete impression of someone based on what they see and hear in the first few seconds of meeting — before the candidate has answered a single substantive question. That impression tends to shape everything that follows. The halo effect carries a strong first impression forward so that subsequent answers look better than they are. The horns effect works in the opposite direction, making a poor first impression difficult to overcome even with strong answers.</p>

<p>Affinity bias pulls interviewers toward candidates who remind them of themselves. Confirmation bias leads interviewers to seek out information that confirms the impression they formed early, and to discount information that contradicts it. These two biases often work together: an interviewer who likes a candidate will ask easier follow-ups and interpret vague answers charitably, without noticing either tendency. For a full breakdown of each bias type and where it enters the process, see our article on <a href="/blog/interview-bias">interview bias</a>.</p>

<p>The practical fixes for bias are structural. Standardized questions make it harder for interviewers to follow different lines of inquiry. Independent scoring before the debrief prevents the most confident voice in the room from pulling everyone else's ratings. Diverse panels reduce the risk that affinity bias runs unchecked. Focusing evaluation criteria on what a candidate will add to the team — rather than how well they fit the existing culture — removes at least some of the room for interviewers to favor candidates who remind them of colleagues they already like.</p>

<h2>The candidate experience during interviews</h2>

<p>How a candidate experiences the interview process shapes whether they accept an offer if one comes, and what they tell other people about the company afterward. <a href="https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/improve-interview-process/">Research from Aptitude Research, cited by Glassdoor</a>, found that 82% of recruiters have lost a top candidate because of a poor interview experience. <p>SHRM reports that <a href="https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/interview-critical-part-hiring-process-candidates-say">68% of candidates say the interview process positively or negatively affects their decision to accept a job offer</a>. That figure is worth sitting with. The interview is not just an evaluation tool for the employer. It is the primary experience through which candidates decide whether they want to work for this company — and through which they form an impression that they carry out into the world regardless of the outcome.</p>

<p>The basics are worth getting right. Share an itinerary before the interview so candidates know who they're meeting and in what order. Start on time. Disclose the salary range early, so candidates aren't spending multiple interview rounds on a role that won't work financially. Finish the full interview with every candidate regardless of early impressions — cutting it short based on disinterest invites reputational risk. Reserve at least ten minutes at the end for candidate questions. A candidate who cannot ask questions cannot assess fit either, and an employer who does not allow them to signals that mutual assessment goes only one way.</p>

<h2>Giving feedback after the interview</h2>

<p>Interview feedback is the final stage of the hiring process to close the interview in a respectful manner. It is usually given to candidates who were not selected, explaining why and offering actionable guidance for improvement. Most companies skip it entirely, and candidates notice.</p>

<p>Feedback given well is specific: it names the gap between what the candidate demonstrated and what the role required, and it gives the candidate something they can act on. Generic responses — "We decided to move forward with other candidates" — are of little value to the person receiving them. Detailed, honest feedback demonstrates respect and, as a secondary effect, leaves the candidate with a better impression of the company than a silence or a form letter would. Some of them will apply again for future roles, refer colleagues, or become customers. For scripts that work across the most common rejection scenarios, see our article on <a href="/blog/interview-feedback-examples">interview feedback examples</a>. For the rejection email itself, see our article on <a href="/blog/job-rejection-email">how to write personalized job rejection emails</a>.</p>

<p>Recording feedback in a consistent format matters as much as giving it. When interviewers submit written reviews through a shared template, the team builds a record of why each candidate advanced or was rejected. That record is useful for the debrief, useful if a decision is later questioned, and useful for identifying patterns in the process — where the team is consistently losing good candidates, or where the criteria being applied don't match the requirements in the job description.</p>

<p>Feedback given well is specific: it names the gap between what the candidate demonstrated and what the role required, and it gives the candidate something they can act on. Generic responses — "We decided to move forward with other candidates" — are of little value to the person receiving them. Detailed, honest feedback demonstrates respect and, as a secondary effect, leaves the candidate with a better impression of the company than a silence or a form letter would. Some of them will apply again for future roles, refer colleagues, or become customers. For scripts that work across the most common rejection scenarios, see our article on <a href="/blog/interview-feedback-examples">interview feedback examples</a>. For the rejection email itself, see our article on <a href="/blog/job-rejection-email">how to write personalized job rejection emails</a>.</p>

<p>Recording feedback in a consistent format matters as much as giving it. When interviewers submit written reviews through a shared template, the team builds a record of why each candidate advanced or was rejected. That record is useful for the debrief, useful if a decision is later questioned, and useful for identifying patterns in the process: where the team is consistently losing good candidates, or where the criteria being applied don't match the requirements in the job description.</p>

## The trade-off between structure and flexibility

### As written

<h2>The trade-off between structure and flexibility</h2>

<p>Structured interviewing is not the only way to run an interview, and hiring teams sometimes resist it because it can feel constraining. Requiring every interviewer to ask the same questions in the same order removes the space for a conversation that develops naturally. Some candidates — particularly those interviewing for creative, senior, or relationship-driven roles — may respond better to a more open exchange than to a question list that feels like a form being filled out.</p>

<p>The value of unstructured interviewing is that it allows an interviewer to follow unexpected threads, respond to what the candidate actually says, and get a richer read on who the person is. The cost is that the information it produces is harder to compare across candidates and more vulnerable to the biases described above. The research is consistent: for most roles and most hiring teams, a structured approach produces better decisions. The practical middle ground is to standardize the questions and the scoring while giving interviewers the latitude to probe and follow up within that frame. That is the structure of a good conversation, not the absence of one.</p>
