## Final

<p>If you find all the different types of interviews confusing, or feel like every interview is a blur, this guide will make everything about interviewing clearer. An interview is a meeting between a candidate and one or more persons in which the potential employer's representatives ask a series of questions.</p>

<p>An interview is also an assessment that runs in both directions, with the employer evaluating the candidate's qualifications, experience, and judgment, and the candidate evaluating whether the role, the team, and the company are worth their time. It can take many forms — a phone screening, a coding exercise, a panel conversation, or a final meeting with a founder — and a single hiring process often uses several in sequence. What interviewers ask, how they score answers, how they structure the conversation, and how they handle bias all determine whether the process produces a sound hiring decision.</p>

<p>This article covers every major interview format and what each is for, how to design questions that produce useful answers, how to score what you hear, how to manage interview bias, and how to close the process with feedback that reflects well on your company.</p>
<h2>How the interview process fits together</h2>

<p>A hiring process typically moves a candidate from application to shortlist to interview rounds to decision. The interview portion usually spans several formats, each designed to answer a different question: a phone screening answers whether a candidate is worth bringing in, a behavioral interview answers how they've handled situations that will come up again in the new role, a technical interview answers whether they can do the specific work, and a final interview answers whether this is the person the whole organization can commit to hiring. When each format is treated as a distinct question to answer, a series of meetings becomes a reliable process.</p>

<p>Few hiring processes use every format; most combine two or three based on the role, the candidate's seniority, and what the team already knows from the application and any assessments. For the full arc from workforce planning to onboarding, see the article on <a href="/blog/talent-acquisition">talent acquisition</a>.</p>
<h2>Phone and video screenings</h2>

<p>A phone screening is a brief call, sometimes as short as ten minutes, in which an employer verifies basic qualifications, clarifies outstanding questions from the application, or confirms the candidate's continued interest, with the goal of building a shortlist before anyone invests more time. It is not a full interview, and it should not be run like one. Its job is to filter efficiently so that the in-depth conversations that follow involve only candidates who genuinely fit the role.</p>

<p>Keeping those goals in mind shapes what to ask. Good screening questions cover whether the candidate's experience matches what the role requires, whether their availability and salary expectations are compatible with the position, and whether their interest in the work is genuine. When answers are vague at the screening stage, brief follow-up questions such as "Can you say more about how you used that?" or "What drew you to that kind of work?" can tell you quickly whether the application reflects real experience or surface-level match.</p>

<p>When those screening conversations move to video, the same logic applies. A live video interview follows the same dynamics as an in-person conversation, with the added logistical advantage of not requiring either party to travel. One-way video interviews, in which candidates record answers to pre-recorded questions on their own schedule, are increasingly common at the screening stage because they let hiring teams review responses outside of real-time constraints. They also create a record every evaluator can review independently, which helps with consistency. Whatever the format, share what candidates can expect beforehand — who they're speaking with, how long the call will take, and what the next steps are.</p>
<h2>Structured versus unstructured interviewing</h2>

<p>The difference between structured and unstructured interviewing matters for every format that follows. A structured interview standardizes the questions and evaluation: each candidate for the same role receives the same questions in the same order and is scored against the same criteria. An unstructured interview is a relatively open conversation in which the interviewer adapts questions to each candidate to get a general read on the person. Most teams land somewhere between the two — a rough list of questions, variable follow-ups, no formal scoring — which produces some of the drawbacks of each.</p>

<p>ERE, citing research from Google's re:Work initiative, reports that <a href="https://www.ere.net/why-structured-interviews-are-the-best-way-to-hire/">structured interviews predict job performance with 26% accuracy</a>, matching general cognitive ability tests as the second-strongest available predictor. Work sample tests come in just above that, at 29%. Gut-feel, unstructured interviewing falls well below both. And among organizations with the best-rated candidate experiences, <a href="https://www.ere.net/articles/5-research-backed-ways-to-improve-your-interviews/">seven of the top ten conduct structured interviews</a>. The discipline of standardizing questions and scoring is part of what makes those experiences good, improving data quality for the employer and giving candidates a process that feels fair and prepared.</p>

<p>Unstructured interviewing lets the interviewer follow unexpected threads and have a real exchange with the candidate. That freedom to adapt is worth preserving. The best structured interviews leave room for real conversation within the framework, rather than treating it as a rigid script. Consistency in what you measure matters more than how loosely or formally you talk through the questions.</p>
<h2>Behavioral interviews</h2>

<p>Behavioral interviews focus on how candidates have acted in real situations in their previous jobs. Past behavior predicts future behavior. A candidate's answer about a real conflict from two years ago is more revealing than their answer about how they would handle a hypothetical one. Questions begin with prompts like "Tell me about a time when…" or "Give me an example of a situation where you…."</p>

<p>The STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) gives candidates a structure for answering these questions and gives interviewers a consistent framework for evaluating responses. When an answer stays at the level of "we" rather than "I," or describes what the team did without naming what the candidate specifically contributed, a follow-up probe brings it back to the individual: "What specifically did you do in that situation?" or "What would you handle differently today?" These follow-up probes reveal whether the candidate can reflect honestly on their own performance.</p>

<p>Those follow-up probes work best when you define the competencies to assess before the first candidate arrives. For a structured set of questions organized by competency — problem solving, judgment, prioritization, ownership, leadership, and adaptability — see our article on <a href="/blog/unique-interview-questions">unique interview questions that reveal how candidates think</a>.</p>
<h2>Situational interviews</h2>

<p>Situational questions present candidates with a hypothetical scenario, often one they'd face in the new role, and ask how they'd respond. Starters include "What would you do if…" and "How would you handle…." They are useful for candidates with limited directly applicable experience, because they test reasoning and judgment in the moment. A recent graduate interviewing for a management role, for example, may not have fired anyone yet, but can be asked how they would approach a performance issue on their team.</p>

<p>The trade-off with situational questions is that the answers describe intent, not action. A candidate can describe a thoughtful, well-structured response without having done anything similar. The answers still reveal how someone approaches an unfamiliar problem, but behavioral evidence from real experience carries more weight. Combining situational and behavioral questions in the same interview covers both what a candidate thinks and what they've actually done, giving you a more complete picture.</p>
<h2>Technical interviews</h2>

<p>Technical interviews ask candidates to solve problems live, complete coding challenges, or walk through case analyses using tools and frameworks specific to the job. They are most common in engineering, data, and finance roles, and they tend to be the longest individual interview format, running anywhere from one to three hours.</p>

<p>To produce useful signal, build rubrics around specific, measurable competencies — problem decomposition, code quality, communication, testing awareness, and trade-off reasoning — rather than general impressions. Questions should be open-ended and have more than one valid approach, so the interviewer can assess reasoning rather than whether the candidate memorized a particular answer. The best technical interview questions reveal how a candidate thinks about edge cases, not just whether they arrived at a correct solution.</p>

<p>What those questions cover depends on who you are assessing. Question difficulty should scale with the seniority of the role. Junior candidates get questions on syntax, standard library usage, and core algorithms. Senior candidates get trade-off and architectural judgment questions, where the right answer depends on context and there is no single correct response. After a take-home assessment or coding exercise, hold a debrief conversation that follows naturally from the submission itself. Ask how the candidate weighed alternatives, why they made the decisions they did, and how they respond to questions about their own work. Code and documents do not capture that reasoning. Ask them to explain a technical concept as if speaking to someone outside their field. The ability to calibrate language for the audience is a signal about communication skills that the code itself cannot give you.</p>

<p>Consistency makes comparison possible, just as it does in behavioral interviews. Asking every candidate for the same role the same core questions, in the same order, with the same time budget, is the only way to compare two candidates fairly.</p>
<h2>Case interviews</h2>

<p>In a case interview, the candidate receives a hypothetical or real business problem to analyze and must propose and explain a solution. Case interviews are most common in consulting and strategy roles, where structured problem-solving under pressure is part of the daily work. For roles where creative thinking and analysis are central, <a href="/blog/problem-solving-interview-questions">problem-solving interview questions</a> can supplement or replace the case format with something more conversational.</p>

<p>What makes the format work, though, is preparation before the candidate arrives. Evaluating a case interview requires deciding in advance what a good answer looks like. Evaluators rarely agree on a single correct answer — in most cases there isn't one — so evaluators must agree on what they are assessing before the conversation begins. That means deciding before the interview whether evaluators are assessing how the candidate structures the problem, how the candidate handles ambiguous information, or how clearly the candidate communicates under pressure. Without agreed criteria, different evaluators will walk away from the same case with incompatible conclusions.</p>
<h2>Panel interviews</h2>

<p>A panel interview involves two or more interviewers — often including the hiring manager, a potential peer, and an HR representative — sitting together to question a single candidate. With several people hearing the same answers at the same time, the format gives multiple stakeholders ownership of the hiring decision, and each panelist records an impression before the group deliberates, making individual bias harder to sustain.</p>

<p>Running a panel well requires careful preparation before anyone enters the room. Assign each member specific questions in advance so the interview follows a logical flow. Have everyone review the resume and job description before the interview, not during it. After the interview, have the most junior panelist share their assessment first, so senior opinions do not set the tone before others have committed to a view. Have each panelist submit an individual scorecard before the debrief conversation, so the group discusses evidence rather than building consensus around whoever spoke first.</p>
<h2>Group interviews</h2>

<p>In a group interview, several interviewers from the hiring organization meet simultaneously with a candidate, with some panel members present only to observe rather than to ask questions. Group interviews are less common than panel interviews. They are most useful when the ability to present to multiple stakeholders, read a room, or perform effectively under observation is a central requirement of the role itself — a sales or client-facing position, for example, where the interview setting approximates real work conditions.</p>

<p>When using a group format, brief the observers in advance on what they're watching for and give them a scoring rubric so their observations can be compared afterward. An observer who attends without a framework will walk away with impressions rather than assessable evidence, which is a poor return on the time invested.</p>

<h2>Final interviews</h2>

<p>A final interview usually involves a senior decision-maker — a founder, an executive, or the hiring manager's manager — who assesses fit at a higher level than the earlier rounds. By this stage, technical qualifications are settled. The final interview answers whether this is the specific person the organization wants to commit to, and it gives the candidate a genuine opportunity to meet the leadership they would be working under and to ask questions that earlier-stage interviewers may not have been able to answer.</p>

<p>Final interviews carry a particular responsibility toward the candidate. By this point, the candidate has sat through multiple rounds, completed assessments, and rearranged their schedule several times. The final interviewer should arrive as prepared as anyone earlier in the process, having read the resume, reviewed the feedback from prior rounds, and thought carefully about what the conversation is meant to resolve.</p>
<h2>Designing interview questions that produce useful answers</h2>

<p>The questions you ask determine what you learn. Generic questions produce generic answers. Questions that begin with "Tell me about yourself" or "What is your greatest weakness?" are so widely rehearsed that the answers reveal little about how a candidate actually works. Strong question design separates a reliable evaluation from an exercise in confirming first impressions.</p>

<p>Effective interview questions share a few qualities. They ask candidates to describe what they did in a specific past situation, not what they would do in a hypothetical one. They have more than one valid answer, so the interviewer can assess reasoning rather than recall. They connect directly to what the role requires. If a customer-facing role demands patience under pressure, the question should probe for a real situation in which the candidate demonstrated that — not for a general statement about their approach to difficult customers. And they are phrased simply enough that the candidate can answer the question asked rather than the question they expected to be asked.</p>

<p>Question design also means knowing what not to ask. Any question that touches on arrest record, military status, pregnancy, age, religion, marital status, or other protected characteristics has no place in an interview. These questions expose the organization to legal risk. More directly, they leave candidates uncertain about whether a casual exchange about their personal life shaped a decision that was supposed to be about their qualifications.</p>
<h2>Scoring what you hear</h2>

<p>An interview without a scoring method produces opinions, not evidence. When each interviewer walks away with a different impression and no common framework for comparing notes, the debrief becomes a negotiation between strong personalities rather than a review of what candidates demonstrated. The person who speaks most confidently tends to carry the most weight, regardless of whether their assessment is the most accurate.</p>

<p>A <a href="/blog/behavioral-interview-scoring-matrix">behavioral interview scoring matrix</a> gives each interviewer a grid that lists the competencies the role requires, with behavioral descriptions of what a weak, adequate, and strong answer looks like for each one. Behaviorally anchored rating scales (BARS) go further, naming what each score looks like in plain behavioral terms so two interviewers rating the same answer are far more likely to reach the same score. A useful scorecard includes space for direct quotes and specific examples alongside numerical scores, giving the debrief something concrete to discuss beyond competing ratings.</p>

<p>That independent record also makes the debrief more resistant to bias. According to a <a href="https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/11-must-ask-behavioral-interview-questions/">Gartner survey</a>, 39% of HR leaders say structured interviews help reduce bias. The scoring method is a large part of why. When scores are recorded independently before the group debrief, each interviewer's judgment is on record and harder to revise in response to social pressure from colleagues.</p>
<h2>Interview bias</h2>

<p>Interview bias is any preference or mental shortcut that shapes how an interviewer judges a candidate on factors unrelated to their ability to do the job. Most of these judgments happen quickly and feel like sound instinct to the person making them, which is exactly what makes bias difficult to address. It rarely feels like bias from the inside.</p>

<p>The types that appear most often in hiring include first impression bias, the halo and horns effect, affinity bias, confirmation bias, the contrast effect, recency and primacy bias, stereotype bias, and groupthink. <a href="/blog/first-impression-bias">First impression bias</a> causes people to form a quick and incomplete impression of someone based on what they see and hear in the first few seconds of meeting, before the candidate has answered a single substantive question. That impression tends to shape everything that follows. The halo effect carries a strong first impression forward so that subsequent answers look better than they are. The horns effect works in the opposite direction, making a poor first impression difficult to overcome even with strong answers.</p>

<p>Beyond those first-impression effects, affinity bias pulls interviewers toward candidates who remind them of themselves. Confirmation bias leads interviewers to seek out information that confirms the impression they formed early, and to discount information that contradicts it. These two biases often work together. An interviewer who likes a candidate will ask easier follow-up questions and interpret vague answers charitably, without noticing either tendency. For a full breakdown of each bias type and where it enters the process, see our article on <a href="/blog/interview-bias">interview bias</a>.</p>

<p>The practical fixes for bias are structural. Standardized questions make it harder for interviewers to follow different lines of inquiry. Independent scoring before the debrief prevents the most confident voice in the room from pulling everyone else's ratings. Diverse panels reduce the risk that affinity bias runs unchecked. Focusing evaluation criteria on what a candidate will add to the team, rather than how well they fit the existing culture, removes at least some of the room for interviewers to favor candidates who remind them of colleagues they already like.</p>
<h2>Pre-employment assessments alongside the interview</h2>

<p>Interviews rarely stand alone in a well-designed hiring process. Pre-employment assessments — skills tests, work samples, structured take-home projects — give the team information about what a candidate can actually do, separate from how they perform in a conversation. SHRM's 2024 Talent Trends Report found that <a href="https://www.shrm.org/content/dam/en/shrm/research/2024-talent-trends-research-overall-findings.pdf">78% of organizations report that pre-employment assessments used during the interview process have improved quality of hire</a>. Work sample tests, which ask a candidate to do a version of the actual work, are among the strongest predictors of job performance available.</p>

<p>Where you place an assessment in the process matters as much as what it asks. A demanding take-home placed early in the process, before a candidate has had any real contact with the team or any sense of whether the role is worth their time, risks losing strong applicants who have other options. After a screening call, when candidates are more engaged and have a clearer picture of the opportunity, more candidates complete the assessment and the work they submit better reflects what they can do on the job.</p>
<h2>The candidate experience during interviews</h2>

<p>How a candidate experiences the interview process shapes whether they accept an offer if one comes, and what they tell other people about the company afterward. <a href="https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/improve-interview-process/">Aptitude Research found that 82% of recruiters have lost a top candidate because of a poor interview experience</a>. When that happens, the search starts over, usually with a smaller pool than before.</p>

<p>That same dynamic shapes the candidate who does accept. <a href="https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/interview-critical-part-hiring-process-candidates-say">SHRM reports that 68% of candidates say the interview process positively or negatively affects their decision to accept a job offer</a>. The interview is also how candidates decide whether they want to work for the company — and the impression they form there stays with them, and travels, regardless of the outcome.</p>

<p>Getting the basics right protects the offer. Share an itinerary before the interview so candidates know who they're meeting and in what order. Start on time. Disclose the salary range early, so candidates aren't spending multiple interview rounds on a role that won't work financially. Finish the full interview with every candidate regardless of early impressions — cutting it short based on disinterest invites reputational risk. Reserve at least ten minutes at the end for candidate questions. A candidate who cannot ask questions cannot assess fit either, and an employer who does not allow them to signals that mutual assessment goes only one way.</p>
<h2>Giving feedback after the interview</h2>

<p>Interview feedback closes the hiring process with respect for every candidate who went through it. Most companies give it to candidates who were not selected, naming the gap and offering actionable guidance for improvement. Few companies do it, and candidates leave with nothing to act on.</p>

<p>Feedback given well is specific: it names the gap between what the candidate demonstrated and what the role required, and it gives the candidate something they can act on. Generic responses like "We decided to move forward with other candidates" are of little value to the person receiving them. Detailed, honest feedback demonstrates respect and leaves the candidate with a better impression of the company than a silence or a form letter would. Some of them will apply again for future roles, refer colleagues, or become customers. For scripts that work across the most common rejection scenarios, see our article on <a href="/blog/interview-feedback-examples">interview feedback examples</a>. For the rejection email itself, see our article on <a href="/blog/job-rejection-email">how to write personalized job rejection emails</a>.</p>

<p>Giving that feedback well is only part of the task. Recording it in a consistent format matters just as much. When interviewers submit written reviews through a shared template, the team builds a record of why each candidate advanced or was rejected. That record serves the debrief, protects the decision if it is later questioned, and reveals patterns in the process — where the team is consistently losing good candidates, or where the criteria being applied don't match the requirements in the job description.</p>
<h2>The trade-off between structure and flexibility</h2>

<p>Structured interviewing is not universally adopted, and hiring teams sometimes resist it because it feels constraining. Requiring every interviewer to ask the same questions in the same order removes the space for a conversation that develops naturally. Some candidates — particularly those interviewing for creative, senior, or relationship-driven roles — respond better to an open exchange than to a question list that feels like a form being filled out.</p>

<p>Unstructured interviewing gives the interviewer room to push on something interesting, skip what's already been covered, and respond to the candidate as an individual. The practical middle ground is to standardize the questions and the scoring while giving interviewers the latitude to probe and follow up within that frame. That is the structure of a good conversation, not the absence of one.</p>
<h2>Put this into practice</h2>

<p>Running a disciplined interview process — from a structured screening call through scored debriefs to prompt, specific feedback — means you get information you can actually use. Candidates experience the company at its best, decisions are easier to make and to explain, and the person hired is more likely to be the right one.</p>
