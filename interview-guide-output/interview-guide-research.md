**Definitions**

**Interview**
Polymer's talent acquisition article describes an interview as "an opportunity for you to see if the candidate is a good fit (and vice versa)," presenting a chance for both sides to ask questions and assess mutual fit. Source: Polymer, "What is talent acquisition? A complete guide," https://www.polymer.co/blog/talent-acquisition

**Structured interview**
A structured interview standardizes the questions and evaluation methods used: each candidate for the same role receives the same questions in the same order and is scored against the same criteria. Polymer describes the process as "using purposefully designed standardized questions and an interview scorecard (or interview rubric) to rate candidates based on their skills, experience, values, and cultural fit." Source: Polymer, "Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix: A Template & Simple Guide," https://www.polymer.co/blog/behavioral-interview-scoring-matrix

**Unstructured interview**
An unstructured interview involves a relatively unplanned conversation with each candidate that differs across interviews, aiming to get a "feel for" the candidate rather than evaluating against a fixed set of criteria. Source: Glassdoor, "Terrific Interview Techniques for Interviewers and Interviewees," https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/guide/interview-techniques/

**Behavioral interview**
Behavioral interviews focus on actions candidates have taken in previous jobs; the interviewer assesses how people approached and resolved various work problems. The principle is that past performance can predict future behavior. Questions typically begin with "Tell me about a time when…" or "Give me an example of a time when…." Source: SHRM, "Interviewing Basics," https://www.shrm.org/mena/topics-tools/news/hr-magazine/interviewing-basics; Polymer, "What is talent acquisition? A complete guide," https://www.polymer.co/blog/talent-acquisition

**Situational interview**
Situational questions get at how candidates might react to a scenario or problem they could face in the new job — often hypothetical — using starters such as "What would you do if…" and "Tell me how you would handle…." They test a candidate's ability to think through problems, but may not illuminate prior work history. Source: SHRM, "Interviewing Basics," https://www.shrm.org/mena/topics-tools/news/hr-magazine/interviewing-basics; Polymer, "What is talent acquisition? A complete guide," https://www.polymer.co/blog/talent-acquisition

**Technical interview**
Common for engineering, data, and finance roles, a technical interview asks candidates to solve problems live, complete coding challenges, or walk through case analyses using tools and frameworks specific to the job. These are typically the longest individual interview format, running one to three hours. Source: Glassdoor, "How to prepare for a job interview," https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/guide/the-ultimate-job-interview-preparation-guide/

**Case interview**
Each applicant is given a hypothetical or existing business situation or problem to analyze, must draw on knowledge and creative abilities to propose a solution, and explains how they arrived at it. Common in consulting and strategy roles. Source: SHRM, "Interviewing Basics," https://www.shrm.org/mena/topics-tools/news/hr-magazine/interviewing-basics

**Panel interview**
A panel interview involves two or more interviewers — who may include HR representatives, hiring managers, and team leads — sitting together to question a single candidate. It gives multiple stakeholders ownership of the hiring decision and can eliminate the need for multiple separate rounds. Source: U.S. Department of Labor, "TEAMS Interview Skills Participant Guide 2026," https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/VETS/files/InterviewSkills_PG_Interactive_Feb2026.pdf

**Group interview**
In a group interview, several people from the hiring organization meet simultaneously with each candidate; panel members take turns asking questions, with some present only to observe. Source: SHRM, "Interviewing Basics," https://www.shrm.org/mena/topics-tools/news/hr-magazine/interviewing-basics

**Phone screening**
A phone screening is a brief, sometimes unscheduled call — as short as 10 minutes — in which an employer verifies basic qualifications, clarifies outstanding application questions, or confirms the candidate's continued interest, with the goal of creating a shortlist before a formal interview. Source: U.S. Department of Labor, "TEAMS Interview Skills Participant Guide 2026," https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/VETS/files/InterviewSkills_PG_Interactive_Feb2026.pdf

**Video interview**
A live video interview takes place online between employer and candidate. A one-way video interview uses a platform that presents pre-recorded questions, which the candidate answers while recording their responses. Source: U.S. Department of Labor, "TEAMS Interview Skills Participant Guide 2026," https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/VETS/files/InterviewSkills_PG_Interactive_Feb2026.pdf

**Final interview**
Not explicitly defined in the sources retrieved.

**Interview bias**
Polymer defines interview bias as "any preference or mental shortcut that shapes how an interviewer judges a candidate on factors unrelated to their ability to do the job." Common types include first impression bias, the halo and horns effect, affinity bias, confirmation bias, the contrast effect, recency and primacy bias, stereotype bias, and groupthink. Source: Polymer, "Interview Bias: The Types, Where It Creeps In, and How to Reduce It," https://www.polymer.co/blog/interview-bias

**Interview scoring / scorecard**
An interview scorecard lists predetermined criteria used to evaluate candidates; interviewers score each candidate against the criteria immediately after the interview. Polymer's behavioral interview scoring matrix uses this approach with a five-point rating scale. Source: Polymer, "Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix: A Template & Simple Guide," https://www.polymer.co/blog/behavioral-interview-scoring-matrix

**Interview feedback**
Polymer defines interview feedback as "the final stage of the hiring process to close the interview process in a respectful manner," usually given to candidates who were not selected, explaining why and offering actionable guidance for improvement. Source: Polymer, "Interview Feedback Examples: What to Say and How to Deliver It," https://www.polymer.co/blog/interview-feedback-examples

**Best practices**

**The Predictive Index — "Interview Best Practices for Hiring Managers"**
https://www.predictiveindex.com/blog/interview-best-practices-for-hiring-managers/

Build the scorecard before candidates arrive: "Scoring criteria defined after a strong candidate impresses you aren't criteria anymore." A useful scorecard includes competencies, a rating scale, space for specific examples and direct quotes, and an overall recommendation field. Brief the panel in advance — everyone should review the resume, job description, and screening notes before the interview, not during it. Follow the 80/20 rule: "Aim for the candidate to carry roughly 80% of the conversation. Resist the urge to fill silence, elaborate on the question, or jump in before the candidate has finished thinking." Root questions in real experience rather than hypotheticals; the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) gives candidates a structure and gives managers a consistent framework. When answers are vague or rehearsed, probe with follow-ups such as "What specifically did you do in that situation?" or "What would you handle differently now?"

**SocialTalent — "Interviewing 101: Best Practice Interview Techniques"**
https://www.socialtalent.com/interviewing/interviewing-best-practices

Define clear hiring criteria that separate must-haves from nice-to-haves before the process begins. Conduct structured screening calls to verify qualifications, motivation, and fit before progressing candidates. For panel debriefs, have the most junior interviewer speak first "so disagreement gets voiced before senior opinions set the tone." On bias: use diverse panels, standardized questions, and focus on culture-add rather than culture-fit. On feedback: "Offer specific, actionable feedback to candidates. Generic comments are of little value; detailed insights demonstrate respect and consideration."

**University of Wisconsin HR — "Best Practices for Employment Interviews"**
https://hr.wisc.edu/hr-professionals/recruitment/best-practices-for-employment-interviews/

For question pacing, allow approximately three minutes for single-scenario questions and closer to six minutes for multipart questions. Reserve at least ten minutes at the end for candidate questions. Consider sending interview questions at least 24 hours in advance to ease nerves and elicit more thoughtful answers. Assign specific questions to each panel member to ensure a structured and efficient process. Ensure all questions are consistent across candidates and avoid any that touch on arrest record, military status, pregnancy, age, religion, marital status, or other protected characteristics.

**Forbes — "Interviewing Etiquette 101: 15 Best Practices For Hiring Managers And Headhunters"**
https://www.forbes.com/councils/forbeshumanresourcescouncil/2023/01/04/interviewing-etiquette-101-15-best-practices-for-hiring-managers-and-headhunters/

Share an interview itinerary with candidates beforehand so they know what to expect. Finish the full interview with every candidate regardless of early impressions: "cutting an interview short, based on disinterest, invites reputational risk to a company." Disclose salary range up front. Never ask questions about protected characteristics — "managers should never leave a candidate wondering whether small talk about their place of worship or sexual orientation may have cost them the job."

**Best practices: technical interviews**

**Source 1: HackerRank, "How to Conduct a Technical Interview: A Complete Guide for Hiring Teams," https://www.hackerrank.com/writing/how-to-conduct-a-technical-interview**

Before writing a single question, define what success looks like in the role for the first six months — the skills you test should map directly to that answer. Select questions in advance: "The best technical interview questions are open-ended, have multiple valid approaches, and reveal reasoning rather than recall." Pilot new questions internally first. Send candidates a clear brief 24–48 hours ahead covering duration, format, what to bring, and who they'll meet. During the interview, take specific notes on what candidates said and did — "Candidate immediately asked about edge cases" is useful; "seemed sharp" is not. Beyond code, assess communication, collaboration signals, problem framing, code quality, and edge-case awareness. Hold a structured debrief with scorecards submitted independently before the group call.

**Source 2: Expert Hire, "How to conduct a technical interview you can actually defend," https://www.experthire.io/blog/how-to-conduct-a-technical-interview**

Ask every candidate for the same role the same core questions, in the same order, with the same time budget: "Consistency is not bureaucracy; it is the only way to compare two candidates honestly." Level questions to seniority — juniors get mechanics questions, seniors get trade-offs and judgment. Common mistakes: trivia over reasoning, improvised questions per candidate, gut-feel scoring ("'I liked them' is where bias hides"), and talking too much ("The candidate should be doing most of the talking and reasoning").

**Source 3: daily.dev Recruiter, "Technical Interview Best Practices: Reducing Bias and Improving Signal," https://recruiter.daily.dev/resources/technical-interview-best-practices-reducing-bias-improving-signal/**

Build rubrics around 5–7 core competencies (problem decomposition, code quality, system design trade-offs, testing strategy, communication), each on a 1–5 numerical scale tied to specific, measurable behaviors — for example, "provided clear examples of resolving technical disagreements" rather than "seemed like a team player." Weight competencies by role importance and set minimum thresholds to avoid hiring candidates with critical gaps. Match format to role: live coding for real-time problem-solving, take-homes for independent work, system design for senior and staff engineers focused on architectural thinking.

**Source 4: Centum Search, "Technical Interview Process: A Hiring Manager's Guide," https://centumsearch.com/blog/technical-interview-process-hiring-managers-guide/**

After a take-home or coding assessment, hold a debrief conversation: "This is often more revealing than the code itself. It surfaces communication skills, self-awareness, and the reasoning behind their decisions." To assess communication, ask a candidate to explain a technical concept as if speaking to a non-engineer and watch whether they calibrate their language or get lost in jargon. For collaboration, ask candidates to describe a time they disagreed with a technical decision and how they handled it.
