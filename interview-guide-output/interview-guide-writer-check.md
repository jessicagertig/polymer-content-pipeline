<h2>What an interview is, and what it does</h2>
<p>An interview is a structured exchange, not simply a conversation. The employer's side is trying to find out whether a candidate can do the job, will do it, and will work well with the team. The candidate's side is trying to find out whether the role and the company are right for them. When both sides are doing this well, an interview produces information that neither side could get any other way.</p>
<p>That mutual-assessment aspect matters more than it often gets credit for. SHRM reports that <a href="https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/interview-critical-part-hiring-process-candidates-say">68% of candidates say a company's hiring process positively or negatively impacts their decision to accept a job offer</a>. The interview is usually the most visible and consequential part of that process.</p>
<p>Most interview processes move candidates through a sequence of formats, each one answering a different question. Phone screenings answer whether a candidate is worth bringing in. Technical interviews answer whether they can do the work. Behavioral interviews answer how they've handled situations that will come up again. The final interview answers whether this is the person everyone can commit to hiring. Understanding what each format is for — and running it accordingly — is what turns a series of conversations into a reliable process.</p>

<h2>The interview process from first contact to decision</h2>
<p>A hiring process typically follows a predictable path: a job is posted, applications arrive, a shortlist is built, candidates move through one or more interview rounds, and a decision is made. The interview portion of that path usually spans several formats. Here is how they fit together.</p>

<h3>Phone and video screenings</h3>
<p>A phone screening is a brief call — sometimes as short as ten minutes — in which an employer verifies basic qualifications, clarifies outstanding application questions, or confirms the candidate's continued interest, with the goal of creating a shortlist before a formal interview. It is not a full interview, and it should not be treated as one. Its job is to filter candidates before anyone invests more time in the process. Good screening questions cover whether the candidate's experience matches the role's requirements, whether their availability and salary expectations are compatible with the position, and whether they're genuinely interested in the work. See our collection of <a href="/blog/problem-solving-interview-questions">problem-solving interview questions</a> for the kinds of questions that reveal real thinking even at the screening stage.</p>
<p>A video interview works the same way, but takes place online. Live video interviews follow the same dynamics as an in-person conversation. One-way video interviews, in which candidates record answers to pre-recorded questions, are increasingly common at the screening stage because they let hiring teams review responses on their own schedule.</p>

<h3>Structured versus unstructured interviews</h3>
<p>The difference between structured and unstructured interviewing matters for every format that follows. A structured interview standardizes the questions and evaluation: each candidate for the same role receives the same questions in the same order and is scored against the same criteria. An unstructured interview is a relatively free conversation with each candidate, aimed at getting a "feel" for the person. Most teams do something between the two extremes — a rough list of questions, variable follow-ups, and no formal scoring — which captures some of the benefits of neither.</p>
<p>ERE, citing research from Google's re:Work initiative, reports that <a href="https://www.ere.net/why-structured-interviews-are-the-best-way-to-hire/">structured interviews predict job performance with 26% accuracy</a>, placing them alongside general cognitive ability tests as among the strongest available predictors. For context, work sample tests come in just above that, at 29%. Gut-feel, unstructured interviews fall well below both. And among top-performing organizations, <a href="https://www.ere.net/articles/5-research-backed-ways-to-improve-your-interviews/">seven of the top ten companies with the highest-rated candidate experiences conduct structured interviews</a>. Consistency and scoring discipline are part of what makes those experiences good.</p>

<h3>Behavioral interviews</h3>
<p>Behavioral interviews focus on how candidates have acted in real situations in their previous jobs. The underlying principle is that past performance can predict future behavior. Questions begin with prompts like "Tell me about a time when…" or "Give me an example of a situation where you…." The STAR method — Situation, Task, Action, Result — gives candidates a structure for answering these questions and gives interviewers a consistent framework for evaluating responses. When an answer is vague or rehearsed, follow-up probes like "What specifically did you do in that situation?" and "What would you do differently today?" push the candidate toward concrete detail.</p>
<p>Behavioral interviews are most useful when the competencies being assessed are defined before the interview begins. Deciding after a strong candidate impresses you which criteria matter is not criteria-setting — it's post-hoc rationalization. For a full set of structured behavioral questions organized by competency, see our article on <a href="/blog/unique-interview-questions">unique interview questions that reveal how candidates think</a>.</p>

<h3>Situational interviews</h3>
<p>Situational questions present candidates with a hypothetical scenario — often one they'd face in the role — and ask how they'd respond. Starters include "What would you do if…" and "How would you handle…." They test reasoning and judgment in the moment, which makes them useful for roles where the candidate has limited directly applicable experience. The trade-off is that situational answers describe intent, not action: a candidate can describe a thoughtful, well-structured response without having ever done anything like it. Combining situational questions with behavioral ones covers both what a candidate thinks and what they've actually done.</p>

<h3>Technical interviews</h3>
<p>Technical interviews ask candidates to solve problems live, complete coding challenges, or walk through case analyses using the tools and frameworks specific to the job. They're most common in engineering, data, and finance roles. These tend to be the longest individual interview format, running anywhere from one to three hours.</p>
<p>The key to a useful technical interview is building rubrics around specific, measurable competencies — problem decomposition, code quality, communication, testing strategy — rather than gut-feel impressions. Questions should be open-ended and have more than one valid approach, so you can see how candidates think rather than whether they memorized the right answer. Level questions to seniority: junior candidates get mechanics questions; senior candidates get trade-offs and architectural judgment. After a take-home assessment or coding exercise, hold a debrief conversation. The debrief often reveals more than the code itself, surfacing communication skills, self-awareness, and the reasoning behind decisions.</p>

<h3>Case interviews</h3>
<p>In a case interview, the candidate receives a hypothetical or real business problem to analyze and must propose and explain a solution. Case interviews are most common in consulting and strategy roles, where structured problem-solving under pressure is part of the daily work. Evaluating a case interview requires deciding in advance what a good answer looks like — not because there's one right answer, but because the evaluators need to agree on what they're looking for before the conversation begins.</p>

<h3>Panel interviews</h3>
<p>A panel interview involves two or more interviewers — which may include HR representatives, hiring managers, and team leads — sitting together to question a single candidate. The U.S. Department of Labor notes that panel interviews give multiple stakeholders ownership of the hiring decision and can eliminate the need for multiple separate rounds. They also create a natural check on individual bias: when several people hear the same answer, the impressions are harder to dismiss. When running a panel, assign each member specific questions in advance, have the most junior interviewer share feedback first so senior opinions don't set the tone, and submit scorecards independently before the debrief conversation.</p>

<h3>Group interviews</h3>
<p>In a group interview, several interviewers from the hiring organization meet simultaneously with each candidate, with some panel members present only to observe. Group interviews are less common than panel interviews and are most useful when the ability to read a room, present to multiple stakeholders, or perform under observation is part of the role itself.</p>

<h3>Final interviews</h3>
<p>A final interview usually involves a senior decision-maker — often the hiring manager's manager, a founder, or an executive — who assesses fit at a higher level than the earlier rounds did. At this stage, the technical qualifications are largely settled. The final interview answers whether this is the specific person the organization wants to commit to, and it gives the candidate a chance to meet the leadership they'd be working under.</p>

<h2>Designing interview questions</h2>
<p>The questions you ask determine what you learn. Generic questions produce generic answers. Questions that begin with "Tell me about yourself" or "What's your greatest weakness?" are so rehearsed that the answers reveal almost nothing about how a candidate actually works.</p>
<p>Effective interview questions share a few qualities. They are rooted in real experience rather than hypotheticals alone. They have more than one valid answer, so the interviewer can assess reasoning rather than recall. And they connect directly to what the role requires. If a customer-facing role demands patience under pressure, the question should probe for a real situation where the candidate demonstrated that — not for a general statement about patience. For roles where solving problems under ambiguity is central, <a href="/blog/problem-solving-interview-questions">problem-solving interview questions</a> test a candidate's ability to listen, strategize, and develop creative solutions.</p>
<p>Question design also means knowing what not to ask. Any question that touches on arrest record, military status, pregnancy, age, religion, marital status, or other protected characteristics has no place in an interview. These questions expose the organization to legal risk and, more directly, leave candidates wondering whether their answer to an apparently casual question cost them the job.</p>

<h2>Scoring what you hear</h2>
<p>An interview without a scoring method is an interview that produces opinions, not evidence. When each interviewer walks away with a different impression and no common framework for comparing notes, the debrief becomes a negotiation between strong personalities rather than a review of the candidate's qualifications.</p>
<p>A <a href="/blog/behavioral-interview-scoring-matrix">behavioral interview scoring matrix</a> gives each interviewer a grid that lists the competencies the role requires and describes — in observable behavioral terms — what a weak, acceptable, and strong answer looks like for each. Behaviorally anchored rating scales (BARS) go further, specifying what each score looks like in plain, specific behavior. That gives two interviewers a much better shot at rating the same answer the same way. A useful scorecard also includes space for direct quotes and specific examples, not just numerical scores, so the debrief has something concrete to discuss.</p>
<p>Gartner research, <a href="https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/11-must-ask-behavioral-interview-questions/">as reported by Glassdoor</a>, found that 39% of HR leaders say structured interviews help reduce bias. The scoring method is a large part of why. When scores are recorded independently before the debrief, each interviewer's judgment is on record and harder to revise in response to social pressure.</p>

<h2>Interview bias and how it enters the process</h2>
<p>Interview bias is any preference or mental shortcut that shapes how an interviewer judges a candidate on factors unrelated to their ability to do the job. Most of these judgments happen quickly and feel like sound instinct to the person making them. That's what makes bias difficult to address: it rarely feels like bias from the inside.</p>
<p>The types that appear most often in hiring include first impression bias, the halo and horns effect, affinity bias, confirmation bias, the contrast effect, recency and primacy bias, stereotype bias, and groupthink. <a href="/blog/first-impression-bias">First impression bias</a> causes people to form a quick and incomplete impression of someone based on what they see and hear in the first few seconds of meeting. That impression tends to shape everything that follows. The halo effect carries a strong first impression forward so that everything the candidate does afterward looks better than it is. The horns effect works in the opposite direction.</p>
<p>Affinity bias pulls interviewers toward candidates who remind them of themselves. Confirmation bias leads interviewers to seek out information that confirms the impression they formed early, and to discount information that contradicts it. These two biases often work together: an interviewer who likes a candidate early will ask easier follow-ups and interpret vague answers charitably. For a full breakdown of each type and where it enters the process, see our article on <a href="/blog/interview-bias">interview bias</a>.</p>
<p>The practical fixes for bias are structural. Standardized questions make it harder for each interviewer to follow a different line of inquiry. Independent scoring before the debrief prevents the most confident voice in the room from pulling everyone else's ratings. Diverse panels reduce the risk that affinity bias will run unchecked. And focusing evaluation criteria on culture-add rather than culture-fit removes at least some of the room for interviewers to favor candidates who remind them of their current team.</p>

<h2>What SHRM says about assessments and the hiring process</h2>
<p>Interviews rarely stand alone in a well-designed hiring process. Pre-employment assessments — skills tests, work samples, structured take-homes — give the team information about what a candidate can actually do, separate from how they perform in a conversation. SHRM's 2024 Talent Trends Report found that <a href="https://www.shrm.org/content/dam/en/shrm/research/2024-talent-trends-research-overall-findings.pdf">78% of organizations report that pre-employment assessments used during the interview process have improved quality of hire</a>. Work sample tests — asking a candidate to do a version of the actual work — come in as the single strongest predictor of job performance.</p>
<p>Assessments work best when they are relevant to the role and introduced at the right point in the process. Placing a demanding take-home early, before a candidate has had any real contact with the team, risks losing strong applicants who have other offers and limited time. Placing it after a screening call, when candidates are more engaged, tends to produce better completion rates and better work.</p>

<h2>The candidate experience during the interview process</h2>
<p>How a candidate experiences the interview process shapes whether they accept an offer if one comes, and what they tell other people about the company afterward. A disorganized process — late starts, interviewers who haven't read the resume, repeated questions across rounds — signals something about how the company operates. <a href="https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/improve-interview-process/">Research from Aptitude Research, cited by Glassdoor</a>, found that 82% of recruiters have lost a top candidate because of a poor interview experience. Losing a candidate you've already screened, interviewed, and selected is expensive in time, and often in the quality of the eventual hire.</p>
<p>The basics are worth getting right. Share an itinerary before the interview so candidates know who they're meeting and in what order. Start on time. Finish the full interview with every candidate regardless of early impressions — cutting it short based on disinterest invites reputational risk. Disclose salary range early. Reserve at least ten minutes at the end of the interview for candidate questions: a candidate who can't ask questions can't assess fit either, and an employer who doesn't allow it signals that fit goes only one way. All of this connects to the broader hiring process; for more on how interviews sit within that process, see our overview of <a href="/blog/hiring-process-steps">hiring process steps</a>.</p>

<h2>Giving feedback after the interview</h2>
<p>Interview feedback is the final stage of the hiring process to close the interview in a respectful manner. It is usually given to candidates who were not selected, explaining why and offering actionable guidance for improvement. Most companies don't do this, and candidates notice.</p>
<p>Feedback given well is specific: it names the gap between what the candidate showed and what the role required, and it gives the candidate something they can act on. Generic comments — "We decided to move forward with other candidates" — are of little value. Detailed feedback demonstrates respect and, as a secondary effect, leaves the candidate with a better impression of the company than they would otherwise have. Some of them will apply again, refer others, or become customers. For scripts that work across the most common rejection scenarios, see our article on <a href="/blog/interview-feedback-examples">interview feedback examples</a>. For the rejection email itself, see our article on <a href="/blog/job-rejection-email">how to write personalized job rejection emails</a>.</p>
<p>Recording feedback in a consistent format matters as much as giving it. When interviewers submit written reviews through a standard template, the team builds a record of why each candidate advanced or was rejected. That record is useful for the debrief, useful if a decision is later questioned, and useful for improving the process over time.</p>

<h2>Structured versus unstructured interviewing: the trade-off</h2>
<p>Structured interviewing is not the only way to run an interview, and teams sometimes resist it because it feels constraining. Requiring every interviewer to ask the same questions in the same order can feel like it removes the space for genuine conversation. Some candidates, particularly those who are interviewing for creative or relationship-driven roles, may respond better to a more natural exchange.</p>
<p>The case for unstructured interviewing is that it allows the interviewer to follow the candidate's lead, pursue unexpected threads, and get a richer read on who the person is. The case against it is that the information it produces is harder to compare across candidates, more vulnerable to bias, and less predictive of actual job performance. The research is consistent on this point. For most roles, a structured approach produces better decisions — but the best interviews also leave room for real conversation within the structure, rather than treating it as a rigid script.</p>

<h2>Put this into practice</h2>
<p>Running a disciplined interview process — from a structured screening call to scored panel debriefs to prompt, specific feedback — means you get information you can actually use. Candidates experience the company at its best, decisions are easier to make and to explain, and the person who gets hired is more likely to be the right one.</p>

## Final

<p>If you find all the different types of interviews confusing, or feel like every interview is a blur, this guide will make everything about interviewing clearer. An interview is a meeting between a candidate and one or more persons in which the potential employer's representatives ask a series of questions.</p>

<p>At its best, an interview is a structured exchange of information — not a conversation that meanders until one person feels a spark. The employer's side is finding out whether a candidate can do the job, will do it, and will work well with the team. The candidate's side is finding out whether the role and the company are right for them. When both sides do this well, the interview produces information that is otherwise unavailable: how a person reasons under pressure, how they communicate about work they've done, and whether they will thrive in this particular environment.</p>

<p>This article covers every common interview format and what each one is for, how to design questions that produce useful answers, how to score what you hear, how to recognize and reduce bias, how to give feedback, and how to run a process that works for the candidates going through it as much as it works for you.</p>

<h2>How the interview process fits together</h2>

<p>A hiring process typically moves a candidate from application to shortlist to interview rounds to decision. The interview portion usually spans several formats, each designed to answer a different question. A phone screening answers whether a candidate is worth bringing in. A behavioral interview answers how they've handled situations that will come up again in the new role. A technical interview answers whether they can do the specific work. A final interview answers whether this is the person the whole organization can commit to hiring. Running each format for its own purpose — rather than improvising the same kind of conversation at every stage — is what turns a series of meetings into a reliable process.</p>

<p>The formats below are the ones you'll encounter across most hiring processes. The right combination depends on the role, the seniority of the position, and how much information the team already has from the application and any assessments. For more on how interviews sit within the broader hiring sequence, our article on <a href="/blog/talent-acquisition">talent acquisition</a> covers the full arc from workforce planning to onboarding.</p>

<h2>Phone and video screenings</h2>

<p>A phone screening is a brief call — sometimes as short as ten minutes — in which an employer verifies basic qualifications, clarifies outstanding questions from the application, or confirms the candidate's continued interest, with the goal of building a shortlist before anyone invests more time. It is not a full interview, and it should not be run like one. Its job is to filter efficiently so that the in-depth conversations that follow involve only candidates who genuinely fit the role.</p>

<p>Good screening questions cover whether the candidate's experience matches what the role requires, whether their availability and salary expectations are compatible with the position, and whether their interest in the work is genuine. When answers are vague at the screening stage, brief follow-up questions — "Can you say more about how you used that?" or "What drew you to that kind of work?" — can tell you quickly whether the application reflects real experience or surface-level match.</p>

<p>A live video interview follows the same dynamics as an in-person conversation, with the added logistical advantage of not requiring either party to travel. One-way video interviews, in which candidates record answers to pre-recorded questions on their own schedule, are increasingly common at the screening stage because they let hiring teams review responses outside of real-time constraints. They also create a record every evaluator can review independently, which helps with consistency. Whatever the format, share what candidates can expect beforehand — who they're speaking with, how long the call will take, and what the next steps are.</p>

<h2>Structured versus unstructured interviewing</h2>

<p>The difference between structured and unstructured interviewing matters for every format that follows. A structured interview standardizes the questions and evaluation: each candidate for the same role receives the same questions in the same order and is scored against the same criteria. An unstructured interview is a relatively open conversation with each candidate, aimed at getting a general sense of the person. Most teams land somewhere between the two — a rough list of questions, variable follow-ups, no formal scoring — which produces some of the drawbacks of each.</p>

<p>ERE, citing research from Google's re:Work initiative, reports that <a href="https://www.ere.net/why-structured-interviews-are-the-best-way-to-hire/">structured interviews predict job performance with 26% accuracy</a>, matching general cognitive ability tests as the second-strongest available predictor. Work sample tests come in just above that, at 29%. Gut-feel, unstructured interviewing falls well below both. And among organizations with the best-rated candidate experiences, <a href="https://www.ere.net/articles/5-research-backed-ways-to-improve-your-interviews/">seven of the top ten conduct structured interviews</a>. The discipline of standardizing questions and scoring is part of what makes those experiences good — not just for the employer's data quality, but for candidates who get a process that feels fair and prepared.</p>

<p>The case for unstructured interviewing is that it allows the interviewer to follow unexpected threads and have a genuine exchange. That value is real. The best structured interviews leave room for real conversation within the framework, rather than treating it as a rigid script. The goal is consistency in what you're measuring, not rigidity in how you talk.</p>

<h2>Behavioral interviews</h2>

<p>Behavioral interviews focus on how candidates have acted in real situations in their previous jobs. The underlying principle is that past behavior can predict future behavior: how someone handled a conflict with a client two years ago is more informative than how they say they would handle one today. Questions begin with prompts like "Tell me about a time when…" or "Give me an example of a situation where you…."</p>

<p>The STAR method — Situation, Task, Action, Result — gives candidates a structure for answering these questions and gives interviewers a consistent framework for evaluating responses. When an answer stays at the level of "we" rather than "I," or describes what the team did without naming what the candidate specifically contributed, a follow-up probe brings it back to the individual: "What specifically did you do in that situation?" or "What would you handle differently today?" These are among the most useful questions in any interview, because they test whether the candidate can reflect on their own performance honestly.</p>

<p>Behavioral interviews are most effective when the competencies being assessed are defined before the first candidate arrives. For a structured set of questions organized by competency — problem solving, judgment, prioritization, ownership, leadership, and adaptability — see our article on <a href="/blog/unique-interview-questions">unique interview questions that reveal how candidates think</a>.</p>

<h2>Situational interviews</h2>

<p>Situational questions present candidates with a hypothetical scenario — often one they'd face in the new role — and ask how they'd respond. Starters include "What would you do if…" and "How would you handle…." They test reasoning and judgment in the moment, which makes them useful when the candidate has limited directly applicable experience. A recent graduate interviewing for a management role, for example, may not have fired anyone yet, but can be asked how they would approach a performance issue on their team.</p>

<p>The trade-off with situational questions is that the answers describe intent, not action. A candidate can describe a thoughtful, well-structured response without having done anything similar. That doesn't make the information worthless — it reveals how someone thinks through a problem — but it shouldn't carry the weight that behavioral evidence from real experience does. Combining situational and behavioral questions in the same interview covers both what a candidate thinks and what they've actually done, giving you a more complete picture.</p>

<h2>Technical interviews</h2>

<p>Technical interviews ask candidates to solve problems live, complete coding challenges, or walk through case analyses using tools and frameworks specific to the job. They are most common in engineering, data, and finance roles, and they tend to be the longest individual interview format, running anywhere from one to three hours.</p>

<p>The key to a technical interview that produces useful signal is building rubrics around specific, measurable competencies — problem decomposition, code quality, communication, testing awareness, and trade-off reasoning — rather than general impressions. Questions should be open-ended and have more than one valid approach, so the interviewer can assess reasoning rather than whether the candidate memorized a particular answer. The best technical interview questions reveal how a candidate thinks about edge cases, not just whether they arrived at a correct solution.</p>

<p>Level questions to seniority. Junior candidates get mechanics questions focused on fundamentals. Senior candidates get trade-off and architectural judgment questions, where the right answer depends on context and there is no single correct response. After a take-home assessment or coding exercise, hold a debrief conversation. The debrief often surfaces more than the written work alone — it reveals how clearly candidates can explain their decisions, whether they considered alternatives, and how they respond to questions about their own work. Ask them to explain a technical concept as if speaking to someone outside their field. The ability to calibrate language for the audience is a signal about communication skills that the code itself cannot give you.</p>

<p>As with behavioral interviews, consistency is what makes comparison possible. Asking every candidate for the same role the same core questions, in the same order, with the same time budget, is the only way to compare two candidates fairly.</p>

<h2>Case interviews</h2>

<p>In a case interview, the candidate receives a hypothetical or real business problem to analyze and must propose and explain a solution. Case interviews are most common in consulting and strategy roles, where structured problem-solving under pressure is part of the daily work. For roles where creative thinking and analysis are central, <a href="/blog/problem-solving-interview-questions">problem-solving interview questions</a> can supplement or replace the case format with something more conversational.</p>

<p>Evaluating a case interview requires deciding in advance what a good answer looks like. Not because there is one correct answer — in most cases there isn't — but because the evaluators need to agree on what they're assessing before the conversation begins. Are they looking at how the candidate structures the problem? How they handle ambiguous information? Whether they communicate clearly under pressure? Without agreed criteria, different evaluators will walk away from the same case with incompatible conclusions.</p>

<h2>Panel interviews</h2>

<p>A panel interview involves two or more interviewers — often including the hiring manager, a potential peer, and an HR representative — sitting together to question a single candidate. The U.S. Department of Labor notes that panel interviews give multiple stakeholders ownership of the hiring decision and can eliminate the need for separate interview rounds. They also create a natural check on individual bias: when several people hear the same answer at the same time, impressions are harder to revise in retrospect.</p>

<p>Running a panel well requires preparation. Assign each member specific questions in advance so the interview has a logical flow rather than overlapping or redundant questions. Have everyone review the resume and job description before the interview, not during it. After the interview, have the most junior panelist share their assessment first so the senior opinions in the room don't set the tone before others have committed to a view. Submit individual scorecards independently before the debrief conversation, so the group is discussing evidence rather than constructing a consensus around whoever spoke first.</p>

<h2>Group interviews</h2>

<p>In a group interview, several interviewers from the hiring organization meet simultaneously with a candidate, with some panel members present only to observe rather than to ask questions. Group interviews are less common than panel interviews. They are most useful when the ability to present to multiple stakeholders, read a room, or perform effectively under observation is a central requirement of the role itself — a sales or client-facing position, for example, where the interview setting approximates real work conditions.</p>

<p>When using a group format, brief the observers in advance on what they're watching for and give them a scoring rubric so their observations can be compared afterward. An observer who attends without a framework will walk away with impressions rather than assessable evidence, which is a poor return on the time invested.</p>

<h2>Final interviews</h2>

<p>A final interview usually involves a senior decision-maker — a founder, an executive, or the hiring manager's manager — who assesses fit at a higher level than the earlier rounds. At this stage, technical qualifications are largely established. The final interview answers whether this is the specific person the organization wants to commit to, and it gives the candidate a genuine opportunity to meet the leadership they would be working under and to ask questions that earlier-stage interviewers may not have been able to answer.</p>

<p>Final interviews carry a particular responsibility toward the candidate. By this point, the candidate has invested significant time in the process — preparing for earlier rounds, completing assessments, and rearranging their schedule across multiple conversations. The final interviewer should arrive as prepared as anyone earlier in the process: having read the resume, reviewed the feedback from prior rounds, and thought carefully about what the conversation is meant to resolve.</p>

<h2>Designing interview questions that produce useful answers</h2>

<p>The questions you ask determine what you learn. Generic questions produce generic answers. Questions that begin with "Tell me about yourself" or "What is your greatest weakness?" are so widely rehearsed that the answers reveal little about how a candidate actually works. The question design stage is where the interview either becomes a reliable evaluation tool or remains an exercise in confirmation of first impressions.</p>

<p>Effective interview questions share a few qualities. They are rooted in real experience rather than hypotheticals alone. They have more than one valid answer, so the interviewer can assess reasoning rather than recall. They connect directly to what the role requires. If a customer-facing role demands patience under pressure, the question should probe for a real situation in which the candidate demonstrated that — not for a general statement about their approach to difficult customers. And they are phrased simply enough that the candidate can answer the question asked rather than the question they expected to be asked.</p>

<p>Question design also means knowing what not to ask. Any question that touches on arrest record, military status, pregnancy, age, religion, marital status, or other protected characteristics has no place in an interview. These questions expose the organization to legal risk. More directly, they leave candidates uncertain about whether a casual exchange about their personal life shaped a decision that was supposed to be about their qualifications.</p>

<h2>Scoring what you hear</h2>

<p>An interview without a scoring method produces opinions, not evidence. When each interviewer walks away with a different impression and no common framework for comparing notes, the debrief becomes a negotiation between strong personalities rather than a review of what candidates demonstrated. The person who speaks most confidently tends to carry the most weight, regardless of whether their assessment is the most accurate.</p>

<p>A <a href="/blog/behavioral-interview-scoring-matrix">behavioral interview scoring matrix</a> gives each interviewer a grid that lists the competencies the role requires and describes — in observable behavioral terms — what a weak, adequate, and strong answer looks like for each. Behaviorally anchored rating scales (BARS) go further, specifying what each score looks like in plain, specific behavior so that two interviewers rating the same answer have a much better chance of arriving at the same score. A useful scorecard includes space for direct quotes and specific examples, not just numerical scores, so the debrief has something concrete to discuss rather than just competing ratings.</p>

<p>Gartner research, <a href="https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/11-must-ask-behavioral-interview-questions/">as reported by Glassdoor</a>, found that 39% of HR leaders say structured interviews help reduce bias. The scoring method is a large part of why. When scores are recorded independently before the group debrief, each interviewer's judgment is on record and harder to revise in response to social pressure from colleagues.</p>

<h2>Interview bias</h2>

<p>Interview bias is any preference or mental shortcut that shapes how an interviewer judges a candidate on factors unrelated to their ability to do the job. Most of these judgments happen quickly and feel like sound instinct to the person making them — which is exactly what makes bias difficult to address. It rarely feels like bias from the inside.</p>

<p>The types that appear most often in hiring include first impression bias, the halo and horns effect, affinity bias, confirmation bias, the contrast effect, recency and primacy bias, stereotype bias, and groupthink. <a href="/blog/first-impression-bias">First impression bias</a> causes people to form a quick and incomplete impression of someone based on what they see and hear in the first few seconds of meeting — before the candidate has answered a single substantive question. That impression tends to shape everything that follows. The halo effect carries a strong first impression forward so that subsequent answers look better than they are. The horns effect works in the opposite direction, making a poor first impression difficult to overcome even with strong answers.</p>

<p>Affinity bias pulls interviewers toward candidates who remind them of themselves. Confirmation bias leads interviewers to seek out information that confirms the impression they formed early, and to discount information that contradicts it. These two biases often work together: an interviewer who likes a candidate will ask easier follow-ups and interpret vague answers charitably, without noticing either tendency. For a full breakdown of each bias type and where it enters the process, see our article on <a href="/blog/interview-bias">interview bias</a>.</p>

<p>The practical fixes for bias are structural. Standardized questions make it harder for interviewers to follow different lines of inquiry. Independent scoring before the debrief prevents the most confident voice in the room from pulling everyone else's ratings. Diverse panels reduce the risk that affinity bias runs unchecked. Focusing evaluation criteria on what a candidate will add to the team — rather than how well they fit the existing culture — removes at least some of the room for interviewers to favor candidates who remind them of colleagues they already like.</p>

<h2>Pre-employment assessments alongside the interview</h2>

<p>Interviews rarely stand alone in a well-designed hiring process. Pre-employment assessments — skills tests, work samples, structured take-home projects — give the team information about what a candidate can actually do, separate from how they perform in a conversation. SHRM's 2024 Talent Trends Report found that <a href="https://www.shrm.org/content/dam/en/shrm/research/2024-talent-trends-research-overall-findings.pdf">78% of organizations report that pre-employment assessments used during the interview process have improved quality of hire</a>. Work sample tests — asking a candidate to do a version of the actual work — are among the strongest predictors of job performance available.</p>

<p>Assessments work best when they are relevant to the role and introduced at the right point in the process. A demanding take-home placed early in the process, before a candidate has had any real contact with the team or any sense of whether the role is worth their time, risks losing strong applicants who have other options. After a screening call, when candidates are more engaged and have a clearer picture of the opportunity, completion rates tend to be higher and the work more representative of what the person can actually do.</p>

<h2>The candidate experience during interviews</h2>

<p>How a candidate experiences the interview process shapes whether they accept an offer if one comes, and what they tell other people about the company afterward. <a href="https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/improve-interview-process/">Research from Aptitude Research, cited by Glassdoor</a>, found that 82% of recruiters have lost a top candidate because of a poor interview experience. Losing a candidate you have already screened, interviewed, and selected is expensive in time and often costly in the quality of the eventual hire.</p>

<p>SHRM reports that <a href="https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/interview-critical-part-hiring-process-candidates-say">68% of candidates say the interview process positively or negatively affects their decision to accept a job offer</a>. That figure is worth sitting with. The interview is not just an evaluation tool for the employer. It is the primary experience through which candidates decide whether they want to work for this company — and through which they form an impression that they carry out into the world regardless of the outcome.</p>

<p>The basics are worth getting right. Share an itinerary before the interview so candidates know who they're meeting and in what order. Start on time. Disclose the salary range early, so candidates aren't spending multiple interview rounds on a role that won't work financially. Finish the full interview with every candidate regardless of early impressions — cutting it short based on disinterest invites reputational risk. Reserve at least ten minutes at the end for candidate questions. A candidate who cannot ask questions cannot assess fit either, and an employer who does not allow them to signals that mutual assessment goes only one way.</p>

<h2>Giving feedback after the interview</h2>

<p>Interview feedback is the final stage of the hiring process to close the interview in a respectful manner. It is usually given to candidates who were not selected, explaining why and offering actionable guidance for improvement. Most companies skip it entirely, and candidates notice.</p>

<p>Feedback given well is specific: it names the gap between what the candidate demonstrated and what the role required, and it gives the candidate something they can act on. Generic responses — "We decided to move forward with other candidates" — are of little value to the person receiving them. Detailed, honest feedback demonstrates respect and, as a secondary effect, leaves the candidate with a better impression of the company than a silence or a form letter would. Some of them will apply again for future roles, refer colleagues, or become customers. For scripts that work across the most common rejection scenarios, see our article on <a href="/blog/interview-feedback-examples">interview feedback examples</a>. For the rejection email itself, see our article on <a href="/blog/job-rejection-email">how to write personalized job rejection emails</a>.</p>

<p>Recording feedback in a consistent format matters as much as giving it. When interviewers submit written reviews through a shared template, the team builds a record of why each candidate advanced or was rejected. That record is useful for the debrief, useful if a decision is later questioned, and useful for identifying patterns in the process — where the team is consistently losing good candidates, or where the criteria being applied don't match the requirements in the job description.</p>

<h2>The trade-off between structure and flexibility</h2>

<p>Structured interviewing is not the only way to run an interview, and hiring teams sometimes resist it because it can feel constraining. Requiring every interviewer to ask the same questions in the same order removes the space for a conversation that develops naturally. Some candidates — particularly those interviewing for creative, senior, or relationship-driven roles — may respond better to a more open exchange than to a question list that feels like a form being filled out.</p>

<p>The value of unstructured interviewing is that it allows an interviewer to follow unexpected threads, respond to what the candidate actually says, and get a richer read on who the person is. The cost is that the information it produces is harder to compare across candidates and more vulnerable to the biases described above. The research is consistent: for most roles and most hiring teams, a structured approach produces better decisions. The practical middle ground is to standardize the questions and the scoring while giving interviewers the latitude to probe and follow up within that frame. That is the structure of a good conversation, not the absence of one.</p>

<h2>Put this into practice</h2>

<p>Running a disciplined interview process — from a structured screening call through scored debriefs to prompt, specific feedback — means you get information you can actually use. Candidates experience the company at its best, decisions are easier to make and to explain, and the person hired is more likely to be the right one.</p>
