<h2>What an interview is — and what it is for</h2>
<p>Interviewing is the stage of hiring where both sides finally get to hear from each other. Before the interview, you have a resume, a cover letter, and maybe an application form. After it, you have something harder to fake: a real conversation. An interview gives you the chance to assess a candidate's judgment, communication, and fit for the role in ways that no written document can.</p>
<p>That said, an interview is not just a vetting tool. The candidate is evaluating you and your company at the same time. According to Glassdoor research, 78% of candidates say the overall experience signals how a company values its people. The interview is often the most direct signal they will get.</p>
<p>The <a href="/blog/hiring-process-steps">hiring process</a> has many steps, but most hiring managers and founders agree that the interview is the most consequential one. It is where good processes separate good candidates from great ones — and where poor processes lose great candidates to employers who run theirs better.</p>

<h2>The types of interviews and what each one does</h2>
<p>Not all interviews serve the same purpose, and different roles call for different formats. Using the right format at the right stage keeps the process efficient and gives you better signal from each conversation.</p>

<h3>Phone screening</h3>
<p>A phone screening is a brief call — sometimes as short as 10 minutes — used to verify basic qualifications, clarify questions from the application, or confirm that a candidate is still interested. Its purpose is to build a shortlist before committing to a full interview. Phone screenings save time for both sides: candidates who clearly aren't a fit are identified early, and candidates who are ready to move forward get there faster. Many teams use a short set of standard screening questions to keep this stage consistent.</p>

<h3>Video interview</h3>
<p>A live video interview works much like an in-person interview, with an added layer of flexibility: neither party needs to be in the same city. A one-way video interview uses a platform that presents pre-recorded questions, which the candidate records their responses to on their own time. Live video interviews are now the standard first full interview for most remote and hybrid roles. One-way formats are more common in high-volume hiring, where watching every candidate answer the same questions in real time isn't practical.</p>

<h3>Behavioral interview</h3>
<p>Behavioral interviews focus on how candidates have acted in real situations. The underlying logic is that past behavior is a reliable predictor of future behavior. Questions typically begin with "Tell me about a time when…" or "Give me an example of when you…." A candidate who describes a real situation, the action they took, and the result of that action gives you far more useful information than one who answers a hypothetical in the abstract. Behavioral questions work well across nearly every role and experience level.</p>

<h3>Situational interview</h3>
<p>Situational questions present a hypothetical scenario and ask what the candidate would do. Where behavioral questions look backward, situational questions look forward: "What would you do if a client escalated a complaint and your manager was unavailable?" They test reasoning and judgment, particularly for candidates who may not have direct prior experience in the role. The tradeoff is that situational answers are easier to rehearse and may reflect what a candidate knows they should say rather than what they would actually do.</p>

<h3>Technical interview</h3>
<p>Technical interviews are common in engineering, data, and finance roles. They ask candidates to solve problems live, complete coding challenges, or walk through case analyses using the tools and frameworks specific to the job. According to Glassdoor, technical interviews typically run one to three hours — the longest individual format. The best technical interviews test reasoning and communication alongside technical knowledge, not just the ability to recall syntax or formulas. The questions should map directly to what the person will be doing in the first few months of the role.</p>

<h3>Case interview</h3>
<p>In a case interview, the candidate receives a hypothetical or real business problem and is asked to analyze it and propose a solution. The interviewer is evaluating structure, analytical thinking, and communication — not just whether the candidate lands on the right answer. Case interviews are most common in consulting and strategy roles. They work best when the problem is realistic enough to reflect actual work and open-ended enough to reveal how the candidate thinks rather than what they have memorized.</p>

<h3>Panel interview</h3>
<p>A panel interview brings two or more interviewers together to question a single candidate. The panel often includes representatives from HR, the hiring team, and other stakeholders. According to the U.S. Department of Labor, panel interviews give multiple people ownership of the hiring decision and can eliminate the need for several separate rounds. They are more efficient than scheduling five one-on-one sessions, and when the panel is diverse in background and perspective, they can reduce the influence of any single interviewer's bias.</p>

<h3>Group interview</h3>
<p>In a group interview, several interviewers meet simultaneously with the candidate, with some panel members present only to observe. SHRM notes this format is used when the team wants different observers to assess different things — for example, one person focused on how the candidate handles pressure and another on how they explain their reasoning. Group interviews require coordination among panel members in advance, particularly around who asks what, to avoid overlap or long silences.</p>

<h3>Final interview</h3>
<p>The final interview is typically the last stage before an offer. It often involves a more senior decision-maker — a founder, VP, or department head — who may not have been part of earlier rounds. The goal at this stage is usually confirmation rather than discovery: the candidate has already cleared the technical, behavioral, and team-fit assessments, and the final conversation is about mutual alignment on expectations, culture, and direction. It is also the candidate's last chance to ask the questions that matter most to them.</p>

<h2>Structured vs. unstructured interviewing</h2>
<p>Every interview falls somewhere on a spectrum between fully structured and fully unstructured. Understanding where your interviews sit on that spectrum — and what the difference costs you — is one of the most important decisions you can make about your hiring process.</p>
<p>A structured interview standardizes the questions and evaluation: each candidate for the same role receives the same questions in the same order and is scored against the same criteria. An unstructured interview is a relatively open conversation that varies by candidate, aimed at getting a feel for the person rather than evaluating against fixed criteria.</p>
<p>ERE, citing research from Google's re:Work, reports that structured interviews have 26% predictive power for job performance — tied with general cognitive ability tests as the second-best predictor, behind work sample tests at 29%. Unstructured interviews, by comparison, produce results that are only slightly better than chance. And according to a Gartner survey cited by Glassdoor, 39% of HR leaders say structured interviews help reduce bias. When you ask every candidate the same questions, you remove one of the most common sources of inconsistency: the interviewer's mood, interests, and assumptions on a given day.</p>
<p>Structured interviewing does require more preparation. You need to design the questions in advance, build a scoring rubric, and brief everyone on the panel before the interview begins — not during it. But that preparation pays off at the decision stage, when you have scores and notes rather than competing gut feelings.</p>

<h2>Designing interview questions that give you useful signal</h2>
<p>The questions you ask are the most direct influence you have on the quality of information you gather. Generic questions get generic answers. The candidate who has interviewed at ten companies this month has already rehearsed "What is your greatest weakness?" and "Where do you see yourself in five years?" The answers tell you little about how that person actually works.</p>
<p>Effective interview questions share a few qualities. They are open-ended, which means they require more than a yes or no. They are rooted in real situations rather than hypotheticals wherever possible. And they are tied to the specific competencies the role requires — problem solving, ownership, communication, judgment, or whatever the job actually demands. The best technical interview questions, as HackerRank notes, are open-ended, have multiple valid approaches, and reveal reasoning rather than recall.</p>
<p>Behavioral questions work well across roles because they ask for evidence. <a href="/blog/unique-interview-questions">Unique interview questions</a> that go beyond the standard set can surface how candidates think, prioritize, and handle pressure in ways that rehearsed answers cannot. <a href="/blog/problem-solving-interview-questions">Problem-solving interview questions</a> are especially useful for roles that require analytical thinking, creativity, or the ability to work through ambiguity without close supervision.</p>
<p>Whatever questions you use, build your bank before candidates arrive. Scoring criteria defined after a strong candidate impresses you are not criteria — they are rationalizations.</p>

<h2>How to use the STAR method and follow-up probing</h2>
<p>When asking behavioral questions, give candidates a structure for their answers. The STAR method — Situation, Task, Action, Result — helps candidates organize their thinking and helps you evaluate what they give you. The situation provides context, the task defines what needed to happen, the action describes what the candidate did, and the result shows the outcome. A complete STAR answer tells you something real about how that person handled a specific challenge.</p>
<p>Many candidates, even strong ones, give incomplete answers. They describe the situation clearly and then jump to the result without explaining what they specifically did. When that happens, probe: "What specifically did you do in that situation?" or "What would you handle differently today?" Follow-up questions separate the candidates who have deep experience from the ones who know how to frame surface-level answers convincingly.</p>
<p>The 80/20 rule is a useful reference point here: aim for the candidate to carry roughly 80% of the conversation. Silence after a question is not a problem. Give the candidate time to think before rephrasing or jumping in.</p>

<h2>Scoring and the interview scorecard</h2>
<p>The interview is only as useful as the record you keep of it. Without a consistent scoring method, your debrief becomes a battle of impressions. The interviewer who liked a candidate the most tends to win, and the person who liked them least is often the only one in the room with a legitimate concern.</p>
<p>An interview scorecard lists predetermined competencies and asks each interviewer to rate the candidate against them, immediately after the interview while the conversation is fresh. A <a href="/blog/behavioral-interview-scoring-matrix">behavioral interview scoring matrix</a> takes this further by defining what a strong, acceptable, and weak answer looks like for each competency — removing much of the subjectivity from the rating process.</p>
<p>A useful scorecard includes the competencies relevant to the role, a consistent rating scale, space for specific examples and direct quotes from the interview, and an overall recommendation. The Predictive Index recommends building the scorecard before candidates arrive. The competencies should map to the role's real requirements — the things that will determine whether this hire succeeds six months in.</p>
<p>For panel interviews, have each interviewer submit their scorecard independently before the group debrief. When the most senior person in the room speaks first, everyone else's scores tend to shift toward theirs — a pattern known as groupthink. SocialTalent recommends having the most junior interviewer speak first so that disagreement gets voiced before senior opinions set the tone.</p>

<h2>Interview bias and how it distorts your decisions</h2>
<p>Bias in interviews is not a character flaw. It is a structural problem. Interviewers make rapid, unconscious judgments about candidates — often within the first few seconds of meeting — that then shape everything that follows. <a href="/blog/interview-bias">Interview bias</a> is any preference or mental shortcut that shapes how an interviewer judges a candidate on factors unrelated to their ability to do the job.</p>
<p>The types that show up most often include first impression bias, the halo and horns effect, affinity bias, confirmation bias, the contrast effect, recency and primacy bias, stereotype bias, and groupthink. Each enters the process at a different point. First impression bias often locks in within seconds of meeting. Confirmation bias operates throughout the interview, as the interviewer unconsciously looks for evidence that confirms their initial read. Recency bias affects how interviewers score candidates they met earlier in the day, whose answers have faded compared to the most recent candidate.</p>
<p><a href="/blog/first-impression-bias">First impression bias</a> deserves particular attention because it triggers the others. When an interviewer forms a strong first impression — positive or negative — they are primed to interpret subsequent answers through that lens. The halo effect follows a good first impression: everything the candidate says sounds better than it might otherwise. The horns effect follows a bad one.</p>
<p>Structured interviewing reduces bias because it constrains the interview to the same questions, the same order, and the same scoring criteria across candidates. It doesn't eliminate bias entirely, but it narrows the window for it. Diverse panels help further, as does the practice of blind resume review before the interview stage. SHRM's 2024 Talent Trends Report found that 78% of organizations using pre-employment assessments during the interview process reported improved quality of hire — a signal that adding objective data points reduces reliance on subjective impression.</p>

<h2>What makes a great interview experience for the candidate</h2>
<p>The candidate's experience of your interview process directly affects whether they accept an offer — and whether they tell others about the company afterward. According to SHRM, 68% of candidates say a company's hiring process positively or negatively impacts their decision to accept a job offer. And Glassdoor data shows that 82% of recruiters have lost a top candidate because of a poor interview experience.</p>
<p>Several practices consistently improve the experience. Share an interview agenda with candidates beforehand so they know what to expect — who they will meet, how long it will take, and what format it will follow. Consider sending interview questions 24 to 48 hours in advance for roles where preparation adds to the quality of the conversation. Reserve at least ten minutes at the end of every interview for the candidate's questions. And complete the interview with every candidate regardless of how the first few minutes go; cutting an interview short sends a signal that is hard to walk back.</p>
<p>The candidate's perception of your process is also your company's first real expression of its culture. A disorganized, inconsistent, or cold interview process tells the candidate exactly what working there will feel like.</p>

<h2>Giving feedback after the interview</h2>
<p>Most companies do not give candidates feedback after a rejection. The ones that do stand out — both to the candidates they reject and to the broader talent market that hears about those experiences.</p>
<p>Interview feedback, as defined in the fuller treatment on this site, is the final stage of the hiring process and closes it in a respectful way. <a href="/blog/interview-feedback-examples">Interview feedback</a> is usually given to candidates who were not selected, explaining the decision and offering specific guidance they can act on. Generic feedback — "We went with someone whose experience was a closer match" — delivers almost no value. Specific feedback — "Your technical depth was strong, but we needed someone with direct experience managing a distributed team, which came through clearly in other conversations" — is both respectful and genuinely useful to the candidate.</p>
<p>Feedback should be delivered promptly. The longer a candidate waits after the final round, the worse the experience becomes regardless of the content of the message. It should be honest without being harsh, and specific without revealing confidential information about other candidates or internal deliberations.</p>

<h2>Rejection emails and closing the loop</h2>
<p>Every candidate who does not receive an offer deserves a response. That includes candidates rejected after a phone screening, after an early-round interview, and after the final round. Silence is the worst outcome — for the candidate, and for your company's reputation with the people who applied.</p>
<p>The <a href="/blog/job-rejection-email">rejection email</a> does not need to be long, but it needs to be sent. At the screening stage, a brief note is enough. At the final interview stage, a more personal message is appropriate — especially if the candidate invested significant time and preparation in the process. The tone should be warm, the decision clear, and the door left open where it honestly can be.</p>
<p>Candidates who receive a thoughtful rejection are more likely to apply again, to refer others, and to remain favorable toward the company. The ones who hear nothing are not.</p>

<h2>Common challenges and how to handle them</h2>
<p>Even well-designed interview processes run into predictable problems. Knowing them in advance makes them easier to manage.</p>

<h3>Inconsistency across interviewers</h3>
<p>When different interviewers ask different questions and score on different criteria, you end up with feedback that cannot be compared. The fix is a structured format with a shared scorecard, assigned questions per interviewer, and a brief before the interview — not during it — where everyone reviews the resume, job description, and screening notes.</p>

<h3>Vague or rehearsed answers</h3>
<p>Some candidates are skilled at sounding specific without being specific. They give answers that follow the STAR structure but replace real detail with plausible-sounding generalities. The fix is probing: follow up with "What specifically did you do?" or "Walk me through the exact steps you took." A candidate with real experience will be able to go deeper. One who is rehearsing a framework will often stall.</p>

<h3>Illegal questions</h3>
<p>Questions about protected characteristics — age, religion, pregnancy, marital status, military status, arrest record, national origin, and others — create legal risk and damage trust with candidates. The University of Wisconsin HR office notes that all questions must be consistent across candidates and free of any that touch on these areas. The practical rule is to keep every question tied to the requirements of the role. If the answer to a question wouldn't change your hiring decision, the question probably shouldn't be asked.</p>

<h3>Interview fatigue and scheduling gaps</h3>
<p>When candidates move through too many rounds over too long a timeline, they disengage — and the best candidates, who often have several processes running simultaneously, accept another offer. The goal is a process that is thorough enough to be reliable and fast enough to remain competitive. SHRM reports that the average time to fill an open role was 41 days in 2024, down from 48 the year before. Each unnecessary round adds to that number.</p>

<h2>The case for structured interviewing — and its limits</h2>
<p>Structured interviewing is the most defensible approach to candidate evaluation available. The research supports it, the bias-reduction benefits are real, and the ability to compare candidates on the same criteria makes the decision-making conversation more productive. For most roles and most teams, it is the right foundation.</p>
<p>That said, it has limits. A structured interview captures what a candidate says in a formal, time-limited setting. It does not capture how they perform over weeks, how they handle the unexpected, or how they build relationships with colleagues. Past behavior predicts future behavior — but only imperfectly. Some candidates present better than they perform. Others perform far better than they present.</p>
<p>These limits are not a reason to abandon structure. They are a reason to treat the interview as one input among several — alongside work samples, reference conversations, and probationary observation when those are available.</p>

<h2>Run a process every candidate remembers well</h2>
<p>When you interview with consistency and care, you give every candidate a fair chance to show you what they can do. You collect the kind of evidence that holds up in a debrief, reduces the influence of bias, and leads to decisions you can explain and stand behind. That is what a well-run interview process produces: not just a hire, but a defensible one.</p>

## Final

<p>If you find all the different types of interviews confusing, or feel like every interview is a blur, this guide will make everything about interviewing clearer. An interview is a meeting between a candidate and one or more persons in which the employer, or the employer's representatives, asks a series of questions. What sounds like a simple exchange carries a great deal of weight in the hiring process, and the way you run it determines how much useful information you actually get out of it.</p>

<p>At its most useful, an interview is a structured evaluation of whether a candidate can do the job, will do it, and will work well with the people already doing it. The candidate's answers — when you ask the right questions and score them consistently — give you evidence. The interview also works in the other direction: the candidate is assessing you, your company, and whether the role is worth pursuing. According to SHRM, <a href="https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/interview-critical-part-hiring-process-candidates-say">68% of candidates say a company's hiring process positively or negatively impacts their decision to accept a job offer</a>. The interview is usually where that impression forms.</p>

<p>This article covers the main interview formats and what each one is for, how to design questions that give you useful signal, how to score candidates consistently, where bias enters the process and how to reduce it, what a strong candidate experience looks like, and how to close the loop with feedback and rejection communication.</p>

<h2>What an interview is for</h2>

<p>The interview sits at the center of the <a href="/blog/hiring-process-steps">hiring process</a> because it is the moment when you can assess things a resume cannot show: how someone reasons through a problem, how they respond under pressure, how they communicate with people who are evaluating them. A resume tells you what a candidate has done. An interview tells you something about how and why.</p>

<p>That is not the whole picture. An interview is a formal, time-limited setting, and some candidates are far better at interviews than they are at the work. Others are the reverse. A well-designed interview process reduces but does not eliminate that gap, by grounding questions in real situations, scoring answers against defined criteria, and gathering input from more than one person. The goal is not a flawless prediction of job performance — it is a more reliable one than you would get from a conversation with no structure at all.</p>

<p>The candidate's side of the conversation matters as much as yours. They are deciding whether to accept an offer if one comes, whether to refer colleagues to your open roles, and what they will say about your company afterward. The care you bring to interviewing communicates something real about how your company operates.</p>

<h2>The interview formats and what each one is for</h2>

<p>Different formats serve different purposes in the hiring process. The right one depends on the stage you are at, the role you are hiring for, and the information you still need to gather. Here is how each format works and where it fits.</p>

<h3>Phone screening</h3>

<p>A phone screening is a brief call — sometimes as short as 10 minutes, according to the U.S. Department of Labor — used to verify basic qualifications, clarify questions from the application, or confirm that a candidate is still interested and available. Its primary purpose is to build a shortlist before committing to a full interview. Done well, a phone screen saves time for everyone: candidates who clearly are not a fit learn that early, and candidates who are ready to move forward get there faster.</p>

<p>The most common mistake at this stage is treating the phone screen as an informal conversation rather than a structured step. Using a consistent set of questions across all screened candidates — covering salary expectations, logistics, and two or three role-relevant qualifications — makes it much easier to compare candidates and justify who moves forward. It also signals to the candidate that your process is organized, which matters for the impression you are making from the first contact.</p>

<h3>Video interview</h3>

<p>A live video interview works much like an in-person interview, with the difference that neither party needs to be in the same location. This format has become the standard first full interview for most remote and hybrid roles, and many teams use it for all first-round interviews regardless of the role's location.</p>

<p>A one-way video interview uses a platform that presents pre-recorded questions, which the candidate records their responses to on their own time. This format is more common in high-volume hiring, where scheduling live conversations with every applicant is not practical. One-way video gives you more candidates faster, but it removes the back-and-forth that lets you probe answers or follow up on something the candidate said. Reserve live video for roles where dialogue matters to the evaluation.</p>

<h3>Behavioral interview</h3>

<p>Behavioral interviews focus on how candidates have acted in real situations at work. The underlying principle is that past behavior reliably predicts future behavior. Questions typically begin with "Tell me about a time when…" or "Give me an example of when you…," and the candidate's answer should describe a specific situation, what they did, and what resulted from it.</p>

<p>This format works well across nearly every role and experience level because it asks for evidence. A candidate who describes a real situation and explains their reasoning in detail is giving you something that is much harder to fake than a hypothetical answer. SHRM notes that behavioral questions are among the most widely used formats precisely because they anchor the conversation in what a candidate has actually done rather than what they think the right answer sounds like. When you pair behavioral questions with a consistent scoring rubric, you have a basis for comparison that holds up in a debrief.</p>

<h3>Situational interview</h3>

<p>Situational questions present a hypothetical scenario and ask what the candidate would do. Where behavioral questions look backward at real experience, situational questions look forward: "What would you do if a client escalated a complaint and your manager was unavailable?" They test reasoning and judgment, and they are especially useful for candidates who are newer to a role or industry and may not have directly relevant experience to draw on.</p>

<p>The tradeoff is that situational answers are easier to rehearse. A candidate who knows the right framework can give a convincing answer without having been in the situation. For that reason, situational questions work best in combination with behavioral ones, not as a replacement. Use them where experience gaps are expected; use behavioral questions where experience is a core requirement.</p>

<h3>Technical interview</h3>

<p>Technical interviews are common in engineering, data, and finance roles. They ask candidates to solve problems live, complete coding challenges, or walk through case analyses using the tools and frameworks specific to the job. Glassdoor notes that technical interviews typically run one to three hours — the longest individual format in most hiring processes.</p>

<p>The best technical interviews test reasoning and communication alongside technical knowledge. HackerRank recommends that the questions you choose be open-ended, have multiple valid approaches, and reveal how the candidate thinks rather than what they can recall. A candidate who works through a problem clearly, asks good clarifying questions, and explains their reasoning as they go is showing you something important about how they will work day to day. Trivia questions that reward memorization, by contrast, tell you little about job performance. Define what success looks like in the first six months of the role before writing a single question, and then make sure the questions map to that answer.</p>

<h3>Case interview</h3>

<p>In a case interview, the candidate receives a hypothetical or real business problem and is asked to analyze it and propose a solution. The interviewer evaluates structure, analytical thinking, and communication — not just whether the candidate reaches the correct answer, because there often is not one. Case interviews are most common in consulting and strategy roles, where the ability to frame a problem clearly and reason through it methodically is itself a core job skill.</p>

<p>Case interviews work best when the problem is realistic enough to reflect actual work in the role and open-ended enough to reveal how the candidate thinks. SHRM notes that case formats give you insight into how candidates draw on knowledge and creative ability under pressure. A candidate who works through uncertainty confidently and explains their reasoning at each step is demonstrating the kind of judgment that is hard to assess with a question-and-answer format alone.</p>

<h3>Panel interview</h3>

<p>A panel interview brings two or more interviewers together to question a single candidate at the same time. The panel typically includes representatives from HR, the hiring team, and other stakeholders who have a legitimate interest in the hire. According to the U.S. Department of Labor, panel interviews give multiple people ownership of the hiring decision and can eliminate the need for several separate one-on-one rounds.</p>

<p>The practical advantages are real. Rather than scheduling five separate conversations, the candidate has one, and the interviewers can hear each other's questions and the candidate's responses in the same session. When the panel is diverse in background and perspective, the format also narrows the window for any single interviewer's biases to dominate. The preparation requirement is higher: each panel member should have assigned questions and should review the candidate's materials before the interview, not during it.</p>

<h3>Group interview</h3>

<p>In a group interview, several interviewers meet simultaneously with the candidate, with some panel members present only to observe. SHRM notes this format allows different observers to focus on different things — one person might be watching how the candidate handles pressure while another is listening for how they explain their reasoning. The debrief that follows benefits from those distinct perspectives.</p>

<p>Group interviews require coordination. If panel members have not agreed on who asks what, the conversation becomes disjointed, with long pauses and overlapping questions that frustrate the candidate. Assign roles clearly in advance: who leads, who observes, and who asks the technical, behavioral, or role-specific questions.</p>

<h3>Final interview</h3>

<p>The final interview is typically the last stage before an offer. It often involves a more senior decision-maker — a founder, VP, or department head — who may not have been part of earlier rounds. By this point, the candidate has cleared the technical, behavioral, and team-fit assessments, and the final conversation is primarily about mutual alignment on expectations, role scope, culture, and direction.</p>

<p>The final interview is also the candidate's last opportunity to ask the questions that matter most to them before deciding whether to accept an offer if one comes. Treat it accordingly: make sure the person leading it has reviewed everything that came before, has a clear sense of what is still unknown about the candidate, and can speak honestly about the role, the company, and what success looks like in the first year.</p>

<h2>Structured versus unstructured interviewing</h2>

<p>Every interview falls somewhere on a spectrum between fully structured and fully unstructured, and where yours sits has a larger effect on your hiring outcomes than most teams realize. A structured interview standardizes the questions and the evaluation: each candidate for the same role receives the same questions in the same order and is scored against the same criteria. An unstructured interview is a relatively open conversation that varies from candidate to candidate, aimed at getting a feel for the person rather than evaluating against fixed criteria.</p>

<p>The research on this is consistent. <a href="https://www.ere.net/why-structured-interviews-are-the-best-way-to-hire/">ERE, citing research from Google's re:Work</a>, reports that structured interviews have 26% predictive power for job performance — tied with general cognitive ability tests as the second-best predictor, behind work sample tests at 29%. Unstructured interviews produce results only marginally better than chance. A Gartner survey found that <a href="https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/11-must-ask-behavioral-interview-questions/">39% of HR leaders say structured interviews help reduce bias</a> — a benefit that follows directly from asking every candidate the same questions rather than letting the conversation drift.</p>

<p>Structured interviewing requires more preparation. You need to design the questions in advance, build a scoring rubric, and brief everyone on the panel before candidates arrive. That preparation pays off at the decision stage, when you have scores and specific notes rather than competing impressions. <a href="https://www.ere.net/articles/5-research-backed-ways-to-improve-your-interviews/">ERE also reports</a> that seven of the ten companies with the highest-rated candidate experiences conduct structured interviews — which suggests that consistency is not just good for accuracy, it is good for how the process feels to candidates.</p>

<h2>Designing questions that give you useful signal</h2>

<p>The questions you ask are the most direct influence you have on the quality of information you gather. Generic questions get generic answers. The candidate who has interviewed at ten companies this month has already rehearsed "What is your greatest weakness?" and knows exactly what a compelling answer sounds like. What they cannot rehearse is a question that asks for a specific situation, a specific decision, and a specific outcome.</p>

<p>Effective interview questions are open-ended, rooted in real situations where possible, and tied to the specific competencies the role requires. For behavioral roles, that means problem solving, ownership, communication, and judgment. For technical roles, it means the ability to reason through a problem, explain a decision, and work through trade-offs. HackerRank recommends choosing questions with multiple valid approaches — the ones that reveal reasoning rather than recall.</p>

<p><a href="/blog/unique-interview-questions">Unique interview questions</a> that go beyond the standard set can surface how candidates think, prioritize, and handle pressure in ways that scripted answers cannot reach. For roles where analytical thinking and creative problem-solving matter, <a href="/blog/problem-solving-interview-questions">problem-solving interview questions</a> give you a window into how a candidate approaches ambiguity when there is no obvious right answer. Whatever questions you use, build your bank before the first candidate arrives. As the Predictive Index puts it, scoring criteria defined after a strong candidate impresses you are not criteria — they are rationalizations.</p>

<h2>The STAR method and how to probe past it</h2>

<p>When asking behavioral questions, give candidates a framework for structuring their answers. The STAR method — Situation, Task, Action, Result — helps candidates organize their thinking and helps you evaluate what they give you. The situation provides context. The task defines what needed to happen. The action describes what the candidate specifically did. The result shows what followed. A complete STAR answer gives you something real to evaluate.</p>

<p>Many candidates, including strong ones, give incomplete answers. They describe the situation clearly and then jump to the result without explaining what they actually did. When that happens, probe: "What specifically did you do in that situation?" or "Walk me through the exact steps you took." A candidate with deep experience will be able to go further. One who is working from a rehearsed framework often cannot. Follow-up questions are where the most useful information frequently lives.</p>

<p>The 80/20 rule is a useful reference point: aim for the candidate to carry roughly 80% of the conversation. Silence after a question is not a problem. The Predictive Index recommends resisting the urge to fill pauses, elaborate on the question, or jump in before the candidate has finished thinking. Give them time before rephrasing.</p>

<h2>Scoring candidates consistently</h2>

<p>The interview is only as useful as the record you keep of it. Without a consistent scoring method, your debrief becomes a contest of impressions — and the interviewer who felt most strongly about a candidate tends to win, regardless of whether their read is the most accurate one.</p>

<p>An interview scorecard lists predetermined competencies and asks each interviewer to rate the candidate against them, immediately after the interview while the conversation is fresh. A <a href="/blog/behavioral-interview-scoring-matrix">behavioral interview scoring matrix</a> takes this further by defining what a strong, acceptable, and weak answer looks like for each competency — removing much of the subjectivity from the rating process. The Predictive Index recommends that a useful scorecard include competencies, a consistent rating scale, space for specific examples and direct quotes, and an overall recommendation. The competencies should map to the requirements of the role — the ones that will determine whether the hire succeeds six months in.</p>

<p>For panel interviews, have each interviewer submit their scorecard independently before the group debrief. When the most senior person in the room speaks first, everyone else's scores tend to shift toward theirs. SocialTalent recommends having the most junior interviewer speak first, so that disagreement gets voiced before senior opinions set the tone.</p>

<h2>Bias and where it enters the process</h2>

<p>Bias in interviews is a structural problem. Interviewers make rapid, unconscious judgments about candidates — often within the first seconds of meeting — that then shape everything that follows. <a href="/blog/interview-bias">Interview bias</a> is any preference or mental shortcut that shapes how an interviewer judges a candidate on factors unrelated to their ability to do the job. Most of these judgments feel, to the person making them, like sound instinct.</p>

<p>The types that appear most often include first impression bias, the halo and horns effect, affinity bias, confirmation bias, the contrast effect, recency and primacy bias, stereotype bias, and groupthink. Each enters the process at a different point. First impression bias locks in within seconds of meeting. Confirmation bias operates throughout the interview, as the interviewer unconsciously looks for evidence to support their initial read. Recency bias depresses scores for candidates interviewed earlier in the day, whose answers have faded compared to the most recent conversation.</p>

<p><a href="/blog/first-impression-bias">First impression bias</a> deserves particular attention because it sets off the others. A strong positive first impression triggers the halo effect, making subsequent answers sound better than they might otherwise. A negative first impression triggers the horns effect, making the candidate seem worse. The interviewer who has formed a strong first impression will often ask questions that confirm it rather than challenge it — which is confirmation bias taking over from first impression bias.</p>

<p>Structured interviewing reduces bias by constraining the interview to the same questions, the same order, and the same scoring criteria across candidates. It does not eliminate bias, but it narrows the window for it considerably. Diverse panels help further. SHRM's 2024 Talent Trends Report found that <a href="https://www.shrm.org/content/dam/en/shrm/research/2024-talent-trends-research-overall-findings.pdf">78% of organizations using pre-employment assessments during the interview process reported improved quality of hire</a> — a signal that adding objective data points reduces reliance on subjective impression.</p>

<h2>What a strong candidate experience looks like</h2>

<p>Candidates form a detailed impression of your company from the way you run your interview process. That impression affects whether they accept an offer, whether they refer others, and what they say about the experience publicly. <a href="https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/interview-critical-part-hiring-process-candidates-say">SHRM reports that 68% of candidates say a company's hiring process positively or negatively impacts their decision to accept a job offer</a>. And Aptitude Research found that <a href="https://www.glassdoor.com/blog/improve-interview-process/">82% of recruiters have lost a top candidate because of a poor interview experience</a>.</p>

<p>Several practices consistently improve the experience. Sharing an interview agenda beforehand — covering who the candidate will meet, how long each stage will take, and what format it will follow — reduces anxiety and signals organization. Reserving at least ten minutes at the end of every interview for the candidate's questions is both respectful and informative: the questions they ask tell you something about how they are thinking about the role. Completing the interview with every candidate regardless of early impressions also matters. The University of Wisconsin HR office notes that cutting an interview short sends a signal the candidate will carry with them, and Forbes points out that it creates reputational risk for the company.</p>

<p>Consider sending interview questions 24 to 48 hours in advance for roles where preparation adds to the quality of the conversation. This practice, recommended by both the University of Wisconsin HR office and HackerRank for technical roles, reduces performance anxiety and tends to produce more substantive answers. It is not about giving candidates the answers — it is about shifting the interview from a test of recall to a test of thinking.</p>

<h2>Where the process breaks down and how to fix it</h2>

<p>Even well-designed interview processes run into predictable problems. Knowing them in advance makes them easier to manage before they cost you a good hire.</p>

<h3>Inconsistency across interviewers</h3>

<p>When different interviewers ask different questions and score on different criteria, the feedback you collect cannot be compared. One interviewer has covered technical depth; another has covered culture; a third has covered everything and nothing in particular. The debrief becomes a conversation about different things rather than a structured evaluation of the same candidate.</p>

<p>The fix is a structured format with a shared scorecard, assigned questions per interviewer, and a brief before the interview — not during it — where everyone reviews the resume, job description, and screening notes. This takes twenty minutes to organize. It saves hours of confused deliberation afterward.</p>

<h3>Vague or rehearsed answers</h3>

<p>Some candidates are skilled at sounding specific without being specific. They give answers that follow the STAR structure but replace real detail with plausible-sounding generalities: "I identified the problem, aligned the team, and delivered the result ahead of schedule." That answer tells you almost nothing. The fix is probing: "What specifically did you do to align the team?" or "What would you have done differently if you had more time?" A candidate with real experience will be able to go deeper. One who is working from a template will often stall at the third follow-up.</p>

<h3>Questions that create legal risk</h3>

<p>Questions touching on protected characteristics — age, religion, pregnancy, marital status, military discharge status, arrest record, national origin, and others — are not just legally problematic; they damage trust with candidates who notice them. The University of Wisconsin HR office advises that all questions must be consistent across candidates and free of anything that touches on these areas. Forbes notes that candidates should never leave an interview wondering whether small talk about their place of worship or personal life may have influenced the outcome. Keep every question tied to the requirements of the role.</p>

<h3>Too many rounds over too long a timeline</h3>

<p>When candidates move through too many rounds over an extended period, the best ones — who typically have multiple processes running at the same time — accept another offer before yours closes. SHRM reports that the average time to fill an open role was <a href="https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/recruiters-express-optimism-for-2025">41 days in 2024, down from 48 days the previous year</a>. Each unnecessary interview round adds to that number. Design a process that is thorough enough to be reliable and fast enough to remain competitive.</p>

<h2>Giving feedback and closing the loop</h2>

<p>Most companies do not give candidates feedback after a rejection. The ones that do stand out — both to the candidates they reject and to the broader talent market that hears about those experiences over time.</p>

<p><a href="/blog/interview-feedback-examples">Interview feedback</a> is usually given to candidates who were not selected, explaining the decision and offering specific guidance they can act on. Generic feedback — "We went with someone whose experience was a closer match" — delivers almost no value. Specific feedback — "Your technical depth was clear, but we needed someone with direct experience managing a distributed team, which came through in other conversations" — is both respectful and genuinely useful. SocialTalent advises that specific, actionable feedback demonstrates respect and consideration. Generic comments do not.</p>

<p>Timing matters. The longer a candidate waits after the final round, the worse the experience becomes regardless of the content of the message. Send feedback promptly, keep it honest without being harsh, and leave the door open where it honestly can be left open.</p>

<p>Every candidate who does not receive an offer also deserves a formal response — including candidates rejected after a phone screening. The <a href="/blog/job-rejection-email">rejection email</a> does not need to be long, but it needs to be sent. At the final interview stage, a more personal message is appropriate given the time the candidate has invested. Candidates who receive a thoughtful rejection are more likely to apply again, to refer others, and to remain favorable toward the company. Those who hear nothing tend to remember it.</p>

<h2>The trade-offs in structured interviewing</h2>

<p>Structured interviewing is the most defensible approach available, and the research supports it clearly. For most roles and most hiring teams, it is the right foundation. Its limits are worth understanding, though, because they affect how much weight you should put on interview scores alone.</p>

<p>A structured interview captures what a candidate says in a formal, time-limited setting. It does not capture how they perform over weeks, how they handle the unexpected when no one is watching, or how they build relationships with colleagues who are not evaluating them. Past behavior predicts future behavior — but only imperfectly. Some candidates present better than they perform. Others perform far better than they present. These are not reasons to abandon structure; they are reasons to treat the interview as one input among several, alongside work samples, reference conversations, and probationary observation when those are part of your process.</p>

<h2>Build an interview process you can stand behind</h2>

<p>When you interview with consistency and care — asking the same questions, scoring against defined criteria, sharing feedback, and closing the loop with every candidate — you build something more than a hiring decision. You build a process you can defend, a record that supports it, and a reputation with candidates that makes the next hire easier than the last one.</p>
