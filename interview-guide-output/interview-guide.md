# The Complete Interview Guide for Hiring Teams

pageTitle: The Complete Interview Guide for Hiring Teams
slug: interview-guide

mainImageAlt: A hiring manager and two colleagues reviewing a structured interview guide document at a conference room table before interviewing candidates

Most hiring teams have hired someone who looked right on paper, performed well in the interview, and then underperformed within months. That outcome usually traces back to a missing standard — no shared questions, no scoring criteria, and no clear definition of what a strong answer looks like. Without that standard, two interviewers can sit with the same candidate, reach opposite conclusions, and have no common record on which to base a decision.

An interview guide puts the same questions, in the same order, in front of every candidate for a role, and trained interviewers score each answer against the same rubric. Each question ties to the specific competencies the role requires, and the hiring decision rests on a consistent record of observed behavior rather than on whoever argued most persuasively in the debrief.

This guide covers what a complete interview guide contains, how to build one from the role definition through the scoring rubric, how to prepare your interviewers to use it well, and how to run the debrief on scored evidence.

## What Makes an Interview Guide Different from a Question List

A question list tells you what to ask. An interview guide tells you what to ask, how to score the answers, and what a strong answer looks like versus a weak one. That distinction matters because two interviewers using the same question list walk into the debrief with no shared standard. A guide gives every interviewer a shared standard.

The guide is also tied directly to the role. It begins with a definition of what success looks like, grounded in the competencies the job actually requires, and every question follows from that definition. When the questions and the scoring both trace back to the role, the evaluation stays anchored to the job requirements rather than to the interviewer's impressions of the candidate.

## Why Structure Produces Better Hiring Decisions

Unstructured interviews feel natural, but they produce unreliable results. When interviewers improvise, they tend to ask different questions of different candidates, follow interesting tangents, and form impressions in the first few minutes that the rest of the conversation then confirms rather than tests. The result is a decision that depends more on how the conversation flowed than on what the candidate actually demonstrated.

The evidence for structure is unambiguous. At a predictive validity of .42 — more than double the .19 of unstructured interviews — structured interviews are the highest-validity single selection method studied, according to research published in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* and [reported by Talent Signal News](<https://www.talentsignalnews.com/news/structured-vs-unstructured-interviews>). That gap means the candidates your process rates highest are far more likely to perform well in the role.

Structure also makes the process faster. [Google found, according to Hunt Scanlon Media,](<https://huntscanlon.com/why-structured-interviews-lead-to-better-hiring/>) that interviewers using structured interviews saved an average of 40 minutes per interview, felt more prepared, and made more predictive hiring decisions. That time comes back because interviewers are not improvising. They know what they are asking and why, so the conversation has direction from the first question.

## The Five Components of a Complete Interview Guide

A complete interview guide has five components. Each one does a distinct job, and leaving any out reduces the guide's value.

* **Role definition.** A summary of what success looks like in the role — the day-to-day duties, the required competencies, and the behaviors that separate an average performer from an exceptional one. This comes before questions are written, because the questions flow from it.
* **Structured question set.** A fixed set of questions asked in the same order to every candidate. The mix typically includes behavioral questions ("Tell me about a time when…"), which predict future performance from past behavior, and situational questions ("What would you do if…"), which are useful when candidates lack direct experience in the area.
* **Scoring rubric.** A standardized rating scale tied to each question, with behavioral anchors at each level. The anchors describe what a strong, adequate, and weak answer looks like — so interviewers score independently, based on what they heard, rather than on overall impression.
* **Space for direct quotes.** Room to capture what the candidate actually said. Quotes give the hiring team something specific to compare and discuss, and they create a record if a decision is ever questioned.
* **Overall recommendation field.** A summary rating and recommendation that the interviewer completes before debriefing with anyone else, while the conversation is still fresh and uninfluenced by other opinions.

## How to Build the Question Set

Start with the role definition and identify three to five competencies the person hired into this role must demonstrate. For each competency, write questions that surface direct evidence of it. Behavioral questions are the foundation: "Tell me about a time when you had to manage a project with an unclear scope — what did you do, and how did it turn out?" is more useful than "Are you comfortable with ambiguity?" because it requires the candidate to produce actual evidence, not just claim a trait.

Behavioral questions are the foundation, but situational questions address the cases where a candidate has not yet encountered a particular scenario. They are hypothetical: "If you joined and found that the team's data reporting was inconsistent across departments, how would you approach fixing it?" A candidate with no direct experience can still show how they think, and you can compare the approach against what success in the role actually requires. Blending both types gives you behavioral answers grounded in what candidates have done and situational answers grounded in how they reason through problems they have not faced.

The STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) gives you a consistent framework for scoring those behavioral answers. When you write your scoring rubric, the highest-rating anchor should describe an answer that covers all four elements clearly and specifically. When candidates give vague answers, the guide should include follow-up probes — write in questions such as "What specifically did you do in that situation?" and "How did that decision affect the rest of the team?" so every interviewer knows to use them rather than letting a thin answer stand.

Keep the question set to six or eight questions. Simple questions take roughly three minutes each, and multipart questions take closer to six. With 45 minutes for core questions and ten minutes reserved for candidate questions, six to eight well-chosen questions fit the time and cover the competencies.

## How to Write the Scoring Rubric

A rubric without behavioral anchors is just a scale. Interviewers looking at a 1–5 scale with no description of what each number means will fill in the blank with their own standards, which defeats the purpose of a shared guide. Write a description for each rating level for each question, specific enough that two interviewers reading the same transcript would land on the same number.

**Sample five-point scoring anchors**

* **5 — Exceptional.** The candidate gave a complete STAR answer with a clear, specific result tied to measurable outcomes. The example shows the exact competency the question targets.
* **4 — Strong.** The answer covered the competency well, with a concrete example and a clear result. Minor gaps in detail but no weaknesses in the core competency.
* **3 — Adequate.** The answer showed some evidence of the competency but was vague on specifics or partial in its STAR structure. Would benefit from probing follow-up.
* **2 — Weak.** The answer was largely general or hypothetical where a concrete example was needed, or the example showed limited evidence of the target competency.
* **1 — No evidence.** The candidate could not provide a relevant example, or the example directly contradicted the target competency.

Score each candidate immediately after the interview, before discussing with anyone else. ["Scoring criteria defined after a strong candidate impresses you aren't criteria anymore."](#) The rubric works only when interviewers score candidates before first impressions settle into judgments.

## Preparing Your Interviewers

Interviewing is a learned skill, and most interviewers on a hiring team have never been taught how to do it. [Employment Technologies](<https://www.employment-technologies.com/>) notes that interviewers who receive training ask sharper, more targeted questions and score candidates with greater consistency across the panel. The guide is the tool, but interviewers who have not practiced with it will use it inconsistently, which undermines the structure it was designed to provide.

Before any candidate comes in, the hiring team should align on two things. First, review the guide together, not to agree on answers in advance, but to make sure everyone understands what each question is trying to surface and what the scoring anchors mean. Second, discuss potential unconscious biases explicitly, including biases toward certain educational backgrounds, before interviews begin. Naming those biases before the interviews start reduces the chance that they operate unexamined during scoring. For more on how bias enters the process and how to counter it, see the guide on [interview bias](/blog/interview-bias).

Knowing what each question surfaces and what the anchors mean is preparation, but not practice. New interviewers should also run mock interviews before meeting any candidate to turn that preparation into practice. Running through the questions on a colleague gives the interviewer a feel for pacing, helps them practice probing for specifics, and surfaces questions that are harder to ask than they looked on paper. Calibration sessions, where the team reviews what a strong answer to each question looks like, serve the same purpose and can be done in under an hour.

Once interviewers are calibrated, the dynamic in the room matters as much as the questions themselves. The candidate should carry roughly 80% of the conversation. The interviewer's job is to ask, listen without interrupting, take notes, and probe when answers are vague. The guide should contain only open questions, and interviewers should let candidates finish their own sentences and keep their reactions neutral.

## What to Send Candidates Before the Interview

[The University of Wisconsin–Madison's HR team](<https://www.wisconsin.edu>) recommends sending candidates the interview questions and a copy of the job description at least 24 hours before the interview. The reasoning is direct — a candidate who has seen the questions ahead of time gives more complete, substantive answers, and the interview time focuses on those answers rather than on a candidate's ability to recall an example under pressure.

Some hiring teams resist this because it removes the element of surprise. But a surprise interview is only valuable if quick recall under pressure is what the role requires. For most roles, you need the candidate's strongest evidence of the competencies the role requires. Candidates who have had time to prepare give fuller, more specific answers to those questions.

Send an interview agenda along with the questions — it should include who they will meet, in what order, and roughly how long each conversation will take. [Forbes Human Resources Council](<https://www.forbes.com>) contributors also recommend disclosing the salary range before the interview rather than asking candidates for their current salary. Both steps let candidates walk in knowing who they will talk to, what the role pays, and what the conversation will cover — so the interview can stay on whether they are right for the role.

With those steps done, candidates arrive knowing who they will meet, what the role pays, and what the conversation will cover. The following section covers how to run the interviews themselves.

## Running the Interview Process

Open every interview the same way — start with a brief description of the role, the structure of the conversation, and how long you expect it to take. That opening signals to candidates that the conversation has a structure and that their time will not be wasted. According to [Cronofy's Candidate Expectations Report](<https://www.cronofy.com/reports/candidate-expectations-report-2024>), 70% of candidates cite the smoothness of the recruitment process as a deciding factor when choosing between multiple offers.

With the interview opened, ask questions in the order the guide specifies. Do not skip questions because a candidate seems strong early, and do not add questions that are not in the guide. Both practices prevent you from comparing candidates fairly. If a candidate's answer addresses a later question, note it and ask the later question anyway — the prepared answer and the in-conversation answer may differ in useful ways.

Once the guide's questions are complete, reserve at least ten minutes at the end for candidate questions. The questions a candidate asks reveal what they care about and how they think. The interview is a two-way evaluation, and the candidate is deciding whether this is the right role for them. Give them the space to do that.

## Running the Debrief

The debrief is where teams most easily lose structure. If the most senior person in the room speaks first, everyone else's scores tend to migrate toward that view. Run the debrief so that every interviewer submits their scorecard independently before the group discussion begins.

Start by sharing scores, not impressions. For each question, go around the room and have each interviewer read their rating and the quote or example they captured. Begin with the questions where scores diverge. When scores diverge, the team should be able to point to specific things the candidate said or omitted to explain the difference. "I didn't get a great feeling" is not evidence. "She described the project delivering on time but could not explain what she personally did to make that happen" is evidence.

That review of scores and evidence should lead the team to advance the candidate, decline, or identify a specific follow-up question before deciding. When the team cannot agree after reviewing the evidence, the guide likely did not define the competency clearly enough, or the team has not aligned on what success in the role looks like. Use disagreements to sharpen the guide for the next round.

## Candidate Experience Through the Process

A well-run interview process shapes how candidates feel about your company, and those feelings determine whether strong candidates stay in or drop out. [87% of candidates say a positive interview experience can change their mind about a company they once doubted.](<https://withe.co/blog/job-interview-statistics>) The same source finds that 83% of talent say a negative interview experience can change their mind about a role or company they once liked. A well-run guide pushes candidates toward that positive 87% and away from the damaging 83%.

Those numbers make the action clear. Communicate at every transition point. When a candidate advances, tell them what happens next and when they can expect to hear from you. When you decide not to move forward, tell them promptly. The Forbes HR Council describes ghosting candidates as "an unacceptable practice." The guide should include standard communication checkpoints so that no candidate falls through the gaps. For templates you can use at each stage, see the article on [how to write a job rejection email](/blog/job-rejection-email).

Communication continues after rejection — feedback after the interview rebuilds the goodwill a rejection would otherwise cost you. [79% of candidates would consider reapplying to a company if they received feedback after an interview, even after rejection.](<https://www.selectsoftwarereviews.com/blog/recruiting-statistics>) [Candidates are four times more likely to consider a company for a future opportunity when the company provides interview feedback.](<https://withe.co/blog/candidate-experience-statistics>) Candidates who were rejected but came away with a positive view of the process may apply again, refer others, or accept an offer the next time. For guidance on what to write, see the article on [interview feedback examples](/blog/interview-feedback-examples).

## Where the Interview Process Breaks Down

The most common failure point is building the guide after the first few candidates have already been interviewed. At that point, the team has informal impressions that will shape what questions get written and what a good answer looks like — which means the guide ends up calibrated to the candidates already seen, not to the role. Build the guide before any candidate comes in.

Inconsistent use is the second most common failure. Some interviewers follow the guide closely; others treat it as a loose starting point and add their own questions, skip questions that feel redundant, or extend conversations in directions the guide does not cover. When the debrief arrives, the team is comparing responses to different questions, which makes scoring meaningless. Establish before the first interview that the guide is used as written, by everyone.

Legal exposure is the failure point teams most often miss. [Forbes HR Council](<https://councils.forbes.com/forbeshumanresourcescouncil>) contributors flag that well-meaning small talk can cross legal lines "if the candidate didn't raise the topic first." Questions about family, age, national origin, religion, and similar topics, even casual ones intended to build rapport, can expose the company to liability. Keeping the conversation on the questions in the guide is the most reliable protection against this.

Finally, watch for [first-impression bias](/blog/first-impression-bias) slipping back in through the debrief. This bias persists even when interviewers recognize it — an impression formed in the first few minutes reshapes how an interviewer reads every answer and score that comes after it. Independent scorecard submission before the group discussion is the structural defense against it. Use it every time.

## The Trade-off Worth Knowing

A structured interview guide reduces bias and improves predictive accuracy, but it limits how far an interviewer can follow a candidate's answers. Candidates who apply for roles that require creative thinking or relationship-building often give their best evidence in an open exchange rather than in response to a fixed question set. A rigid guide cuts off those exchanges before the most useful evidence appears.

The answer is to leave one question slot open and keep the scored sections fixed. Reserve one question slot per interview for an open-ended prompt, such as "Is there anything about your background that you think is relevant to this role that our questions didn't surface?" and treat the ten minutes of candidate questions as an informal, unscored part of the conversation. Candidates can then surface evidence the structured questions missed, without any inconsistency entering the scored sections.

## Building Your First Interview Guide

The guide does not need to be perfect before you use it. It needs to be better than what you have now. Start with the role, build the structure, run it, and improve it from there. Here are the steps to follow.

1. **Write the role definition first.** Describe what the person hired into this role will do in their first 90 days, what they will need to know, and what behaviors will make them excellent rather than merely adequate. This document is the foundation for everything else.
2. **Identify three to five core competencies.** For each one, write one behavioral question and one situational question, then choose which fits better in the guide. Add scoring anchors for each question before showing the guide to anyone else.
3. **Pilot with one round of interviews.** After that round, debrief the interviewers on the guide itself, not just the candidates. Which questions produced the most useful evidence? Which scoring anchors caused disagreement? Adjust, and use the revised version for every subsequent role with similar requirements.

Over time, the guide becomes a library your team can draw from rather than something you rebuild from scratch each time. Every hire that follows benefits from the structure, the questions, and the scoring anchors you built into the first version.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is an interview guide?

An interview guide is a structured recruitment tool that gives every candidate for a given role the same questions, in the same order, scored against the same rubric. It differs from a question list in that it also defines what a strong, adequate, and weak answer looks like for each question, so interviewers can evaluate candidates against a shared standard rather than a personal impression.

### How many questions should an interview guide include?

Most guides include six to eight questions for a standard interview of 45 to 60 minutes, leaving ten minutes at the end for candidate questions. Allowing roughly three minutes per simple question and six minutes for multipart questions helps keep the conversation on track. Fewer, well-chosen questions tied directly to the role's core competencies produce better evaluation data than a long list of loosely related questions.

### What is the difference between behavioral and situational interview questions?

Behavioral questions ask candidates to describe how they handled a past situation ("Tell me about a time when…"), producing evidence based on actual experience. Situational questions present a hypothetical scenario ("What would you do if…"), which is useful for candidates who may not have direct experience in a particular area. A well-built interview guide typically includes both types, using behavioral questions where experience exists and situational questions to fill gaps.

### Should you send interview questions to candidates in advance?

Yes. The University of Wisconsin–Madison's HR team recommends sending candidates the interview questions and job description at least 24 hours before the interview. Candidates who have seen the questions ahead of time give more complete, substantive answers, and the interview time is spent on evaluating evidence rather than watching candidates recall examples under pressure.

### How does an interview scorecard reduce bias?

A scorecard reduces bias by requiring each interviewer to rate every candidate against the same behavioral anchors immediately after the interview, before group discussion begins. This prevents the most common bias patterns — such as first-impression bias and anchoring to the most recent candidate — from shaping scores. When scores are submitted independently first, the debrief starts with evidence rather than with whoever speaks first.

### What should happen in a hiring debrief?

Every interviewer should submit their scorecard independently before the group discussion begins. The debrief then starts by sharing scores and the specific quotes or examples behind them, question by question, before moving to any overall impressions. The goal is to reach one of three outcomes — advance, decline, or identify a follow-up question — based on what candidates actually said, not on the general feeling the conversation left.

### How do you improve an interview guide over time?

After each round of interviews, debrief the team on the guide itself: which questions produced the clearest evidence of each competency, and which scoring anchors caused disagreement. Revise the anchors or questions where the team struggled to align, and track how well the guide's ratings predict actual performance for candidates who are hired. Over several rounds, the guide becomes a tested library the team can adapt for similar roles rather than rebuild from scratch.
