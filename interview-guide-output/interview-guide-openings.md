## Before

<p>If you find all the different types of interviews confusing, or feel like every interview is a blur, this guide will make everything about interviewing clearer. An interview is a meeting between a candidate and one or more persons in which the employer, or the employer's representatives, asks a series of questions. What sounds like a simple exchange carries a great deal of weight in the hiring process, and the way you run it determines how much useful information you actually get out of it.</p>

<p>At its most useful, an interview is a structured evaluation of whether a candidate can do the job, will do it, and will work well with the people already doing it. The candidate's answers — when you ask the right questions and score them consistently — give you evidence. The interview also works in the other direction: the candidate is assessing you, your company, and whether the role is worth pursuing. According to SHRM, <a href="https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/interview-critical-part-hiring-process-candidates-say">68% of candidates say a company's hiring process positively or negatively impacts their decision to accept a job offer</a>. The interview is usually where that impression forms.</p>

<p>This article covers the main interview formats and what each one is for, how to design questions that give you useful signal, how to score candidates consistently, where bias enters the process and how to reduce it, what a strong candidate experience looks like, and how to close the loop with feedback and rejection communication.</p>



## Draft

<p>If you find all the different types of interviews confusing, or feel like every interview is a blur, this guide will make everything about interviewing clearer. An interview is a meeting between a candidate and one or more persons in which the employer, or the employer's representatives, asks a series of questions. What connects every format — from a ten-minute phone screen to a final conversation with a founder — is that the interview is where the two sides of a hire get to assess each other directly, in real time, in ways no resume or application can replicate.</p>

<p>In practice, an interview is rarely one thing. It is a set of structured or unstructured conversations, spread across formats and stages, that together give your hiring team the information they need to make a defensible decision. Each format serves a different purpose: a phone screening narrows the field before you invest more time, a behavioral interview grounds the conversation in real experience, a technical interview tests reasoning under pressure, and a panel brings multiple perspectives to bear on the same candidate. The questions you ask, the way you score the answers, and the care you take to limit bias all shape whether the information you gather is reliable — and whether the candidate who sits across from you, or on the other end of a video call, leaves with a fair impression of your company.</p>

<p>This article covers the full scope of interviewing: the formats and what each one is for, the difference between structured and unstructured interviewing, how to design questions that give you useful signal, how to score candidates consistently, where bias enters the process and how to reduce it, what a strong candidate experience looks like, and how to close the loop with feedback and a timely response to every candidate.</p>
