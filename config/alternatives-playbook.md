# Playbook: writing a "[Competitor] alternatives" post for Polymer

Two documents come before this one.

1. ../Polymer_Competitive_Positioning_Guidelines.md is the positioning. Read it in full before writing. Its vocabulary, its differentiators, and its competitive principle are the source for every Polymer sentence in the article.
2. The published Workable alternatives post (https://www.polymer.co/blog/workable-alternatives, source in workable-alternatives-2026-09-11/draft.md) is the approved example. Read it in full before writing. This document states the rules it follows.

## 1. What the article is

1. The search is "[competitor] alternatives". The answer is a list of alternatives and what the choice between them turns on. The answer is not Polymer.
2. The reader is a growing company or startup, from a founder interviewing their first hires to a company with hiring managers and a recruiter. No headcount numbers anywhere.
3. Competitors are described truthfully, and their sections read as heavy, complex software that a recruiting department runs. Their sections lead with the features a growing company does not want. The features a growing company wants most are shown in Polymer's section and buried or left out of the competitors'. Competitors have more than enough other features to fill five bullets.
4. Resist the compulsion to use competitors' copy from their websites verbatim, because doing so will result in you promoting them instead of Polymer.
5. The list is by fit, not rank. Polymer is first, with one disclosure sentence saying it is our product and that for every tool, including ours, the article says where the incumbent or another option is the better choice.
6. The opener does not name Polymer as the winner.
7. The comparison table and the shortlist answer the query "What are the best alternatives to [competitor]?"
8. The incumbent gets its own section before the list. Its subsections follow the template in section 6. The framing is fit, not fault, and never its promoter.
9. The closing section answers how much recruiting software the reader's company needs, in reader terms, then gives one verdict per tool. Never "there's no universal winner".
10. FAQ answers are self-contained. The first sentence answers the question. Then the reasoning, then the tool to pick when the reader's situation differs. Never "Polymer." as an answer. They feed FAQPage schema verbatim.
11. It is a blog post at /blog/<competitor>-alternatives. The title follows the keyword workbook. The reader goes in the body and the meta description, not the title.

## 2. Structure

### 2.1 Article skeleton

1. Opener, three or four paragraphs. What the incumbent is and that it is right for some companies. Who this comparison is for. What the choice turns on. What the article covers.
2. Table of contents.
3. Comparison table. Columns: Alternative, Best for, Starting price, Pricing published, AI review. Prices as published on each vendor's site in the month of writing. Quote-only prices shown as reported ranges and labelled as such.
4. The incumbent's section (section 6).
5. The shortlist. One line per tool, "Name: Best for [who]", with the disclosure sentence before it (section 1).
6. One section per tool, Polymer first (section 2.2).
7. "How to choose a [competitor] alternative". A bulleted list of the criteria the choice turns on, each naming where the tools fall. Hiring volume, recruiting team structure, need for sourcing or a recruiting CRM, workflow complexity, implementation tolerance, AI requirements, integrations and API, budget and pricing model.
8. Closing (section 1).
9. FAQ (section 1). The questions are the ones searchers ask: best for a small business, best for a startup, cheaper alternative, easiest to use, best for a lean hiring team, whether an enterprise ATS is needed.

### 2.2 The per-tool template

Every tool, Polymer included, gets these subsections in this order.

1. H2 in the form "N. Name: Best for [who]". [who] is short and names a team, not a feature or a price. No two tools share ground. For a competitor, [who] rules out the reader. The shortlist and the comparison table carry the same label.
2. Verdict paragraph. What it is, then who it is built for. Two sentences for a competitor, three for Polymer.
3. Screenshot, where there is one.
4. "Why consider it". The first sentence answers the question. Three to four sentences, sixty to seventy words.
5. "Key features". Exactly five bullets for every tool. Polymer may bundle several things inside one bullet. A competitor gets one feature per bullet.
6. "Pricing". Published prices stated as fact. Quote-only prices given as reported ranges with the source named.
7. "Where it beats [incumbent]".
8. "Where [incumbent] wins".
9. "Best fit". One bolded "Choose X if" sentence, sometimes followed by a second sentence naming the tool to pick instead.

Rules for the template:

1. No tool gets a subsection the others don't have.
2. Word counts for the same subsection are comparable across tools. Polymer's "Where [incumbent] wins" is the shortest of the seven.
3. Subsection headings are identical across tools. The push script turns "### Heading" into H3 blocks.

## 3. Voice and sentence rules

Two rules for alternatives articles:

1. Ranking language. See section 1.
2. Numbers or superlatives that promote a competitor ("200+", "one of the most widely used", "one of the most established"). Say "posts to many job boards".

## 4. How to position Polymer

1. Read ../Polymer_Competitive_Positioning_Guidelines.md in full. It is the positioning, and its vocabulary and framing are for use in the article. The Language Guidelines in it govern every descriptor applied to Polymer.
2. Customer quotes exist, and Jessica has them. They are not used in alternatives articles. Do not go looking for them.
3. The primary differentiator is utility-based: what the product does for the customer.
4. Unlimited users may be a differentiator, but it is plan-based and monetary rather than utility-based. Do not make it the primary differentiator if at all possible. If the competitor specifically limits users, you may use the angle of not having to pick and choose who participates in hiring.
5. For "Where it beats [incumbent]", look for a real feature gap in the incumbent first. Against some incumbents there is none; Greenhouse and Lever have no feature gap against Polymer. Then get creative. The Workable article's differentiator is setup, which is not a feature but is still the utility of the product to the customer. Read it as an example of how to do that, not as the differentiator for every incumbent. Use judgment. There may be a better one.
6. Polymer's verdict paragraph, "Why consider it", "Key features", "Pricing", "Best fit", and the closing paragraph do not depend on the incumbent. Model them on the Workable article. The substance stays; the sentences vary from article to article. "Where it beats" and "Where [incumbent] wins" are written fresh for each incumbent.

### 4.1 Verdict paragraph

What Polymer is, then who it is for, in the same shape as the competitors' openers. The reader range is stated inclusively, from a founder interviewing their first hires to a company with hiring managers and a recruiter. Unlimited users is stated as a fact. Plans are "tiered by how many jobs you need open at once", not "per job" and not "by jobs posted". Three sentences.

### 4.2 Why consider it

1. It sells Polymer on its own merits. Differentiation from the incumbent belongs in "Where it beats".
2. The first sentence gives the reader a reason and carries several ideas at once: a complete ATS they can start using today, with what they need to hire, without weeks of onboarding.
3. The second paragraph draws on the positioning doc's core position: capable and flexible, intentionally focused, sophisticated hiring requirements without the workflows and complexity a growing company does not need.
4. It names no roles. It is not about the plan. It is not a scenario. It is not a product description. It does not say what Polymer lacks. It does not list the incumbent's modules. It does not oversell Plato, whose reviews are capped per plan. It does not detail everything; leave the reader something to discover. Money stays out.

### 4.3 Key features

Five bullets, in this order.

1. Message automations and templates.
2. Plato AI screening.
3. Candidate reviews and review requests.
4. Company job board, with customizable application forms folded in at the end.
5. Customizations. The customer API first, then notifications, analytics, LinkedIn posting, Zapier triggers, and a custom hiring pipeline last. Not every integration; listing them all shows how few there are.

How a bullet is written:

1. The bolded lead is a feature name, not a category. Team collaboration and dashboard are not features.
2. The first sentence is a hybrid: what the reader gets and what the feature is, integrated, in plain language. Then one plain sentence of what they can do with it.
3. The feature is the subject of the sentence, not "you".
4. No implementation detail.
5. The shape and the benefit vocabulary change from bullet to bullet.
6. Plato is never first. The careers page is not the top feature. Job distribution is not pushed.

### 4.4 Pricing

Prices are published, so state them. Every plan has the complete feature set, unlimited users, and unlimited candidates. Plans differ by how many jobs can be published at once and by monthly Plato reviews. Jobs, board, and team can be set up before a payment method is entered, and the free trial starts when the first job is published.

### 4.5 Where it beats [incumbent]

1. One paragraph, built on one big thing (item 5 above). A second paragraph on the pricing model is allowed when the incumbent prices by company headcount.
2. The first sentence carries a benefit.
3. The incumbent is named at most twice, as the comparison. The subsection is about Polymer; the incumbent is not its topic.
4. When setup is the thing: ready to go out of the box, publishing a job takes a title and a description, Plato needs nothing more than that, customize any of it whenever you like. Plato appears as proof of the setup claim, not as the differentiator. It does not read as a setup how-to.
5. Small differentiators are not the thing: permission details, credit packs, one integration the incumbent lacks.

### 4.6 Where [incumbent] wins

The shortest "Where [incumbent] wins" of the seven. The incumbent's own ground only. Published pricing, a free trial, self-serve setup, and AI review are Polymer's ground too and are not given to the incumbent. No job board counts.

### 4.7 Best fit and closing

Best fit: "Choose Polymer if you're a growing company that wants a capable, flexible ATS that's ready to use the day you sign up." The closing paragraph uses "capable, intuitive" and ends with setting up before payment and the trial starting on the first published job. Both lines are established; reuse them.

### 4.8 Not in the Polymer section

1. A price or dollar figure in a sell sentence, or a claim that Polymer costs less. Those belong in "Pricing" and the cheaper-alternative FAQ. The pricing paragraph of "Where it beats" says what each product bases its price on, not whether it costs more or less.
2. Unlimited users as the lead.
3. Plan sizing outside "Pricing".
4. A headcount number for the reader.
5. A recruiter the reader does not have.
6. A subsection the competitors don't have.
7. Plato leading anything.
8. A point already made in the verdict paragraph.

## 5. How to frame competitors

Write the competitors before Polymer.

### 5.1 Verdict paragraph

What it is, then who it is built for. Name the team it assumes, and that team is not the reader: a talent acquisition department, a sourcing team, a human resources department, a large company with many departments and countries. A competitor's opener never carries one of Polymer's selling points. Vary the shape across the six.

### 5.2 Why consider it

The first sentence answers the question and describes a situation the reader is not in. Vary the opener across the six. The rest of the paragraph follows from the first sentence and reads as the administration a recruiting department does.

### 5.3 Key features

1. Five bullets, one feature per bullet.
2. No paid add-ons listed as features.
3. Nothing Polymer has in the lead position. Small-business favourites, interview scheduling among them, are not in the first two bullets. Lead with what a growing company does not want.
4. The AI bullet is last and titled "Generative AI". Other AI features first; scoring or screening at the end of the sentence or left out. A vendor's product name may appear inside the sentence, never as the lead and never as the title.
5. Bullets are of equal length within a list.
6. Dry and truthful. Vendor product names are fine. Vendor marketing copy is not (section 1).
7. A competitor's feature is not described by what it doesn't do.
8. Not all of a competitor's integrations. Count comparisons lose.
9. Research their AI from their own pages before writing the AI bullet.
10. When a list is thin, fill it from the vendor's own pages with full sentences.

### 5.4 Where it beats [incumbent]

The competitor's feature, "which [incumbent] doesn't", then two or three sentences of plain detail about the feature. The incumbent is mentioned once. Pad with detail of the feature, not with the incumbent. It is not a repetition of "Why consider it". When a fact holds on the vendor's pages and in the research, it is stated as true.

### 5.5 Where [incumbent] wins

1. The incumbent's own ground only. For Workable that was the sourcing database, the HR suite, texting, video interviews, and assessments in the platform, published prices, and monthly billing with no minimum term. Never a Polymer selling point handed to the incumbent.
2. Full sentences. The fact, not the consequence. No speculation.
3. Add one plain sentence of the competitor's deficiency. Neither side gets all the copy.
4. "Separate purchases" is fine. "Separate vendors" and "another vendor to integrate" are not, because they are true of Polymer too.
5. Verify a no-contract or no-minimum claim on the vendor's site before making it.

### 5.6 Best fit

Rules out the reader. Names the team the tool assumes. No snark, no "you're buying", no "look at Polymer first". The H2's "Best for" also rules out the reader.

## 6. How to describe the incumbent

Subsections, in order: "Where [incumbent] is strongest" (one paragraph, then a bolded "Choose [incumbent] if"), "How [incumbent] is priced", "What's included and what's added on", "Who tends to look elsewhere", then one line pointing to the list.

1. Fit, not fault, and never its promoter. The opener is "[Incumbent] is a widely used applicant tracking system (ATS), and for some companies it's the right one."
2. Lead with its distinguishing recruiting feature, not job distribution. "Posts to many job boards", no counts, no superlatives.
3. Pricing mechanics stated as facts, including what the price is gated on.
4. Add-ons named as add-ons, with prices where published. An AI add-on is "a paid add-on on every plan" when that is what it is.
5. Describe it the way it sells itself. Do not narrate its history.
6. If Polymer's copy does not differentiate from the incumbent as described, change the way the incumbent is described until it does.
7. "Who tends to look elsewhere" gives the reasons that are true for the reader: the pricing model, the complexity, the setup. It does not name Polymer.
8. Audit every place the article praises the incumbent on Polymer's ground.

## 9. Self-review before handing over

Run this on the whole draft before Jessica sees any of it.

1. Reread the reference posts and the marketing site before writing.
2. A rule given once applies to every sentence after it. When a defect is found in one place, audit every parallel section for the same defect.
3. Every subsection reads the same across tools. Read each subsection for all seven tools side by side: same structure, same bullet count, comparable length.
4. Any deviation from the per-tool template is fixed, not reported.
5. Every sentence is checked against section 3 and every Polymer claim against polymer-feature-inventory.md.
