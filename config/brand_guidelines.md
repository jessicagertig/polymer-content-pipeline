# Polymer Brand Guidelines

This guide draws on polymer.co (the homepage, the features pages, the about page, and the pricing page), the Polymer feature inventory, the Polymer Competitive Positioning Guidelines, and published Polymer blog articles.

---

## Company Description

Polymer is an applicant tracking system and job board software for growing teams. Teams publish roles, manage applicants, and make decisions in one platform. Polymer includes a branded job board, a candidate pipeline with custom hiring stages, built-in candidate messaging, scored reviews and shared comments for the hiring team, and Plato, an AI reviewer that scores each application against the job's own requirements. Every plan includes the complete feature set and unlimited users.

**One-sentence version (for meta descriptions).**
Polymer is an applicant tracking system and job board software where a growing team runs its whole hiring process, from the job post to the decision, in one place.

**Mission.**
Polymer has no published mission statement. The about page states four principles, quoted below.
- **Question the standards.** "While other hiring platforms exist to just serve the status quo, we're building something different. We know there's a better way that exists outside of what's typical."
- **Hiring is for everyone.** "Hiring is foundational to creating great work culture. It's a responsibility that goes beyond specific job titles or roles."
- **The details matter.** "We regard code as craft and strive to build software that we're proud of."
- **Start with simple.** "Even the most powerful tools should be easy to get started with."

## Target Audience

Primary buyers and readers:
- **Founders and executives at growing companies** who are hiring without a recruiting department and often run the first hires themselves.
- **Hiring managers** who own a role from the job post to the offer and want the whole team's input on each candidate.
- **Everyone else on the hiring team.** Because hiring is a team sport and user seats are unlimited, engineers, advisors, department heads, and partners take part in reviewing candidates.

These readers may have sophisticated hiring requirements, and they value speed, usability, flexibility, and focus. They do not need software designed around enterprise companies or large recruiting departments. They take hiring as seriously as those companies do.

Use plain hiring terms such as applicant tracking system (ATS), hiring team, hiring manager, hiring stages, candidates, job post, and job board. Explain any other recruiting term the first time it appears.

Pain points these readers feel:
- Candidates scattered across spreadsheets, email threads, and notes, with no one place to see where everyone stands.
- Needing to move fast on a good candidate before another company does.
- Wanting the whole team involved without paying per seat and without losing control of who sees what.
- No time and no staff for a long implementation or a steep learning curve.
- The complexity, clutter, and cost of enterprise recruiting software, none of which these readers need.

## Voice

**Expert. Direct. Grounded in business decisions.**

Polymer speaks like an experienced article writer who is calm, candid, and fair-minded. Specific — not vague. The voice assumes the reader's competence without assuming expertise, and earns authority through precision, not volume.

Every claim connects back to hiring and business outcomes. A business outcome is something concrete the reader gains or avoids. Business outcomes in hiring include:
- a more efficient hiring process
- time saved
- better relationships between candidates and employers
- better relationships between colleagues on the hiring team
- a better chance of evaluating a candidate accurately
- better hiring decisions and fewer bad hires
- roles filled faster
- a lower cost per hire
- fairer, more consistent evaluation of every candidate
- fewer strong candidates dropping out of the process
- more offers accepted
- new hires who stay longer
- a clear record of why each decision was made
- a better experience for candidates
- less legal risk in hiring
- more qualified applicants
- more referrals
- strong past candidates willing to apply again
- clear ownership of each step in the hiring process
- hiring that keeps up with the company's growth
- the ability to hire well without a dedicated recruiter
- more effective interviews
- team alignment on what the role requires

Name a cost only when it is specific and real, and state it once.

When the article teaches how to do something, the reader has already decided to do it. The purpose of the article is to give the reader what they came for. You may describe the benefits of doing it, because that reinforces the decision the reader already made, but do not try to persuade the reader with threats or costs. An article about a behavioral interview scoring matrix can explain what the matrix improves, but it does not need to warn the reader about what happens without one. When the article's topic includes why the reader should do something, it is appropriate to devote more of the article to making that case.

Features are never described in isolation. The question is always "what does this do for the business?"

**Core voice attributes:**
- **Confident** — makes definitive statements without hedging. No "might help you" or "could potentially."
- **Precise** — uses specific numbers, timeframes, and named frameworks. "Surface signals that appear 6–12 months before a customer churns" — not "early warning signals."
- **Empathetic to the buyer** — understands the person who is hiring, or who has to handle a team, and their real frustration. Acknowledges the pain before offering the solution.
- **Analytically credible** — backs claims with data (Gartner, Bain, Qualtrics, Deloitte). Quotes specific percentages and multipliers.
- **Credible information** — where there is no number to cite, such as when you are explaining how to write a rejection email, draws on information from good sources.

## Tone

- **Authoritative but human.**
- **Serious without alarmism.**
- **Thoughtful and analytical.**
- **Empathetic to the reader.**
- **Fair-minded and balanced.**

**Do not be:**
- Breathless or hype-y ("game-changing," "revolutionary," "disrupting")
- Jargon-heavy for the sake of it
- Vague or generic
- Fear-mongering (imply consequences, don't dramatize them)
- Colloquial (no "Hey!" intros, no exclamation points in product copy). Idioms are fine.

## Writing Style

Write by the rules of Strunk's The Elements of Style.

The reader doesn't have your context, and the reader doesn't know what you're thinking. The person reading the article has not seen these instructions, the research brief, or the outline. Every sentence has to make sense from the contents of the article alone.

Unless this is a versus, alternatives, or listicle about ATSs, do not advertise a competitor.

Address the reader as you. We means everyone.

### Sentence structure

Write sentences by the rules of The Elements of Style, which follows at the end of this prompt.

### Paragraph structure

Write paragraphs by Rules 8 and 9 of The Elements of Style, which follows at the end of this prompt.

### Framing patterns
- **Problem → consequence → solution**: Identify the pain, explain what happens if you don't fix it, then offer the answer.
- **"This doesn't mean..."**: Proactively handle objections. "This doesn't require overpromising or building every feature."
- **"X, but Y"**: Contrast to sharpen a point. "Customers forgive 'no' but never forget silence."
- **Specificity as credibility**: Always cite specific research, stat, or example when making a broad claim.
- **Segmentation as sophistication**: Show the reader that nuance matters. "Not all feedback is equal."

### Headers
- Blog H2s are often full questions ("How do feedback loops shape product strategy?"), but no blog should have only H2s that are full questions.
- Use bold within body text sparingly — for key terms or framework labels only

## Terminology

### Use these terms

Describe Polymer with nine descriptors, which are intuitive, capable, flexible, focused, modern, easy to adopt, quick to get started, thoughtfully designed, and nimble. Vary them. A descriptor may repeat within an article, but do not settle on one. Never use focused, nimble, or modern alone, and never pair them with each other. Use each of the three with other words, and add them only sometimes. The other six descriptors may stand alone.

| Preferred | Instead of |
|---|---|
| applicant tracking system, then ATS | recruiting platform, talent acquisition suite |
| hiring team | recruiting team, talent team |
| hiring manager | recruiter |
| hiring stages, hiring pipeline | funnel, requisition |
| job post | job ad, listing (except in product names such as External Listings and LinkedIn Limited Listings) |
| job board, or careers page when the board stands in for one | careers portal |
| hiring platform, when the whole product is meant | solution |
| Plato, Polymer's AI candidate reviewer | the AI, AI screening tool |
| Hiring Made Simple, the slogan, exactly | simple ATS, simple hiring software |

Growing company and growing team are the main terms for the reader's company. Readers also search for startup and small business, so use those terms too, but do not use only one of these terms throughout an article. Do not use SMB.

### Avoid these terms

- Basic, lightweight, bargain, budget, stripped-down, entry-level, or "an ATS only for very small companies." Polymer is neither cheap nor stripped down. The product is easier to use because it is intentionally focused.
- "Simple" for the product itself. Polymer makes hiring simple. Apply the word to the hiring, never to the product.
- "One-click" distribution, "immediately distributed," "posts everywhere," or any wording that says publishing a job puts it in front of candidates at once. Google Jobs is on by default when a job publishes, and Google indexes the post on its own schedule, which may take up to seven days. LinkedIn Limited Listings cost nothing extra, LinkedIn may decline a listing, and an approved listing appears hours after publishing and only when the team has configured the LinkedIn integration.
- Distribution as a selling point. Distribution is a weak point. Where an article needs distribution, state it as a fact and move on.
- A headcount number for the reader. The phrase "growing team" describes the reader's company without one.
- A competitor's own marketing language. You may call a competitor cluttered.

### Product-specific terminology

- **Polymer**, one word, capital P.
- **Plato**, Polymer's AI candidate reviewer. "Plato AI" on the site. Auto-reviews are turned on per role. A review gives each requirement in the job description one of three verdicts (clear evidence, related evidence, no evidence) and the candidate one of five fit levels (Excellent, Good, Mixed, Weak, Poor), with the reasoning shown. Name, location, and email are removed before scoring. Every plan includes a monthly allowance of Plato credits. Plato is one capability inside Polymer, never the identity of the product.
- **Hiring stages** are the stages of the pipeline. The defaults are Inbox, Screen, Interview, Decide, Offer, Hired, and Archived. Teams rename, reorder, add, and delete the stages between Inbox and Hired.
- **Hiring team** is the set of people on a job. Account roles are Owner, Admin, Member, and Interviewer.
- **Candidate reviews** are scored, written feedback visible to the hiring team. **Review templates** are reusable review content. A **review request** sends one candidate's **Review kit** to anyone, on the hiring team or not.
- **Comments** are shared with the hiring team. **Private notes** are visible only to the person who wrote them.
- **Message templates** are reusable candidate messages with placeholders. **Hiring stage message automations** send a template on their own when a candidate enters a stage.
- **Job board** is the branded board every account gets at signup, on a custom domain if the team wants one. **External Listings** is the per-job setting for Google Jobs, LinkedIn Limited Listings, and X Hiring.
- **Application form** is per job, with standard fields and **custom questions**. A **hiring document** is a per-job template copied to each candidate on apply.
- **Plans** are Starter, Growth, and Scale. Every plan has the complete feature set, unlimited users, and unlimited candidates. Plans differ by how many jobs can be published at once and by monthly Plato credits. The 14-day free trial starts when the first job publishes.

## AEO and SEO Writing Conventions

**Structured for AI citation:**
- Semantic triples: subject → predicate → object with specifics
- Cite specific stats with the source in-text when used
- Each H2 section must be self-contained — AI engines extract sections, not full pages
- Tables and lists for comparisons and lists of attributes

## Examples of On-Brand Copy

Each passage below quotes a published Polymer article word for word.

### Opening with what is at stake

> Get onboarding right, and you'll set employees up for long-term success. Get it wrong, and your next hire may prematurely leave for something better.

— *What is Employee Onboarding? A Definitive Guide*

> Crafting a respectful, effective job rejection email requires a range of skills, including emotional intelligence. It can be such a challenge to get right that many companies don't bother sending them.

— *How to Write Personalized Job Rejection Emails*

### Defining a term in one sentence

> First impression bias causes people to form a quick and incomplete impression of someone based on what they see and hear in the first few seconds of meeting.

— *How to Overcome (and Recognize) First Impression Bias When Hiring*

### Citing a number and saying what it costs the reader

> This isn't anecdotal. Around 3.2 million people quit their job in June 2026 alone. And according to Gallup, only 12% of employees felt their company did a great job with onboarding.

— *What is Employee Onboarding? A Definitive Guide*

> The median time to fill an open role is 39 days, and every one of those days is time your own team spends on hiring rather than the work they were hired to do.

— *Talent Acquisition vs. Recruitment: What's the Difference?*

### Telling the reader what to do, and why

> You should send the email as soon as you decide not to hire the candidate. That way, you won't waste their time, and they can move on with their job search.

— *How to Write Personalized Job Rejection Emails*

### Admitting where the advice runs out

> The behavioral interview scoring matrix is useful for understanding more about a candidate's behavior — but it doesn't always give you an accurate representation of how someone might behave today.

— *Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix: A Template & Simple Guide*

> Sometimes, however, new hires don't work out. Be careful not to fire too fast.

— *What is Employee Onboarding? A Definitive Guide*

### Writing for the small team

> You don't always know when you'll need to fill a role—especially at a startup or small business. Ideally, you'll have plenty of notice, but that's not always the case.

— *Talent Acquisition vs. Recruitment: What's the Difference?*

### Taking the candidate's side

> Put yourself in the shoes of a job seeker. If you attend an interview that feels unprepared and unstructured, it's hard to gather your thoughts.

— *Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix: A Template & Simple Guide*

### Showing the weak line beside the better one

> ❌ "You're not a good fit for this position."
>
> ✅ "You have strong technical skills, but you need more experience with X program to thrive in this role."

— *How to Write Personalized Job Rejection Emails*

### Mentioning Polymer late and plainly

> A hiring platform, like Polymer, helps companies track and manage the entire recruitment process. From posting the job to offering the role, you can manage every step within the platform.

— *How to Overcome (and Recognize) First Impression Bias When Hiring*
