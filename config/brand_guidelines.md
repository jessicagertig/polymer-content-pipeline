# Polymer Brand Guidelines

Sourced from polymer.co (the homepage, the features pages, the about page, and the pricing page), the Polymer feature inventory, the Polymer Competitive Positioning Guidelines, and five published Polymer blog posts.

## Company Description

Polymer is an applicant tracking system and job board software for growing teams. It streamlines the hiring process. Teams publish roles, manage applicants, and make decisions all in one platform. Polymer includes a branded job board, a candidate pipeline with custom hiring stages, built-in candidate messaging, scored reviews and shared comments for the hiring team, and Plato, an AI reviewer that scores each application against the job's own requirements. Every plan includes the complete feature set and unlimited users.

## Target Audience

Primary buyers and readers:
- **Founders and executives at growing companies** who are hiring without a recruiting department and often run the first hires themselves.
- **Hiring managers** who own a role from the job post to the offer and want the whole team's input on each candidate.
- **Everyone else on the hiring team.** Hiring is a team sport, and user seats are unlimited, so engineers, advisors, department heads, and partners take part in reviewing candidates.

They can have sophisticated hiring requirements, and they value speed, usability, flexibility, and focus. They do not need software designed around enterprise companies or large recruiting departments. The distinction is organizational need, not organizational seriousness.

Use plain hiring terms such as applicant tracking system (ATS), hiring team, hiring manager, hiring stages, candidates, job post, and job board. Explain any recruiting term beyond those the first time it appears.

Pain points they feel:
- Candidates scattered across spreadsheets, email threads, and notes, with no one place to see where everyone stands.
- Needing to move fast on a good candidate before another company does.
- Wanting the whole team involved without paying per seat and without losing control of who sees what.
- No time and no staff for a long implementation or a steep learning curve.
- The complexity, clutter, and cost of enterprise recruiting software, none of which they need.

## Voice

The voice is that of an experienced colleague talking a busy manager through this week's hiring problem. It addresses the reader in the second person, and that reader is a specific person: someone at a small or growing company, hiring alongside a real job, often with no HR department to lean on. The writer speaks in the first person plural, as a peer rather than a lecturer, and assumes competence without assuming expertise.

The diction is plain business English: short, common words, contractions, and the idioms of everyday work talk rather than the terminology of HR textbooks. Hiring jargon appears only when the reader will meet it elsewhere, and each term is defined in the sentence that introduces it. Claims lean on numbers, and every number names its source. Adjectives run warm and approving; the register encourages and never scolds.

The syntax moves quickly. Sentences are short, paragraphs rarely exceed three sentences, and a single sentence often stands alone for emphasis. Sections open with a direct question or a brief hypothetical that places the reader inside a scene, then shift to imperatives. Parenthetical asides promise that a point will come later or slip in a caveat. Signposting at the start of each section tells the reader what is coming.

The presence behind the words is optimistic and practical. It takes the candidate's side as readily as the employer's, treating respect for applicants as good business rather than charity. It is even-handed, giving each recommendation its drawbacks. Humor is rare, mild, and never at the reader's expense. Polymer enters briefly and matter-of-factly, one tool among several mentioned, never the point of the post.

## Tone

Write as a colleague who has set up hiring at a small company and is telling the reader how to do it in the time the reader has. The attitude is practical, even-handed, and on the side of everyone at the table: the reader, the candidate, and the new hire. It takes hiring seriously without raising its voice, and it never sells.

### Toward the reader

**Write to one person.** The reader is "you": someone hiring at a startup or small business, often without an HR team, with more to do than time to do it. Say so when it matters. "You don’t always know when you’ll need to fill a role—especially at a startup or small business. Ideally, you’ll have plenty of notice, but that’s not always the case" (*Talent Acquisition vs. Recruitment*). "If you don’t have an HR team, the hiring manager should be responsible for following their report’s onboarding" (*Employee Onboarding*).

**Assume competence and good intent.** The gap the post fills is a missing system, not a missing conscience. When companies fall short, the explanation is workload, and the remedy follows in the same breath. "There’s not always a negative intention behind failing to send rejection letters" and "Hiring managers often don't send rejection letters because they are too busy and understaffed" lead to "Most of the problems mentioned above are easily avoidable. All you need is a system to screen, track and communicate with potential new hires" (*Job Rejection Emails*).

**Neither flatter nor scold.** Do not compliment the reader. Do not tell the reader off either. The reader may be biased, rushed, or months behind on rejection emails; say so plainly and move to the fix. "Whether or not you know it, first impression bias can influence your hiring decisions, sometimes even before your first face-to-face" (*First Impression Bias*).

**Remind, as a peer would.** When an instruction is easy to dismiss, admit it and insist anyway. "Having these processes set up sounds obvious, but many businesses (especially busy startups) don’t remember to prioritize onboarding and documentation" (*Employee Onboarding*). "Don't forget to proofread the email before sending it; sounds obvious, but many hiring managers have fallen victim to a typo!" (*Job Rejection Emails*).

**Count the cost.** Say what the advice takes and what the reader can afford. "It takes a dedicated effort to set up initially, but it’s well worth your time" (*Employee Onboarding*). "Not everyone has the resources to put a team behind their job rejection email overhaul, but you can draw inspiration from Shira’s excellent work here" (*Job Rejection Emails*). A section can open by conceding the burden it is about to lighten: "The points mentioned above make writing your rejection email sound like a lot of work" (*Job Rejection Emails*).

**Trust the reader to adapt.** Offer suggestions as experiments and leave the fitting to the reader. "Typically new employees start on a Monday, but why not try starting them later in the week?" and "Play around to see what works with your company culture" (*Employee Onboarding*). "No company is the same, so you’ll want to focus on questions that make sense for your business" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*).

**Lower your voice in parentheses.** Asides are where the writing sounds most like one person talking to another: a forward reference, a small confidence, a permission. "(We’ll cover what questions to ask in a moment.)" and "(more on this later too)" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*); "(reading this article is a good starting point)" (*First Impression Bias*); "(this isn’t necessary, but could help new hires feel part of the team so that first day is less intimidating)" (*Employee Onboarding*). Keep them short and keep them occasional.

**Stand with the reader, not above.** On bias, "we" means everyone, writer included. "Everyone has biases. Whether it’s the clothes you like to wear, the music you listen to, or the food you eat, we all have cultural preferences that inform our opinions—and occasionally lead to errors in judgment" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*). "Everyone creates first impressions. It’s our brains’ clever way of using shortcuts to make quick judgments. But they’re not always right" (*First Impression Bias*).

Otherwise "we" is Polymer, the people who wrote the post: "we’ll show you", "we’d recommend", "At Polymer, we’ve created this template interview scoring matrix" (*Job Rejection Emails*). There is no "I".

### Toward the subject

**State the stakes once, plainly, with a number.** Hiring has consequences, and the opening says so and moves on. "Get onboarding right, and you'll set employees up for long-term success. Get it wrong, and your next hire may prematurely leave for something better. This isn't anecdotal. Around 3.2 million people quit their job in June 2026 alone" (*Employee Onboarding*). Note "may". The consequence is real, and the writing does not pretend it is certain.

**Keep the candidate in the room.** Candidates and employees are people, and the writing keeps crossing to their side of the table. "Candidates get nervous, forget things, and may not give you the information you need to determine if they’re right for the job" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*). "Nobody enjoys receiving a rejection letter" (*Job Rejection Emails*). "They were the newbie once too, which makes them relatable" (*Employee Onboarding*). When it helps, put the reader in the candidate's place: "Put yourself in the shoes of a job seeker. If you attend an interview that feels unprepared and unstructured, it’s hard to gather your thoughts" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*). "Put yourself in the candidate's shoes and think about what you'd like to know if you received this message" (*Job Rejection Emails*).

**Argue decency and payoff together, and preach neither.** Treating people well is right, and it pays; the writing makes both cases and does not choose. "Sending job rejection emails isn’t compulsory, but it’s a respectful way to communicate with people" stands a few sections from Virgin Media's $5 million a year in lost revenue (*Job Rejection Emails*). Diversity enters by its results: "Diversity is ideal for innovation" (*First Impression Bias*). Bias is a shortcut, not a sin: "First impression biases themselves are not inherently bad. They’re simply a limitation in our brains’ way of forming patterns (human information processing)" and "The danger happens when we’re unaware of where these first impressions can go wrong" (*First Impression Bias*).

**Say what the advice cannot do.** Give each method its limits, in a section of their own where they deserve one. "Although using an interview matrix has a lot of benefits, there are some hurdles to be aware of" opens a run of drawbacks that includes "Reviewing past behavior to determine future behavior isn't always accurate" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*). "Talent acquisition, done well, is a full-time job" is the drawback of the approach the post goes on to recommend (*Talent Acquisition vs. Recruitment*).

**Then take a position.** After both sides, say what you would do. "Despite the added time and preparation, we’d recommend using a complex rating scale over a very simple one (with five options ideal for most businesses)" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*). "Unless you’re hiring for a temporary position, recruitment is often a short-term fix to a long-term problem" (*Talent Acquisition vs. Recruitment*). "Writing a job rejection email is a crucial part of the hiring process, so don’t skip it" (*Job Rejection Emails*). The recommendation is firm; the hedges ("often", "for most businesses") sit where the facts put them.

**Match certainty to knowledge.** What to do is a command. What will happen takes "may", "could", or "likely". "Add up the total score, compare all candidate’s scores, and you’ll see who ranks the highest. This is likely the best person for the job" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*). Commit to the method; be honest about the outcome.

### Toward Polymer

**The advice stands without the product.** A reader with a spreadsheet and an email account could follow any of these posts to the end; the interview scoring template is a Google Sheet. Polymer enters late, as one example of its category. "A hiring platform, like Polymer, helps companies track and manage the entire recruitment process" and "Take a look at Polymer as an example" (*First Impression Bias*). "A tracking system like Polymer is an easy way to track everyone in your pipeline and keep them informed about the process" (*Talent Acquisition vs. Recruitment*).

**Name what it does, not how good it is.** "Polymer can even deliver documents or assessments with templated messages when candidates move to the next stage" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*). "With Polymer, for example, you can easily collaborate with your team to review job descriptions before they go live" (*First Impression Bias*). No superlatives, no competitors, no claims about the market.

**Close with an invitation.** The last section restates the post's point first; Polymer, when it appears, comes after, and the offer is a line. "If you’re thinking about hiring more fairly, give Polymer a try" (*First Impression Bias*). "Take a look at how Polymer can help and start simplifying your hiring" (*Job Rejection Emails*). "Get started here" (*Talent Acquisition vs. Recruitment*).

### How the tone shifts with what is being said

**Opening.** Brisk. A question the reader has already asked, or a problem stated as fact, then a number, then a plain statement of what the post will do. "What’s the first thing you look for when hiring a new candidate? Their skills? Their experience? Their qualifications?" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*). "In the current struggle to attract top talent, employers must rethink their recruitment communication strategies" (*Job Rejection Emails*). The promise is literal, not teasing: "In this article, we’ll show you what a behavioral interview scoring matrix is, how to use it effectively, and give you a downloadable matrix template you can use to get the ball rolling" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*).

**Defining.** Flat and declarative, in one sentence, first thing under the heading. No hedge, no color. "Recruitment is the process of filling vacant positions at your company" (*Talent Acquisition vs. Recruitment*). "A job rejection email is a courteous response to job applicants who don’t pass your initial screening and interview processes" (*Job Rejection Emails*). "First impression bias causes people to form a quick and incomplete impression of someone based on what they see and hear in the first few seconds of meeting" (*First Impression Bias*).

**Instructing.** The imperative, short, with a signpost before the steps: "Here’s what you need to do" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*). "Tally the scores" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*). "Don’t skip these meetings" (*Employee Onboarding*). "Cut to the chase and tell the candidate why they didn't get the job" (*Job Rejection Emails*). "Now you know the importance of talent acquisition, implement a plan" (*Talent Acquisition vs. Recruitment*). Where there is no single right answer, say so and hand it back: "Who should do new employee check-ins? That depends on your organizational structure" (*Employee Onboarding*).

**Illustrating.** "Imagine", "Let’s say", "Say your company". A named role, a few concrete details, a short walk through what happens, and a question or a one-line verdict to land it. "Imagine you’re interviewing a job candidate for a remote position. You spot from their resume that they’re from your hometown across the country, attended the same university as you, and wear glasses" ends in "Your positive first impression has unfairly influenced the job interview" and then "Is this just because you have something in common and got along well the first time you met? Or are they actually the right person for the job?" (*First Impression Bias*). "This disagreement happened a long time ago. Who’s to say the candidate would respond the same way now?" (*Behavioral Interview Scoring Matrix*).

**Qualifying.** The limit follows the instruction in the same paragraph, with "though", "that said", or a mid-sentence "however". "Don’t overdo it, though; this could make your employee feel micromanaged" (*Employee Onboarding*). "That said, when you’re actively recruiting for open positions, it’s important to continually assess your company’s requirements" (*Talent Acquisition vs. Recruitment*). "Sometimes, however, new hires don’t work out. Be careful not to fire too fast" (*Employee Onboarding*).

**Showing bad practice.** Present it and let it speak. "As an example of what not to do, here’s a post that went viral on Twitter for all the wrong reasons:" introduces the landscaping email, and the post does not pile on (*Job Rejection Emails*). Intel's old template is introduced with its "rejection email crimes like lacking empathy and containing broken links" (*Job Rejection Emails*): "crimes" is light, and the specifics do the criticizing.

**Joking.** Rare, dry, and at the expense of an effect or a situation, never of the reader or the candidate. "If your candidate brings a light show and pyrotechnics to the interview, expect the framing effect to be in play" and, after Gordon Ramsay's chicken nugget, "In reality, it’s the same cold nugget" (*First Impression Bias*). A one-word paragraph can carry it: "Win-win" (*Employee Onboarding*). Lightness comes from the phrasing, not the punctuation.

**Citing.** Name the source, give the figure, and say what it means for the reader in the same sentence. "A University of Warwick study found employees are 12% more productive in positive working conditions, so it’s important to design your onboarding process with long-term happiness in mind" (*Employee Onboarding*). A statistic is never left to impress on its own.

**Turning to hard work.** A line of sympathy, then the method. "Letting people down is never easy" opens the templates section (*Job Rejection Emails*). "Breaking the bad news to a candidate isn't such a struggle if you know what to include in your message" (*Job Rejection Emails*).

**Closing.** Restate the point in the post's own terms, then the product, then the invitation. "Onboarding isn’t a checklist of requirements to rush through. It’s a way to continuously nurture your employees so you can create an enviable company culture" (*Employee Onboarding*).

### What produces it on the page

- Contractions throughout: "you’ll", "don’t", "isn’t", "we’d".
- Plain idioms, one at a time: "get the ball rolling", "cut to the chase", "show your new hire the ropes", "on their radar", "the newbie". Never stacked.
- A little house vocabulary, used without irony: "A-players" and "top talent" for the people the reader wants to hire.
- Rhetorical questions to open a post, open a section, or land an example.
- One-sentence paragraphs to land a point: "In reality, it’s the same cold nugget." "Your positive first impression has unfairly influenced the job interview." "Win-win."
- Parentheses for the aside, not for the argument.
- Terms defined in passing, without ceremony: "customer relationship management system (CRM)", "standard operations procedures (SOPs)".

### What breaks it

- Flattering the reader, or warming up with "let's be honest" and "we've all been there".
- Alarm. Stakes are stated and left; the reader is never frightened into the method.
- Preaching about fairness or diversity. Make the case by results and by plain respect, then move on.
- Superlatives for Polymer, comparisons with competitors, or Polymer before the advice.
- Jokes about candidates, new hires, or the reader's mistakes.
- "I". The writer is "we", and "we" is Polymer or everyone.
- Hedging an instruction ("you might want to consider") or asserting an outcome ("this will find you the best candidate").
- A statistic with no "so".
- Exclamation marks as a substitute for lightness in the phrasing.

## Writing Style

The posts speak to one reader: a person at a growing company who has to hire and has little time to read about it. The writer addresses that person as "you" from the first sentence to the last and never steps back to discuss employers in the third person. The writer's own voice is "we," the Polymer team, and it appears in the introduction to promise what the article covers, in the body to recommend one approach over another, and at the close to offer the product. The voice is a confident colleague's, encouraging rather than scolding, and as ready to take the candidate's side as the employer's.

Word choice is plain. The posts prefer the short, common word to the long one and the concrete noun to the abstract: a hire, a scorecard, a first day, an email, rather than a process, an initiative, or a touchpoint. Verbs carry the sentences, and in instructional sections most are imperatives. Contractions are standard. The posts use the vocabulary of hiring freely, spell out each acronym at first use, and admit a familiar idiom now and then if it is brief. Spelling is American. Each statistic names its figure and its source in one sentence, and the first arrives within the opening paragraphs to establish that the problem is real.

Sentences are short to medium and mostly one clause. A two-part sentence sets a condition and then gives the instruction, or states the rule and then supplies the case. A few moves recur. A question opens the article or turns a section. A hypothetical scenario invites the reader to imagine a specific role (a copywriter, a sales manager, a new designer) and follows it for a few sentences. A parenthetical aside points forward to a later section or qualifies a claim without breaking the main clause. A single short sentence, occasionally a fragment, follows a longer explanation and closes the thought.

Paragraphs are one to three sentences, each on one idea, and a paragraph of one sentence is common. The reader skims, and the white space is part of the argument. Lists hold only discrete items, steps in order or things to include; an item that needs explaining opens with a short lead phrase ending in a period, and the explanation follows. A sentence ending in a colon announces every list, and the first sentence of every section says what the section will do before it does it. Tables appear where two things are set side by side.

The rhythm is claim, then evidence or example, then instruction. The sentence after a general statement supplies a number, a named company, or a scenario that shows it. Headings are questions or imperatives, and numbered steps carry the how-to sections. Each article opens by stating the stakes, defines its subject in its own section, weighs advantages against drawbacks, and ends under a heading that restates the thesis as an instruction, followed by a brief close offering Polymer as the tool that makes the advice practical. Polymer also appears in the body, named plainly as one tool a reader might use, never as the reason the advice is true.

### Sentence structure

The posts build nearly everything from the plain statement: one subject, one active verb, one object, and at most one dependent clause. The subject is the reader, a person in the hiring process, or the thing being explained, and the verb names what it does. Subject and verb sit close together, and the point of the sentence sits at the end. Most run under twenty words; one that reaches thirty has grown a trailing result phrase and an aside, and the next sentence is short. A sentence of two or three words sometimes closes a thought.

The instruction comes next in frequency. It opens with a bare imperative verb. A reason, when needed, follows in the same sentence, joined by so or as a trailing participial phrase stating the result. A caution follows the main clause after a semicolon or the word though. The shortest instructions close a post: a verb, an object, and nothing else.

The condition-and-consequence sentence puts a situation in a clause beginning with if or when and what follows from it in the main clause. The condition usually comes first; when it trails, it marks an exception. The consequence is often softened with may, might, or could, since the posts describe tendencies, not certainties. Two are sometimes set side by side in matching form, one for the good case and one for the bad.

The scenario sentence invites the reader into a small story. It opens with imagine or let's say, puts the reader in a hiring situation, and narrates it in the present tense over two or three short sentences. It ends with a sentence naming the bias, mistake, or lesson the story was built to show.

Questions do two jobs. At the top of a post, two or three in a row, some of them fragments, name the problem the reader came with. Inside a section, a single question states a doubt and the next sentence answers it, or a short run of questions lists what the reader must decide.

The definition opens any section whose heading asks what something is. The term is the subject; the verb is usually a form of to be, followed by a category noun and a relative clause that says what the thing does or who it is for. When the term itself acts, the verb names the action. A parenthetical supplies any alternative name.

The statistic sentence usually names its source first, then the number, then what the number measures. The next sentence draws the consequence for the reader; the figure never sits unexplained.

Signpost sentences say what the next stretch of text will do. They are short, use we or here, and often end in a colon that opens a list. A list item is a bare noun phrase, an imperative sentence, or a short label followed by one to three sentences of explanation; a list keeps to one kind.

The joinery is the same throughout. Sentences begin freely with but, and, so, or plus. Asides go in parentheses or between dashes rather than between commas. Two closely related statements share a semicolon when the second explains or qualifies the first. Contractions appear everywhere, and the passive voice appears only when the actor does not matter.

### Paragraph structure

Paragraphs in these posts are short. Most run one to three sentences, and a paragraph of a single sentence is routine. Each paragraph makes one move. Its first sentence names that move, whether a claim, an example, or an instruction, and any sentence after it supports the first. A thought with two parts becomes two paragraphs. The single-sentence paragraph lands a point, turns a corner, or closes a section. Every section is assembled from a small set of paragraph types, each with a fixed job.

A **definition paragraph** opens any section whose heading asks what something is. The term is the subject of the first sentence, the predicate says what it is, and a second sentence at most says what it covers. The next paragraph separates the term from its nearest neighbor.

An **evidence paragraph** follows a claim and supports it with a number. It names the source in the sentence, states the figure, and stops. The paragraph after it draws the implication for the reader's own hiring.

A **scenario paragraph** runs a hypothetical in the second person. It asks the reader to picture a candidate, a new hire, or a hiring committee, then describes what happens in two or three sentences. A **consequence paragraph** always follows, often a single sentence, and names the lesson: the bias at work, the cost incurred, or the decision gone wrong.

A **lead-in paragraph** is one sentence, usually ending in a colon, that points at what comes next: a list, a table, or an image. A **wrap-up paragraph** of one or two sentences then states what the reader has just seen and why it matters.

An **instruction paragraph** uses the imperative. A procedure runs across several of them, one step per paragraph, joined by order words.

A **caveat paragraph** qualifies the paragraph before it. It opens with a contrast word, warns against overdoing the advice just given, and is rarely more than two sentences.

A **case paragraph** reports what a named company did, across two or three short paragraphs: the situation, the action, and the result. A **product paragraph** names Polymer and ties one feature to the point just made; it sits at the end of a section or of the post, never mid-argument.

Movement from one paragraph to the next follows a few patterns. The most common is logical order: claim, then evidence, then implication; or statement, then example, then instruction. Connectives mark the joins: order words through a procedure, contrast words into a caveat, result phrases into an implication. Repetition joins paragraphs without connectives: the noun that ends one paragraph becomes the subject of the next, or a question closes one paragraph and the first sentence of the next answers it. Explicit pointers join distant paragraphs: a parenthetical promise to return to a topic later, and a back-reference when an earlier example returns.

Headings do much of the moving. A subsection opens with a paragraph that addresses its heading directly, with no transition from the subsection before, and ends with a paragraph that sums the subsection up or points to the next. Every post opens with a **hook paragraph**, a question, an antithesis, or a claim that an evidence paragraph immediately supports, and ends its introduction with a **roadmap paragraph** stating what the article will show. The post closes with two or three short paragraphs that restate the thesis and offer Polymer.

### Framing patterns Polymer uses

**Stakes before definition.** A post opens with what the reader loses by getting the subject wrong, pins the loss to a survey figure, and only then defines the term. Use this at the top of every post and at the head of any section a reader might skip.

**The reader does the hiring.** Every instruction addresses the person who will carry it out. The reader posts the job, runs the interview, writes the email, and plans the first week. The posts assume no HR team stands between the reader and the task, say so when a step would normally be delegated, and add that it can be handed off once the company grows. Use this throughout; write what the reader should do, not what a company or a recruiter should do.

**The imagined scene.** To explain an abstract mechanism, the post builds a short situation with concrete, slightly arbitrary details and puts the reader inside it: a candidate who shares the reader's hometown, a portfolio in the wrong genre, a committee swayed by its loudest member. The scene plays out to its consequence. Use this for biases and process flaws, wherever a definition alone would not land.

**With and without.** A practice is defined against its absence or its nearest neighbor: an interview with a scorecard and one without, what onboarding covers that training does not, a closed question beside its open form, a rejection line before and after rewriting. Use this to separate a term from one the reader may confuse it with, and to argue for a practice by showing the mess it prevents.

**The candidate's seat.** When the benefit of a practice to the reader is indirect, the post asks the reader to switch chairs and go through the process as the applicant does: the interview that wanders, the application that never gets a reply. Use this to justify courtesy, structure, and consistency.

**Raise the objection, then answer it.** No recommendation is presented as free. A heading asks whether the thing is actually bad or has drawbacks; a pros list is followed by a cons list; a recommended scale is admitted to cost more interviewing time. The post then recommends it anyway and says why. Use this after the case is made and before the close, and wherever a small team would reasonably push back.

**Someone else already does this.** A recommendation is framed as something a recognizable company already does, with one specific detail: a channel created for new hires, a welcome box, an email rewritten and what changed. Studies and experiments play the same role for claims about human behavior. Use this when advice could otherwise sound invented, and keep to one detail per example.

**The chore reframed as an investment.** Where readers treat the subject as admin, the post names that smaller view and replaces it with a larger one: a rejection email seeds a future talent pool, onboarding is the first year of retention, a hiring plan breaks the hire-churn-rehire loop. The payoff is deferred and larger than the task. Use this to open or close the case for anything readers are tempted to skip.

**The missing system.** The obstacle to any practice is the lack of a system, not a lack of will: rejection emails go unsent because nothing tracks who needs one; interviews drift because nothing fixes the questions. Polymer then appears as that system. Use this at the close of every post, and in the body only where a step maps directly onto a feature.

### Headers and structure

A post has one title, an introduction with no heading, four to nine main sections, and a closing section. This guide calls an H2 a main section, an H3 a subsection, and an H4 an item heading. Most main sections divide into subsections; a subsection divides into item headings only when its items each need a paragraph or two.

The title names the topic in the words a reader would type into a search box. It takes one of three shapes: a how-to phrase naming the action the reader wants to take; a question asking what the topic is; or the keyword phrase, a colon, and a label for what the post offers, such as a guide or a template. The title is in title case. Every other heading is in sentence case.

The introduction runs four to six short paragraphs. It opens with a hook: a question, a two-sided claim, or a statistic from a named source. It states what is at stake for the reader's company and ends with a one-sentence paragraph announcing what the post will show, teach, or provide. That announcement is the last thing before the first main section.

The first main section defines the topic, and its heading is a question asking what the topic is, with the full keyword phrase in it. When a post compares two things, the heading names both and asks what the difference is. The definition section may carry one subsection answering a follow-up question, such as what a related term means.

The middle main sections carry the substance. Their headings are long phrases that repeat the topic noun rather than a pronoun or a shortened form, so each makes sense on its own. They take a few recurring forms: a question asking why or when the reader should do something; a how-to phrase; a count followed by the noun, as in a numbered set of stages or templates; or a noun phrase naming a resource, a company's example, or a set of metrics. The usual order is definition, then why it matters, then the steps or the breakdown, then supporting material such as a template, a case study, or a pros-and-cons comparison. Where the product applies, one paragraph in a middle section names Polymer.

Every main section opens with at least one paragraph before its first subsection, saying what the subsections will cover. Paragraphs run one to three sentences, and a section often points forward to a later one in a parenthetical. Subsection headings are shorter than main-section headings and drop the keyword phrase, relying on the main heading above them for context. Sibling subsections share one grammatical form. Under a how-to section they are imperatives addressed to the reader, sometimes numbered. Under a section that lists things, such as biases or scenarios, they are noun phrases naming each item. Under a comparison they are labels for the pros and the cons. Item headings appear only inside a pros-and-cons subsection or a long tips subsection; each is a short sentence or imperative naming one advantage, drawback, or tip, followed by one to three paragraphs.

The last main section closes the post. Its heading is an imperative that tells the reader what to do with what they have read, tied to their business or team, never a word such as conclusion. The section runs two to four short paragraphs: it restates the benefit, then names Polymer and invites the reader to try it.

### Avoid these terms

- Basic, lightweight, bargain, budget, stripped-down, entry-level, or "an ATS only for very small companies." Polymer is not the cheap option and is not easier to use because it does less. It is easier to use because the product is intentionally focused.
- "Simple" for the product itself. Polymer makes hiring simple. The product is not the simple thing.
- "One-click" distribution, "immediately distributed," "posts everywhere," or any wording that says publishing a job puts it in front of candidates at once. Google Jobs is on by default when a job publishes, and Google indexes the post on its own schedule, which can take up to seven days. LinkedIn Limited Listings are included at no extra cost, LinkedIn approval is not guaranteed, and a listing appears hours after publishing and only when the integration is configured.
- Distribution as a selling point. It is a weak point. State it as a fact where a post needs it and move on.
- A headcount number for the reader. "Growing team" carries the positioning without one.
- A competitor's own marketing language. Competitors can be described as cluttered.
- The vocabulary listed under Writing Style.

### Product-specific terminology

- **Polymer**, one word, capital P.
- **Plato**, Polymer's AI candidate reviewer. "Plato AI" on the site. Auto-reviews are turned on per role. A review gives each requirement in the job description one of three verdicts (clear evidence, related evidence, no evidence) and the candidate one of five fit levels (Excellent, Good, Mixed, Weak, Poor), with the reasoning shown. Name, location, and email are removed before scoring. Every plan includes a monthly allowance of Plato credits. Plato is one capability inside Polymer, never the identity of the product.
- **Hiring stages** are the stages of the pipeline. The defaults are Inbox, Screen, Interview, Decide, Offer, Hired, and Archived. Teams rename, reorder, add, and delete the stages between Inbox and Hired.
- **Hiring team** is the set of people on a job. Account roles are Owner, Admin, Member, and Interviewer.
- **Candidate reviews** are scored, written feedback visible to the hiring team. **Review templates** are reusable review content. A **review request** sends one candidate's **Review kit** to anyone, on the hiring team or not.
- **Comments** are shared with the hiring team. **Private notes** are visible only to the person who wrote them.
- **Message templates** are reusable candidate messages with placeholders. **Hiring stage message automations** send a template on their own when a candidate enters a stage.
- **Job board** is the branded board every account gets at signup, on a custom domain if the team wants one. **External Listings** is the per-job setting for Google Jobs, LinkedIn Limited Listings, and X Hiring.
- **Application form** is per job, with standard fields and **custom questions**. A **hiring document** is a per-job template copied to each candidate on apply.
- **Plans** are Starter, Growth, and Scale. Every plan has the complete feature set, unlimited users, and unlimited candidates. Plans differ by how many jobs can be published at once and by monthly Plato credits. The 14-day free trial starts when the first job publishes.

## AEO and SEO Writing Conventions

**Structured for AI citation:**
- Each H2 section stands on its own. A reader who lands on one section gets a complete answer.
- When a statistic is used, name its source in the same sentence.
- Use a table for facts that compare and a list for facts that enumerate.

**FAQ section:**
Every post ends with a FAQ section. The writer supplies the questions and answers as `faq_items` and does not write them into the body. The engine appends them after the body as an H2 reading "Frequently asked questions" with one H3 per question and a paragraph answer under each, and the site renders that section as an accordion and builds FAQPage structured data from it. The first sentence of each answer answers the question.

**Titles and the description:**
- A post has two titles. The page title, shown in the browser tab and in search results, is under 65 characters and contains the keyword. The editorial title, the H1 on the page, can be longer and must contain the keyword.
- The meta description is also the excerpt readers see under each title on the blog index and under related posts. 155 to 160 characters, includes the keyword, compelling enough to earn the click. Write it knowing readers will see it.

**Internal links:**
- Link to existing Polymer blog posts and product pages where they help the reader, at least two per post, and only to real URLs on polymer.co.

**Structured data:**
- The site builds Article, BreadcrumbList, and FAQPage structured data from the post's fields and its FAQ section. The writer emits no JSON-LD.
