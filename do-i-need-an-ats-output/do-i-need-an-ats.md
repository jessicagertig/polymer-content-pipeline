# Do You Need an Applicant Tracking System? An Honest Decision Guide

mainImageAlt: Founder reviewing a hiring pipeline on a laptop screen, deciding whether to adopt an applicant tracking system

Most companies get an ATS one hire too late. By the time a strong candidate has gone quiet, feedback has scattered across inboxes, and the team disagrees on where things stand, the hire has already cost more time than it needed to. Whether you're at that point yet, or close enough to get ahead of it, is what this guide is here to answer.

An applicant tracking system is hiring software that gives you one central place where job applications are submitted, then tracks each candidate through every stage of your hiring process, from inbox to hired. Each candidate gets one record with their resume, every message you've exchanged, your team's feedback, and a log of every stage they've moved through. That record lives in the system, not in a spreadsheet someone forgot to update.

This guide covers how a manual process breaks down and when, the honest thresholds where a spreadsheet stops working, what an ATS fixes and what it doesn't, and what to look for if you're a small company evaluating options.

## How a hiring pipeline works without an ATS

Before looking at the thresholds, it helps to understand what breaks down and why. When hiring runs through a shared inbox and a spreadsheet, the process depends on everyone remembering to update the same document at the same time. Applications arrive in different places — whoever's email they happened to land in. Someone downloads a resume and saves it somewhere the rest of the team can't find it. The spreadsheet shows five candidates in "Interview" but doesn't say who interviewed them, what anyone thought, or whether a decision was made.

That's manageable for one open role with one decision-maker and a handful of applicants. The moment the complexity grows — a second role opens, a second reviewer joins, or volume picks up — the patchwork falls apart. Information gets lost between people, candidates don't hear back, and the team can't get aligned because there's no shared record of what happened. An ATS replaces that patchwork with a single system where every application, message, piece of feedback, and stage change lives in one place.

## The honest thresholds for when to get an ATS

The question most founders and hiring managers want answered is whether they've reached the point where a spreadsheet is costing them more than it saves. There are five concrete signals worth watching for.

### You regularly have more than one role open at once

A single open role is manageable with a shared inbox and a well-maintained spreadsheet. Once two or three roles are running at the same time, the complexity multiplies. Candidates apply to the wrong job. Reviewers aren't sure which pipeline they're looking at. Someone updates one tab and forgets the other. The tracking problem that one role barely creates becomes three overlapping tracking problems — and the fix is no longer a better spreadsheet. Industry guidance puts this trigger clearly: a small business needs an ATS once it has more than one role open at a time.

### More than one person reviews or interviews candidates

As soon as a second person is involved in evaluating candidates — a co-founder, a department head, a future colleague — the process depends on information flowing reliably between people. Email chains work until they don't. Someone gets dropped from a thread. Feedback sits in a reply the hiring manager never saw. Two reviewers form an opinion of the same candidate without knowing what the other thought. An ATS gives everyone a shared record where feedback is attached to the candidate, not buried in a thread, so the debrief takes ten minutes instead of thirty.

### Applicants arrive from more than one source

When you post the same role on LinkedIn, Indeed, and your own site, applications arrive through separate channels. Without a central inbox, candidates get missed, and you have no clean way to tell which source sends the best applicants. That second problem is easy to underestimate: losing track of where someone came from isn't just inconvenient, it makes it impossible to get better at sourcing over time.

### Candidates are going quiet because responses are slow

Speed matters in hiring. When a good candidate applies and doesn't hear back for a week, they've often moved on — to another company's process, or simply to other priorities. Manual processes slow everything down: the confirmation email doesn't go out until someone remembers, the scheduling message waits until the hiring manager has a free moment, the rejection doesn't happen until after the offer is accepted. That delay has a real cost. [SHRM research](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/candidate-resentment-rise) found that 34% of job candidates reported not hearing back from employers two months after applying. An ATS with automated messaging eliminates most of that silence, because confirmations, stage-change notifications, and rejections go out without anyone drafting them each time.

### You need a defensible record of hiring decisions

Equal employment opportunity requirements mean that any employer, regardless of size, may need to show why a candidate was rejected and when. If that record lives only in someone's memory or in a spreadsheet that's been edited several times, it isn't a record. An ATS keeps a timestamped log of every stage move, every message, and every piece of reviewer feedback — which is what a defensible record looks like. This matters most when volume is high, but it applies to any employer who wants to hire fairly and be able to demonstrate it.

## The five-to-ten-hire rule and what it misses

A widely cited threshold puts the ATS trigger at roughly five to ten hires per year — the point at which most teams are regularly juggling multiple open roles, multiple reviewers, and candidates coming from more than one place. Some guides set the bar higher, at twenty open positions annually, with the logic that below that volume the ROI may not justify the subscription cost. Neither number is wrong, but both can mislead if taken too literally.

The right figure depends less on how many people you hire annually and more on how many roles are open at the same time, how many people are involved in each decision, and how much applicant volume each role generates. A startup that makes eight hires a year by filling one role at a time, with a single decision-maker and a trickle of applicants, may be fine with a spreadsheet longer than a 50-person company running three concurrent searches with panel interviews and referrals coming in from several directions.

Company headcount is similarly unreliable as a proxy. A five-person company planning to double in six months has a different hiring problem than a 100-person company with low turnover and one or two hires a year. The trigger is the complexity of the process — not the size of the team you've already built. If your process is currently straightforward and you can confidently say every applicant gets a reply and no one has been lost between email threads, you haven't hit the threshold yet. When that stops being true, you have.

## What you give up by waiting too long

The cost of staying with a manual process past the right threshold shows up in a few predictable ways. The most visible is the missed candidate: a strong applicant who went quiet because the scheduling email was delayed, or who accepted another offer while your team was still trying to coordinate feedback. By the time the ATS is in place, that hire is gone. The search restarts, the role sits empty longer, and everyone involved spends more time on hiring than they should.

The financial cost is real. [SHRM's 2025 benchmarking data](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat) puts the average nonexecutive cost per hire at $5,475. That figure covers advertising, recruiter time, interview time, and administrative overhead — and it doesn't include the cost of the role being unfilled for weeks, or the cost of a bad hire made because the evaluation process wasn't rigorous enough to catch the right person.

Getting the system in place before the pressure is on also means your team learns it on a lower-stakes search. You build your hiring stages, write your message templates, and work out how the pipeline maps to your actual process — before those things matter urgently. When the important search comes along, the system is already set up and everyone knows how to use it.

## The benefits that compound over time

The most immediate gain from an ATS is time. When an application arrives, the system creates the candidate record, sends the confirmation, and places the person in the first stage of the pipeline — without anyone doing it manually. When the team moves a candidate forward, an automated message can go out the same day. The team's feedback is attached to the candidate record rather than scattered across email, so alignment happens faster and decisions get made more cleanly.

Speed follows naturally from that structure. [HR Brew reported](https://www.hr-brew.com/stories/2024/10/22/new-ta-and-recruiter-tech-is-making-it-easier-to-fill-open-requisitions-survey-reveals) that companies using recruiting technology cut their median time to fill from 48 days in 2023 to 41 days in 2024 — a seven-day reduction. In industries with tight candidate pools, the difference was sharper: education saw a 27% reduction in time to hire, and retail saw 20%.

Consistency is a less obvious benefit, but it compounds. When every candidate goes through the same stages, gets the same information, and receives a response at each transition, the process becomes predictable and professional. Candidates who don't get the role leave with a better impression — which matters for referrals and for your reputation as an employer over the long run. Teams that review candidates against the same criteria, using a shared rubric, make decisions that are easier to explain and harder to second-guess later. If you want a framework for how to structure that evaluation, a [behavioral interview scoring matrix](/blog/behavioral-interview-scoring-matrix) gives you the foundation for scoring every candidate the same way.

## What an ATS doesn't fix

An ATS organizes your hiring process. It doesn't design it. If you don't have clear hiring stages, the system gives you defaults — but you still have to decide what your process looks like. If your team disagrees about what a good candidate looks like for a given role, structured feedback templates help surface that disagreement, but you have to resolve it. If your job descriptions aren't attracting the right applicants, the ATS will collect the wrong ones more efficiently.

There's also a scope limit worth acknowledging. An ATS is built for active candidates — people who apply to your jobs. It doesn't replace proactive sourcing, referral programs, or the relationships that surface candidates before they're looking. If your biggest recruiting challenge is finding candidates at all, rather than managing the ones who apply, an ATS addresses only part of the problem. The difference between managing inbound applicants and building a proactive talent pipeline is the difference between recruitment and talent acquisition — and those two things call for different approaches. For a full picture of that distinction, [this guide on talent acquisition vs. recruitment](/blog/talent-acquisition-vs-recruitment) covers it thoroughly.

Nor does an ATS replace good judgment. AI-assisted screening — which scores each application against the job's requirements automatically — is genuinely useful for high-volume roles, and it removes some of the most time-consuming early-stage work. But it surfaces signals, not verdicts. The hiring decision still belongs to the people on your team.

## Applicant tracking system for small companies: what to look for

Not every ATS is built for a growing team. Enterprise systems carry features, complexity, and pricing models designed for large recruiting departments, and those don't translate well to a team of ten or twenty people hiring a handful of roles each year. Here are the criteria worth anchoring on when you're evaluating options as a small company.

### Pricing that doesn't punish growth

Some ATS platforms charge per user seat, which means every hiring manager, interviewer, and reviewer you add to the process raises the monthly bill. That creates a real tension: the more people you involve in hiring decisions, the more the system costs — right when you're trying to keep overhead low. Look for a system that charges by the number of active job posts rather than by user count, so you can involve your whole team without the math getting complicated every time a new stakeholder joins a search.

### A setup that takes hours, not weeks

Enterprise recruiting platforms are built for dedicated HR teams with time to implement, configure, and train on a complex system. A growing company without a recruiting department can't absorb that investment. The right system for a small team is one you can set up in an afternoon — job board, hiring stages, application form, and basic templates — and start using on a real search the same week. If a vendor's onboarding timeline is measured in weeks, that's a signal the system was built for someone else.

### The features a small team actually uses

The list of must-have features for a small team is shorter than vendors often suggest. What you need is a careers page that reflects your brand, a visual pipeline that shows where each candidate stands, shared notes and scored reviews so the team can align, email templates so responses go out quickly and consistently, and interview scheduling that doesn't require a back-and-forth email chain. AI-assisted screening is increasingly useful for roles that attract high volumes of applicants, so it's worth checking whether it's included in the base plan or priced as an add-on.

### A candidate experience worth testing

Before committing to any system, apply to your own job on a phone. That is the experience your candidates have. If the form is hard to complete on mobile, long, or confusing, you will lose applicants before they finish. The candidate experience is part of your employer brand, and once you choose the right tool, it is entirely within your control. A system that makes applying frictionless also signals to candidates that your company is organized and worth their time.

## When you genuinely don't need one yet

If you're making fewer than five hires a year, filling one role at a time, with one decision-maker and a manageable volume of applicants from a single source, a shared inbox and a well-structured spreadsheet can work. The key is that every applicant gets a reply, the spreadsheet is kept current by one person, and no candidate falls through a gap between email threads.

That said, the moment any of those conditions change, the complexity changes character. The most useful thing you can do before you hit the threshold is decide in advance what it looks like. If you open more than one role at a time, or bring in more than one interviewer, you move to an ATS. That way, the decision is made before the crunch, not during it — when a good candidate is waiting and there's no time to evaluate systems.

It's also worth considering what else you need. If your growing company needs employee records, onboarding tracking, and time-off management in addition to hiring support, a combined platform may be worth evaluating — not because an ATS falls short on hiring, but because running two separate systems where one would do creates its own overhead. For teams where hiring is the primary pain point, a standalone ATS is the cleaner choice. For teams building out HR infrastructure at the same time, it's a question worth asking before you sign up for anything.

## The trade-offs worth knowing before you decide

Moving from a manual process to an ATS is a small but real investment of time upfront. You'll set up your hiring stages, write your templates, configure your application forms, and decide how roles are structured in the system. For most growing teams, that setup takes a few hours. For a team that has never had a formal process, it may surface questions you haven't answered yet — which stages a candidate moves through, who is responsible at each one, what a rejection looks like — and working through those is worth doing even if it takes longer than expected.

There is also the question of fit. An ATS built for large recruiting departments carries complexity your team doesn't need and may never use. Features you don't use don't hurt you directly, but they add cost and cognitive overhead — and they signal that the system was designed for a different kind of team. The goal is to match the system to the scale of the problem. A tool built for growing companies will generally serve you better than one built down from an enterprise product, even if the enterprise product's feature list looks more impressive on paper. ## Frequently asked questions

### How many hires do I need to make before an ATS is worth it?

Most teams find the value clearly outweighs the cost once they're making five to ten hires a year, or whenever more than one role is open at the same time. Annual hire count matters less than process complexity: if more than one person is involved in evaluating candidates, or applicants are arriving from multiple sources, those are stronger signals than any single number.

### Can a spreadsheet replace an applicant tracking system for a small business?

A spreadsheet works for a single open role with one decision-maker and a manageable number of applicants from one source, as long as every applicant gets a reply and the sheet is kept current. Once you have two roles open simultaneously, multiple reviewers, or candidates arriving from several places, a spreadsheet creates more coordination overhead than it saves.

### What's the difference between an ATS and a job board?

A job board is where candidates find and apply to open roles. An ATS is where your team manages the candidates who apply — tracking them through hiring stages, reviewing their applications, communicating with them, and making decisions. Most modern ATS platforms include a built-in job board, so the two functions are part of the same system.

### Does an ATS work for a startup with no HR department?

Yes — in fact, a startup without dedicated HR staff is often the best fit for a well-designed ATS, because the system does the administrative work that would otherwise fall to the founder or hiring manager. The key is choosing a system built for small teams, one that can be set up quickly and doesn't require a dedicated administrator to maintain.

### Will an ATS help me hire faster?

Generally, yes. Companies using recruiting technology cut their median time to fill by seven days in a recent year, according to HR Brew reporting on industry data, with some sectors seeing reductions of 20% or more. The speed gain comes from automated communications, faster team alignment through shared reviews, and the elimination of the coordination delays that slow manual processes down.

### What features does an ATS need to have for a small business?

The essentials are a branded careers page, a visual hiring pipeline, shared candidate reviews and notes, email templates with automation, and a mobile-ready application form. AI-assisted screening is worth evaluating if you fill roles that attract high applicant volumes. Per-seat pricing and complex implementation are features to avoid, since both add cost and friction that small teams don't need.
