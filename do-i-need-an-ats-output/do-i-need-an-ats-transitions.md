## Final

<p>Most companies get an ATS one hire too late. By the time a strong candidate has gone quiet, feedback has scattered across inboxes, and the team disagrees on where things stand, the hire has already cost more time than it needed to. Whether you're at that point yet, or close enough to get ahead of it, is what this guide is here to answer.</p>
<p>An applicant tracking system is hiring software that gives you one central place where job applications are submitted, then tracks each candidate through every stage of your hiring process, from inbox to hired. Each candidate gets one record with their resume, every message you've exchanged, your team's feedback, and a log of every stage they've moved through. That record lives in the system, not in a spreadsheet someone forgot to update.</p>
<p>This guide covers how a manual process breaks down and when, the honest thresholds where a spreadsheet stops working, what an ATS fixes and what it doesn't, and what to look for if you're a small company evaluating options.</p>
<h2>How a hiring pipeline works without an ATS</h2>
<p>Before looking at the thresholds, it helps to understand what breaks down and why. When hiring runs through a shared inbox and a spreadsheet, the process depends on everyone remembering to update the same document at the same time. Applications land in whoever's email inbox they happened to reach, resumes get downloaded and saved somewhere the rest of the team can't find, and the spreadsheet shows five candidates in "Interview" with no record of who interviewed them, what anyone thought, or whether a decision was made.</p>
<p>That's manageable for one open role with one decision-maker and a handful of applicants. When complexity grows — a second role opens, a second reviewer joins, or volume picks up — the patchwork falls apart. Information gets lost between people, candidates don't hear back, and the team can't get aligned because there's no shared record of what happened. An ATS replaces that patchwork with a single system where every application, message, piece of feedback, and stage change lives in one place.</p>
<h2>The honest thresholds for when to get an ATS</h2>
<p>The question most founders and hiring managers want answered is whether they've reached the point where a spreadsheet is costing them more than it saves. Five concrete signals tell you when that point has arrived.</p>

<h3>You regularly have more than one role open at once</h3>
<p>A single open role is manageable with a shared inbox and a well-maintained spreadsheet. Once two or three roles are running at the same time, the complexity multiplies. Candidates apply to the wrong job. Reviewers aren't sure which pipeline they're looking at. Someone updates one tab and forgets the other. The tracking problem that one role barely creates becomes three overlapping tracking problems, and the fix is no longer a better spreadsheet. The clearest trigger is this: once a small business has more than one role open at a time, a spreadsheet works against it rather than for it.</p>

<h3>More than one person reviews or interviews candidates</h3>
<p>As soon as a second person is involved in evaluating candidates — a co-founder, a department head, a future colleague — the process depends on information flowing reliably between people. Email chains work until they don't. Someone gets dropped from a thread. Feedback sits in a reply the hiring manager never saw. Two reviewers form an opinion of the same candidate without knowing what the other thought. An ATS gives everyone a shared record where feedback is attached to the candidate, not buried in a thread, so the debrief runs in a fraction of the time.</p>

<h3>Applicants arrive from more than one source</h3>
<p>When you post the same role on LinkedIn, Indeed, and your own site, applications arrive through separate channels. Without a central inbox, candidates get missed, and you have no clean way to tell which source sends the best applicants. That second problem is easy to underestimate: losing track of where someone came from makes it impossible to improve sourcing decisions from one hire to the next.</p>

<h3>Candidates are going quiet because responses are slow</h3>
<p>Good candidates don't wait. A strong applicant who goes a week without hearing back has time to move forward with other processes, and many do. Manual processes slow everything down: the confirmation email doesn't go out until someone remembers, the scheduling message waits until the hiring manager has a free moment, and the rejection doesn't happen until after the offer is accepted. Those delays cost you candidates. <a href="https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/candidate-resentment-rise">SHRM research covering 200,000 candidates</a> found that 34% reported not hearing back from employers two months after applying. An ATS with automated messaging eliminates most of that silence, because confirmations, stage-change notifications, and rejections go out without anyone drafting them each time.</p>

<h3>You need a defensible record of hiring decisions</h3>
<p>Equal employment opportunity requirements mean that any employer, regardless of size, may need to show why a candidate was rejected and when. If that record lives only in someone's memory or in a spreadsheet that's been edited several times, it isn't a record. An ATS keeps a timestamped log of every stage move, every message, and every piece of reviewer feedback, which is what a defensible record looks like. This matters most when volume is high, but it applies to any employer who wants to hire fairly and be able to demonstrate it.</p>
<h2>The five-to-ten-hire rule and what it misses</h2>
<p>A commonly cited threshold puts the ATS trigger at roughly five to ten hires per year — the point at which most teams are regularly juggling multiple open roles, multiple reviewers, and candidates coming from more than one place. Some guides set the bar higher, at twenty open positions annually, with the logic that below that volume the subscription cost outweighs the return. Neither number is wrong, but both can mislead if taken too literally.</p>
<p>The right figure depends less on how many people you hire annually and more on how many roles are open at the same time, how many people are involved in each decision, and how much applicant volume each role generates. A startup filling one role at a time, with a single decision-maker and a trickle of applicants, needs no ATS even at eight hires a year. A 50-person company running three concurrent searches with panel interviews and referrals coming in from several directions does.</p>
<p>Company headcount is similarly unreliable as a proxy. A five-person company planning to double in six months has a different hiring problem than a 100-person company with low turnover and one or two hires a year. The trigger is process complexity — how many moving parts the process has, how many people touch each decision, and how many applicants each role draws. If every applicant gets a reply and no candidate has been lost between email threads, you have not hit the threshold yet. When that stops being true, you have.</p>
<h2>What a manual process costs you</h2>
<p>Waiting past the right threshold with a manual process has a predictable cost. The most visible is the missed candidate: a strong applicant who accepts a competing offer while your team is still coordinating feedback, or who stops responding because a scheduling email never arrived. By the time the ATS is in place, that hire is gone. The search restarts, the role sits empty longer, and everyone involved spends more time on hiring than the role demands.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat">SHRM's 2025 benchmarking data</a> puts the average nonexecutive cost per hire at $5,475. That figure covers advertising, recruiter time, interview time, and administrative overhead — and it excludes the cost of the role sitting unfilled for weeks, or the cost of a bad hire made because the evaluation process lacked the structure to surface the right person.</p>
<p>That structure is exactly what an ATS builds ahead of time. Setting the system up before a demanding search begins means the team learns it on a lower-stakes hire. You build your hiring stages, write your message templates, and work out how the pipeline maps to your actual process before any of that is urgent. When the important search comes along, the system is already set up and everyone knows how to use it.</p>
<h2>The benefits that compound over time</h2>
<p>The most immediate gain from an ATS is time. When an application arrives, the system creates the candidate record, sends the confirmation, and places the person in the first stage of the pipeline — without anyone doing it manually. When the team moves a candidate forward, an automated message can go out the same day. The team's feedback is attached to the candidate record rather than scattered across email, so alignment happens faster and decisions get made more cleanly.</p>
<p>Speed follows naturally from that structure. <a href="https://www.hr-brew.com/stories/2024/10/22/new-ta-and-recruiter-tech-is-making-it-easier-to-fill-open-requisitions-survey-reveals">HR Brew reported</a> that companies using recruiting technology cut their median time to fill from 48 days in 2023 to 41 days in 2024 — a seven-day reduction. In industries with tight candidate pools, the difference was sharper: education saw a 27% reduction in time to hire, and retail saw 20%.</p>
<p>Consistency is a less obvious benefit, but it compounds. When every candidate goes through the same stages, gets the same information, and receives a response at each transition, the process runs with the same level of care for every candidate. Those who don't get the role leave with a stronger impression of your company, which produces more referrals and makes strong candidates more willing to apply again. Teams that review candidates against the same criteria, using a shared rubric, make decisions that are easier to explain and harder to second-guess later. If you want a framework for how to structure that evaluation, a <a href="/blog/behavioral-interview-scoring-matrix">behavioral interview scoring matrix</a> gives you the foundation for scoring every candidate the same way.</p>
<h2>What an ATS doesn't fix</h2>
<p>An ATS organizes your hiring process. It manages structure — the stages, workflows, and templates — not the decisions that determine what that structure should be. If you don't have clear hiring stages, the system gives you defaults, but you still have to decide what your process looks like. If your team disagrees about what a good candidate looks like for a given role, structured feedback templates help surface that disagreement, but you have to resolve it. If your job descriptions aren't attracting the right applicants, the ATS will collect the wrong ones more efficiently.</p>
<p>There's also a scope limit worth acknowledging. An ATS is built for active candidates — people who apply to your jobs. It doesn't replace proactive sourcing, referral programs, or the relationships that surface candidates before they're looking. If your biggest recruiting challenge is finding candidates at all, rather than managing the ones who apply, an ATS addresses only part of the problem. Managing inbound applicants and building a proactive talent pipeline are different work, and they call for different approaches. <a href="/blog/talent-acquisition-vs-recruitment">The guide on talent acquisition vs. recruitment</a> covers that distinction in full.</p>
<p>AI-assisted screening scores each application against the job's requirements automatically, which is genuinely useful for high-volume roles because it removes some of the most time-consuming early-stage work. It surfaces signals, not verdicts. The hiring decision still belongs to the people on your team.</p>
<h2>Applicant tracking system for small companies: what to look for</h2>
<p>Not every ATS is built for a growing team. Enterprise systems carry features, complexity, and pricing models designed for large recruiting departments, and those don't translate well to a team of ten or twenty people hiring a handful of roles each year. These are the criteria worth anchoring on when you're evaluating options as a small company.</p>

<h3>Pricing that doesn't punish growth</h3>
<p>Some ATS platforms charge per user seat, which means every hiring manager, interviewer, and reviewer you add to the process raises the monthly bill. The more people you involve in hiring decisions, the more the system costs — right when you're trying to keep overhead low. Look for a system that charges by the number of active job posts rather than by user count, so you can involve your whole team without the math getting complicated every time a new stakeholder joins a search.</p>

<h3>A setup that takes hours, not weeks</h3>
<p>Enterprise recruiting platforms are built for dedicated HR teams with time to implement, configure, and train on a complex system. A growing company without a recruiting department can't absorb that investment. The right system for a small team is one you can set up in an afternoon — job board, hiring stages, application form, and basic templates — and start using on a real search the same week. If a vendor's onboarding timeline is measured in weeks, that's a signal the system was built for someone else.</p>

<h3>The features a small team actually uses</h3>
<p>Vendors tend to oversell features, padding the price and adding to the learning curve. What you need is a careers page that reflects your brand, a visual pipeline that shows where each candidate stands, shared notes and scored reviews, email templates, and interview scheduling that doesn't require a back-and-forth email chain. AI-assisted screening is worth having for roles that attract high volumes of applicants, so check whether it's included in the base plan or priced as an add-on.</p>

<h3>A candidate experience worth testing</h3>
<p>Before committing to any system, apply to your own job on a phone. That is the experience your candidates have. If the form is hard to complete on mobile, long, or confusing, you will lose applicants before they finish. The right tool puts your candidate experience within your control. A straightforward application process signals that your company is organized and respects applicants' time.</p>
<h2>When you genuinely don't need one yet</h2>
<p>If you're making fewer than five hires a year, filling one role at a time, with one decision-maker and a manageable volume of applicants from a single source, a shared inbox and a well-structured spreadsheet can work. The key is that every applicant gets a reply, the spreadsheet is kept current by one person, and no candidate falls through a gap between email threads.</p>
<p>The moment any of those conditions change, the process outgrows both tools. Set your threshold before you reach it: define the conditions under which you move to an ATS. If you open more than one role at a time, or bring in more than one interviewer, you move to an ATS. That way, the decision is made before the crunch, not during an active search when a good candidate is waiting.</p>
<p>Before committing to a standalone ATS, consider what else you need. If your growing company needs employee records, onboarding tracking, and time-off management alongside hiring support, a combined platform handles all of it without requiring a second system. Running two separate systems where one would do creates its own overhead. For teams where hiring is the primary pain point, a standalone ATS is the cleaner choice. For teams building out HR infrastructure at the same time, that question is worth settling before you commit to any vendor.</p>
<h2>What to know before you decide</h2>
<p>Moving from a manual process to an ATS is a small but real investment of time upfront. You'll set up your hiring stages, write your templates, configure your application forms, and decide how roles are structured in the system. For most growing teams, that setup fits into a single workday. For a team that has never had a formal process, it may surface questions you haven't answered yet — which stages a candidate moves through, who is responsible at each one, what a rejection looks like — and working through those is worth doing even if it takes longer than expected.</p>
<p>The question of fit matters as much as timeline. An ATS built for large recruiting departments carries complexity your team doesn't need and may never use. Features you don't use drive up cost and clutter the interface. A system built for a different scale of operation will always have more of them. The goal is to match the system to the scale of the problem. A tool built for growing companies will serve you better than one stripped down from an enterprise product, even if the enterprise product's feature list is longer.
